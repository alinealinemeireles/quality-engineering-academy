## Chapter 137: Causality and Experimentation for Quality Engineering ✦

### The engineering question

> *"The correlation between temperature and defects is strong. If I lower the temperature, do
> defects go down? And if I can't run an experiment — the line can't stop — what can I claim?"*

Every process improvement is a **causal** claim: "if I change X, Y improves." But almost all the
data available at a plant is **observational** — generated by normal operation, not by a design.
This chapter provides the tools to know when a causal claim is defensible, when it isn't, and what
to do in between.

It's also the chapter that explains why **"controlling for everything I measured" isn't
conservative** — it can be exactly what ruins the analysis.

---

## 137.1 The hierarchy of causal evidence

| Level | Design | What it lets you claim | Cost/feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Randomized experiment (DOE, A/B) | **Causal effect**, including unknown confounders | High; not always possible |
| 2 | Quasi-experiment (DiD, RD, interrupted time series) | Causal effect **under verifiable assumptions** | Medium |
| 3 | Observational with adjustment (DAG + regression/matching) | Causal effect **under the assumption of no omitted confounder** | Low |
| 4 | Correlation with no design | **Association only** | Very low |

Randomization's unique advantage is that it acts on variables **nobody knows exist**. No statistical
adjustment can do that: you can only adjust for what was measured.

---

## 137.2 DAGs: the three structural roles

A DAG (directed acyclic graph) represents the assumed causal relationships. Its practical use boils
down to one thing, and it's a big one: **telling you which variables to adjust for, and which to
never adjust for.**

### Confounder — common cause: X ← C → Y

Temperature ← Shift → Defects. Shift affects both. **Adjusting for C is mandatory**; failing to
adjust biases the estimate of X's effect.

### Mediator — on the causal path: X → M → Y

Temperature → Viscosity → Defects. Adjusting for M **removes part of the effect you want to
measure**. It gives X's *direct* effect, not its *total* effect. If the question is "does lowering
the temperature reduce defects?", the relevant effect is the **total** one — and you shouldn't
adjust for viscosity.

### Collider — common effect: X → K ← Y

The counterintuitive, dangerous case. Adjusting for a collider **creates** an association between
variables that were independent.

Concrete industrial example: a part gets sent to reinspection (K) if it's dimensionally marginal
(X) **or** if it looks questionable (Y). The two causes are independent in production. But
**within the set of reinspected parts**, knowing the part is dimensionally fine raises the
probability that appearance was what triggered the reinspection — a negative correlation between X
and Y emerges that doesn't exist in the process. Analyzing only the reinspected parts is
conditioning on a collider.

> **Practical consequence:** analyzing only the cases that reached the lab, only the ones that
> generated a complaint, or only the ones flagged by a system, is almost always conditioning on a
> collider. It's the most common form of selection bias in quality data.

**The rule:** adjust for confounders. Don't adjust for mediators (if the total effect is what you
care about). **Never** adjust for colliders or for descendants of the outcome.

---

## 137.3 Difference-in-differences (DiD)

When you can't randomize but you have a **comparison group** and measurements **before and after**:

$$ \widehat{\text{effect}} = (\bar{Y}^{\text{treated}}_{\text{after}} - \bar{Y}^{\text{treated}}_{\text{before}}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{control}}_{\text{after}} - \bar{Y}^{\text{control}}_{\text{before}}) $$

The second difference removes anything that affected **both** groups over the same period:
seasonality, a shared vendor change, a mix change, a general learning effect.

**The critical assumption is parallel trends:** absent the treatment, the two groups would have
evolved in parallel. It's not directly testable, but it's **falsifiable** — if there are several
periods before the intervention, you can check whether the trajectories were parallel over that
period. If they weren't, DiD isn't defensible and you need another design (synthetic control,
interrupted time series).

**Where it's used in quality:** evaluating a change rolled out on one line but not another, a
training program on one shift, a new supplier at one of two plants.

---

## 137.4 What to do when you can't randomize

In order of practical preference:

1. **Randomize anyway, on a small scale.** This is often more feasible than it looks: randomize the
   order of lots, alternate the configuration by day, apply the change to half the mold cavities.
2. **Stepped-wedge randomization.** Every line gets the change, but in random order over time.
   Politically acceptable ("no one is left out") and statistically valid.
3. **DiD** with a comparison group and a parallel-trends check.
4. **DAG-guided adjustment for confounders**, explicitly stating the no-omitted-confounder
   assumption.
5. **Sensitivity analysis:** how strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be to wipe out the
   estimated effect? If the answer is "implausibly strong," the conclusion is robust.

---

## 137.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Control for everything I measured" | Can open up collider bias | Draw the DAG and adjust only for confounders |
| Adjusting for the mediator when the total effect is wanted | Underestimated effect | Don't adjust; or report direct and total separately |
| Analyzing only flagged/complained-about cases | Selection bias (collider) | Define the population before filtering |
| DiD without checking parallel trends | The effect is confounded with a differential trend | Check pre-intervention periods |
| Confusing a statistical effect with a causal effect | Acting on the wrong variable | Spell out the design and its assumptions |
| Not randomizing because you assume it's impossible | You lose the only design that handles the unknown | Look for partial or stepped-wedge randomization |

--- python
# ============================================================================
# Capitulo 137 - Exemplo resolvido
# (1) Confundimento: o que acontece quando NAO se ajusta
# (2) Vies de collider: o que acontece quando se ajusta a mais
# (3) Diferenca-em-diferencas com verificacao de tendencias paralelas
# (4) Analise de sensibilidade a um confundidor nao medido
# ============================================================================
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(137)
N = 4000

def ols(y, X):
    """Minimos quadrados com intercepto; devolve (coefs, erros-padrao)."""
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])
    beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
    resid = y - X @ beta
    gl = len(y) - X.shape[1]
    s2 = resid @ resid / gl
    cov = s2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    return beta, np.sqrt(np.diag(cov))

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (1)
print("=" * 74)
print("(1) CONFUNDIMENTO  -  Turno afeta Temperatura E Defeitos")
print("=" * 74)
# DAG:  Turno -> Temperatura -> Defeitos  e  Turno -> Defeitos
#       (Turno e confundidor da relacao Temperatura->Defeitos)
EFEITO_REAL_TEMP = 0.30      # a verdade que queremos recuperar

turno = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, N)                     # 0 = dia, 1 = noite
temp  = 180 + 8 * turno + rng.normal(0, 3, N)       # noite trabalha mais quente
defeitos = (EFEITO_REAL_TEMP * (temp - 180)
            + 4.0 * turno                            # efeito direto do turno
            + rng.normal(0, 2, N))

b_sem, se_sem = ols(defeitos, temp)
b_com, se_com = ols(defeitos, np.column_stack([temp, turno]))

print(f"  Efeito causal REAL da temperatura: {EFEITO_REAL_TEMP:+.3f} defeitos por grau\n")
print(f"  SEM ajustar por turno : {b_sem[1]:+.3f}  (EP {se_sem[1]:.3f})"
      f"   vies = {b_sem[1]-EFEITO_REAL_TEMP:+.3f}")
print(f"  COM ajuste por turno  : {b_com[1]:+.3f}  (EP {se_com[1]:.3f})"
      f"   vies = {b_com[1]-EFEITO_REAL_TEMP:+.3f}")
print("\n  Sem ajustar, o efeito aparece MUITO maior do que e: parte do que se")
print("  atribui a temperatura e, na verdade, efeito do turno. Baixar a temperatura")
print("  produziria uma fracao da melhoria prometida no relatorio.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (2)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(2) VIES DE COLLIDER  -  ajustar a mais cria associacao que nao existe")
print("=" * 74)
# DAG:  Dimensional -> Reinspecao <- Aspeto     (Reinspecao e COLLIDER)
#       Dimensional e Aspeto sao INDEPENDENTES no processo
dim    = rng.normal(0, 1, N)     # desvio dimensional (padronizado)
aspeto = rng.normal(0, 1, N)     # indice de aspeto (padronizado), independente
# a peca vai a reinspecao se QUALQUER um dos dois for marginal
reinspecao = ((dim > 1.0) | (aspeto > 1.0)).astype(int)

r_total = np.corrcoef(dim, aspeto)[0, 1]
sel = reinspecao == 1
r_cond = np.corrcoef(dim[sel], aspeto[sel])[0, 1]
b_aj, se_aj = ols(aspeto, np.column_stack([dim, reinspecao]))

print("  DAG: Dimensional -> Reinspecao <- Aspeto   (Reinspecao e COLLIDER)")
print("  No processo, Dimensional e Aspeto sao INDEPENDENTES por construcao.\n")
print(f"  Correlacao na populacao total          : {r_total:+.4f}   <- ~0, correto")
print(f"  Correlacao SO nas pecas reinspecionadas: {r_cond:+.4f}   <- associacao FABRICADA")
print(f"  Coef. de Dimensional ajustando por Reinspecao: {b_aj[1]:+.4f} (EP {se_aj[1]:.4f})")
print(f"  ({100*sel.mean():.1f}% das pecas vao a reinspecao)")
print("\n  Analisar apenas as pecas reinspecionadas (ou reclamadas, ou enviadas")
print("  ao laboratorio) e condicionar num collider. Aparece uma relacao real")
print("  nos dados e inexistente no processo - e nenhum teste estatistico avisa.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (3)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(3) DIFERENCA-EM-DIFERENCAS  -  nova regulacao aplicada so a Linha A")
print("=" * 74)
EFEITO_REAL_DID = -1.80          # a intervencao reduz 1,8 defeitos/turno
n_periodos_pre, n_periodos_pos = 6, 6
periodos = np.arange(n_periodos_pre + n_periodos_pos)
n_turnos = 40                    # turnos observados por linha e por periodo

def gerar_linha(nivel_base, tratada):
    obs = []
    for t in periodos:
        tendencia_comum = -0.25 * t                    # ambas as linhas melhoram
        efeito = EFEITO_REAL_DID if (tratada and t >= n_periodos_pre) else 0.0
        media = nivel_base + tendencia_comum + efeito
        obs.append(rng.normal(media, 2.0, n_turnos))
    return np.array(obs)

A = gerar_linha(nivel_base=22.0, tratada=True)     # linha tratada
B = gerar_linha(nivel_base=17.0, tratada=False)    # linha de controlo

print("  Verificacao das TENDENCIAS PARALELAS no periodo pre-intervencao:")
print(f"  {'Periodo':>8}{'Linha A':>10}{'Linha B':>10}{'A - B':>10}")
print("  " + "-" * 38)
for t in range(n_periodos_pre):
    ma, mb = A[t].mean(), B[t].mean()
    print(f"  {t:>8}{ma:>10.2f}{mb:>10.2f}{ma-mb:>10.2f}")
dif_pre = np.array([A[t].mean() - B[t].mean() for t in range(n_periodos_pre)])
incl, _, _, p_incl, _ = stats.linregress(np.arange(n_periodos_pre), dif_pre)
print("  " + "-" * 38)
print(f"  Inclinacao da diferenca (A-B) no pre: {incl:+.4f} por periodo (p = {p_incl:.3f})")
print(f"  -> {'tendencias PARALELAS: DiD e defensavel' if p_incl > 0.05 else 'ATENCAO: tendencias divergentes, DiD NAO e defensavel'}")

A_pre, A_pos = A[:n_periodos_pre].mean(), A[n_periodos_pre:].mean()
B_pre, B_pos = B[:n_periodos_pre].mean(), B[n_periodos_pre:].mean()
dif_ingenua = A_pos - A_pre
did = (A_pos - A_pre) - (B_pos - B_pre)

print(f"\n  {'':22}{'antes':>9}{'depois':>9}{'variacao':>11}")
print("  " + "-" * 51)
print(f"  {'Linha A (tratada)':22}{A_pre:>9.2f}{A_pos:>9.2f}{A_pos-A_pre:>11.2f}")
print(f"  {'Linha B (controlo)':22}{B_pre:>9.2f}{B_pos:>9.2f}{B_pos-B_pre:>11.2f}")
print("  " + "-" * 51)
print(f"\n  Efeito real da intervencao        : {EFEITO_REAL_DID:+.3f}")
print(f"  Estimativa INGENUA (so antes/depois de A): {dif_ingenua:+.3f}"
      f"   erro = {dif_ingenua-EFEITO_REAL_DID:+.3f}")
print(f"  Estimativa DiD                    : {did:+.3f}"
      f"   erro = {did-EFEITO_REAL_DID:+.3f}")
print("\n  A comparacao antes/depois SOBRESTIMA o efeito, porque atribui a")
print("  intervencao tambem a melhoria geral que aconteceu nas duas linhas.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (4)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(4) SENSIBILIDADE A CONFUNDIDOR NAO MEDIDO")
print("=" * 74)
efeito_estimado = 0.30
print(f"  Efeito estimado apos ajustar pelos confundidores conhecidos: {efeito_estimado:+.3f}")
print("  Pergunta: quao forte teria de ser um confundidor NAO MEDIDO U para")
print("  reduzir este efeito a zero?\n")
print(f"  {'corr(U, X)':>12}{'corr(U, Y)':>12}{'vies induzido':>16}{'efeito ajustado':>18}")
print("  " + "-" * 58)
sigma_y_sobre_x = 2.0
for r in (0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50):
    vies = r * r * sigma_y_sobre_x
    print(f"  {r:>12.2f}{r:>12.2f}{vies:>16.4f}{efeito_estimado - vies:>18.4f}")
print("  " + "-" * 58)
print("\n  Leitura: seria preciso um confundidor nao medido com correlacao ~0,39")
print("  simultaneamente com X e com Y para anular o efeito. Se nenhuma variavel")
print("  conhecida do processo tem correlacao dessa ordem com ambos, a conclusao")
print("  e robusta. Se tem, a conclusao nao se sustenta - e deve dize-lo.")
print("\n  Esta e a analise que transforma 'assumimos que nao ha confundidores")
print("  omitidos' (indefensavel) em 'a conclusao resiste a confundidores ate")
print("  esta magnitude' (defensavel).")

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Confounder, mediator, or collider?**
*Classify each variable in the relationship "injection pressure → surface defects":
(a) mold temperature, which affects both the pressure the operator chooses and the defects;
(b) material viscosity at the moment of injection, changed by the pressure and a cause of the defects;
(c) flagging the part for reinspection, caused both by the recorded pressure and by appearance.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**(a) Mold temperature — CONFOUNDER.** A common cause of X and Y (Temp → Pressure, Temp → Defects).
→ **Adjusting is mandatory.** Without adjustment, part of temperature's effect gets attributed to
pressure.

**(b) Viscosity — MEDIATOR.** It sits on the causal path (Pressure → Viscosity → Defects).
→ **Don't adjust**, if the question is pressure's **total** effect. Adjusting gives the **direct**
effect (what flows through other mechanisms), which answers a different question. Both can matter,
but they should be reported as distinct, named quantities.

**(c) Flagging for reinspection — COLLIDER.** A common effect (Pressure → Flag ← Appearance).
→ **Never adjust**, and never restrict the analysis to flagged parts. Doing so creates a spurious
association between pressure and appearance.

**The rule that sums up all three cases:** draw the DAG **before** writing the regression formula.
The list of variables to include follows from the assumed causal structure, not from what's
available in the table or from what improves R².

</details>

**E2 — Stepped-wedge randomization.**
*Leadership refuses to randomize: "we can't leave half the lines without the improvement." Propose
an acceptable design.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Stepped wedge.** Every line gets the intervention; what's randomized is the **order** of rollout
over time.

With 8 lines and 8 months: draw the sequence at random; in month 1 one line changes, in month 2
another, and so on. In the end, every line has changed — the ethical and political objection
disappears.

**Why it works statistically:**

- In every month, there are already-treated lines and not-yet-treated lines → a concurrent
  comparison exists.
- Each line serves as its **own control** before it changes → removes fixed differences between
  lines.
- Random order ensures the sequence isn't correlated with line characteristics.
- The analysis (a mixed model with a line effect, a period effect, and a treatment indicator)
  separates the intervention's effect from the shared time trend.

**Additional practical advantages:** phased rollout is often the only feasible option
resource-wise (there's no team to change 8 lines at once), and it lets you learn and adjust between
steps.

**Limitation to state:** if there's a strong learning effect, the later lines benefit from
accumulated experience — the estimated effect is an average over different maturity conditions.

</details>

**E3 — DiD and parallel trends.**
*You rolled out a change on line A and used line B as a control. In the pre-intervention period,
the A−B difference was shrinking by 0.4 units per month. Is DiD valid?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**No.** The parallel-trends assumption is visibly violated: A was already converging toward B
before any intervention.

The DiD estimator will attribute that preexisting convergence to the intervention. With 6 months of
post-intervention data, the bias is roughly 6 × 0.4 = **2.4 units** — likely on the same order of
magnitude as the effect you're trying to measure.

**Alternatives, in order of preference:**

1. **DiD with group-specific trends:** include a linear trend per line in the model. It extrapolates
   the pre trend into the post period and estimates the deviation from it. Requires a long enough
   pre period (≥ 8-10 points) to estimate the trend precisely.
2. **Synthetic control:** build a control as a weighted combination of several untreated lines, with
   weights chosen to replicate A's pre trajectory. More robust, requires several candidate lines.
3. **Interrupted time series** on line A alone: model the level and slope before and after, testing
   for a change in both at the intervention point. Doesn't need a control group, but it's vulnerable
   to concurrent events at the same moment.
4. **Look for a better control:** another line whose pre trajectory is actually parallel.

**What not to do:** apply the simple DiD and mention the diverging trend in a footnote. If the
violation is this large, the main estimate is wrong, and the footnote doesn't fix it.

**General lesson:** the value of checking parallel trends lies precisely in being able to
**reject** the design before investing in the analysis. A hypothesis you never test isn't a
hypothesis, it's an assumption.

</details>

**E4 — Sensitivity to an omitted confounder.**
*You estimated that a new tool reduces scrap by 0.8 percentage points, adjusting for known
confounders. How do you respond to "what if there's something you didn't measure?"*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

This is the right question, and the honest answer isn't "there isn't." It's to **quantify how
strong that something would have to be**.

The reasoning: an omitted confounder U biases the estimate by a magnitude that grows with the
product of its associations with the treatment (X) and with the outcome (Y). You flip the
calculation and ask: what's the minimum pair (corr(U,X), corr(U,Y)) capable of wiping out 0.8
percentage points?

Suppose the calculation (like the one in the cell above) returns: it would take a U with correlation
~0.45 simultaneously with tool adoption **and** with scrap.

**The answer, then, is factual and checkable:**

> *"To wipe out the estimated effect, there would need to be an unmeasured variable with a
> correlation of ~0.45 with both tool adoption and scrap. Among the variables we measured, the
> strongest — shift — has a correlation of 0.22 with adoption and 0.18 with scrap. An omitted
> confounder would have to be twice as strong as the strongest known confounder. We consider that
> unlikely, but it's an explicit, falsifiable assumption."*

**Why this is better than the alternative:** it turns an unverifiable claim ("we assume there are no
omitted confounders") into a verifiable, arguable one ("the conclusion holds up against confounding
up to this magnitude"). The skeptical listener can now contribute something concrete — naming a
candidate for U — instead of just doubting.

**Note:** there are formal methods for this — E-value, Rosenbaum bounds, Cinelli-Hazlett sensitivity
analysis. They all answer the same question with different formalizations. What matters is asking
the question and reporting the answer.

</details>
