## Chapter 34: Define

### Creating a Project Charter

A project charter, or team charter, is a short document that includes information about the team and what it plans to accomplish. The charter's purpose is to establish expectations that both the team and the sponsor or executive leaders can agree on, keep the team focused on the goal, ensure the project stays aligned with business objectives, and document the fact that control of a process is being transferred from a business executive or manager to a Six Sigma project team.

At a minimum, team charters should include:

- A complete, concise problem statement that follows the guidelines set out in Chapter 9.
- A list of critical-to-quality metrics, or those measurements that will ultimately determine the project's or process's success. Critical to quality was introduced in Chapter 6.
- The names and roles of everyone on the team. Selecting appropriate team members and roles is covered in Chapter 16.
- A list of process customers, both internal and external. Use a SIPOC, discussed in Chapter 5, to start defining internal or external customers.
- The name of a sponsor and/or champion.
- A duration for the project.

Teams can also include information such as a list of stakeholders who aren't customers, an estimated timeline for each project phase, scope definitions for the process or project, and financial drivers for the project.

The information for the team charter generally can't be gathered in a single brainstorming session; the charter is an output of the whole define phase, not a quick note jotted down at the start. By taking the time to properly consider every element of a team charter, Six Sigma teams build a more solid foundation for the rest of their work.

#### Benefits of an Organizational Team Charter Template

Companies rolling Six Sigma out across the whole organization may want to consider creating or using a specific template for team charters. Templates simplify the define phases and make it easier for leadership teams and other employees to quickly understand the process's critical components. Although the team's final documentation will likely be extensive, and even in the define phase the teams themselves may work with lengthy requirements documents, the charters themselves should be as concise as possible. Some organizations condense charters onto a single page, while others use multi-page documents. A one-page charter template is attached at the end of this chapter.

#### Charter Element Details

We've already covered some of the charter's most important elements in detail in earlier chapters, but here's a quick recap of a few items we didn't cover in as much detail and that are worth mentioning again.

**Business Case**

The business case can also be called the financial drivers behind a project. Closely related to the problem statement, the business case is a short statement that provides a reason why the project should be done. The problem statement tells someone where, when, and how; the business case says why it matters. If you recall Chapter 9, we said dollar values or another financial metric were important to include in the problem statement. If you include a business case in your charter, you'd build on that basic financial statement to explain why, specifically, the loss of money, efficiency, or quality matters to consumers, employees, or the organization. You can also make the case for why the problem needs to be solved now; in essence, why is this project being run now instead of some other project?

**Project Scope**

We briefly introduced the concept of scope in Chapter 9. For the purposes of the team charter, the scope should include a well-defined start and end for the process or problem under consideration. You can also include a short list of items or activities that are in and out of your project's scope. A SIPOC diagram helps teams identify a project's parameters, and you can also use the In and Out of the Box method, described later in this chapter, to understand a project's intended scope.

The scope should be clear. Listing a project's or process's scope as "starting at the order step and ending with fulfillment" isn't clear, because different people might consider different points to be the start of the order step or the end of fulfillment. A better scope statement would be "starting when a customer places an order and ending when the order is packed for shipping." Going even further, a team might consider return and replacement processes out of scope for a project, so they're only dealing with original orders. Successful projects have a well-defined scope, approved and backed by a project sponsor or champion.

**List the Stakeholders**

Listing the key stakeholders on the charter helps the team remember who and what they're likely to impact, beyond the end customers. Having the list visible during meetings reduces the chance the team will start changes that could have a negative or unintended effect on other process owners or processes, and helps point the team toward resources outside the team who can provide help, access, or information related to the project.

**Team Member Roles**

Team members and roles were covered in Chapter 16, and the team charter simply needs to list the names of every team member along with their role and expected time commitment. Adding time commitments to the charter helps sponsors and executive leadership understand the human-resource requirements for the project; a Six Sigma team leader often needs to seek approval for employees from other areas to dedicate a specific amount of time to the project.

Time commitments can be listed in hours per week, but are often listed as a percentage of the employee's total time. For example, a subject-matter expert who should attend every team meeting to provide input, but shouldn't do data collection, analysis, or improvement work, might be listed as providing 10% of their time to the team. A list of team members on a charter might look like the list below. You don't need to list every employee you could possibly consult during the course of the project.

- Mike Smith, Black Belt, 100%
- Chase Michaels, Green Belt, 100%
- Lisa Javes, Green Belt, 100%
- Rosalie Myers, Process Owner, 25%
- Brent Reed, subject-matter expert, 10%
- Brenda Tran, subject-matter expert, 10%

**Milestones**

In Chapter 16, we covered creating a preliminary timeline for a Six Sigma project. The diagram included in Chapter 16 that laid out a project's timeline is called a Gantt chart. Adapted by Henry Gantt in the early 20th century, a Gantt chart is a bar chart that displays a project's phases against time. One benefit of using a Gantt chart to display an approximate project timeline is that it can easily be included in a one-page project charter; anyone reviewing the charter can quickly visualize the time element required for the project.

Teams should make sure a date is provided for the end of each of the DMAIC phases and that every team member agrees the dates are plausible, given what the group wants to do. In some cases, milestones may be set by the project sponsor or champion, but the team should agree the milestone dates are achievable. If the dates seem implausible, teams can present an alternative timeline with logical arguments for why the original timeline wouldn't work.

Beyond the milestones at the end of each project phase, Six Sigma teams may also want to define milestones for the work within each phase — specifically for the more labor-intensive measure, analyze, and improve phases. Although the team should document every milestone it agrees to, detailed milestones don't necessarily belong in the one-page charter document.

**Measure of Success**

Everyone needs to know how the team will measure success. If a sponsor is measuring success by customer satisfaction scores and the team is measuring success by internal quality scores, ideas about the project's outcome are likely to diverge. Generally, measures of success can be drawn from the critical-to-quality metrics discussed in Chapter 6. If teams can convert a CTQ into a measurement, they can understand which key metrics determine a project's success. Although teams may start collecting measurements or analyzing existing measurements as early as the define phase, finalizing the metrics can extend into the measure phase.

**Expected Financial Benefits**

Financial information is likely already included in the charter, both in the business case and in the problem statement. Teams can include the expected financial benefits in a charter's business-case section, but it needs to be included somewhere. For some sponsors and executive leaders, the financial benefit is the most important piece of information included in a charter. An estimated savings or revenue increase also provides a yardstick leaders can use to weigh resource requests for a project.

A Six Sigma specialist should never overstate estimates about financial benefits; it's almost always better to promise less and deliver more. If you tell leaders a project will save $500,000 in the first year, because a big number means you're more likely to get project approval and every resource you ask for, you're the one who'll have to explain yourself when the project only saves $80,000. As with any aspect of a Six Sigma project, be as accurate as possible, but be conservative with estimates when accuracy is in doubt.

**Review the Charter with Success in Mind**

Before a Six Sigma team presents a charter for approval, it should take time to review the document as a group, to make sure the charter sets a foundation for success. Some questions a team can ask itself about a charter include:

- Is everything — especially the goals, financial expectations, and timeline — challenging but realistic?
- Can everyone on the team actually commit the amount of time promised to the project?
- Is the project backed by a sponsor or champion with enough influence to get critical assistance and resources?
- Does the team expect to be supported by auxiliary departments, like information technology, human resources, compliance, accounting, or legal, as needed for the project's success?
- Does the team expect to have the freedom needed to implement a solution it designs, once the solution is approved by the sponsor, champion, or executive committee?
- Does the team have a leader well versed in Six Sigma tools and project management?

If the answer to any of the questions above is no, then the team may be setting itself up for failure. Before moving forward, the team should address these concerns and, if possible, make changes that turn negative answers into positive ones.

#### Project Ground Rules

Before moving forward with any work — even defining a team charter — it's a good idea for a Six Sigma team to establish some basic rules and requirements for the team. We touched on this briefly in Chapter 16, when discussing team management. A project's ground rules should be kept in writing and approved by every team member, but they don't need to be part of an official charter document. The reason for documenting the rules and having every team member sign off on them is so that no single team member can later claim they didn't know the rules.

At the same time, generating rules within a Six Sigma team shouldn't be a fully democratic process. Some of the more obvious or critical rules can simply be provided by the Black Belt or team leader. For example, ground rules should cover topics like who needs to attend each meeting and the fact that team members must keep certain information confidential. A Black Belt can simply state that team members must observe confidentiality and attendance rules, arrive on time for meetings, and respect one another. The team itself will likely vote on how often meetings happen and when they should be scheduled. Seeking input from team members ensures everyone can actually commit to the meeting times. For consistency, it's best to hold meetings on the same days and times every week, but it's understandably hard to keep such a schedule throughout the life of some projects.

Black Belts can also provide some tips and suggestions on how team members should participate during meetings, particularly during group brainstorming sessions. For more on running a brainstorming session, see Chapter 114. Black Belts should also dictate the rules for building an agenda and running a meeting according to that agenda, though they can delegate some of these functions, like timekeeper and secretary.

### Define Phase Tools

We've already covered several define tools in earlier chapters, including the SIPOC diagram and the 5 Whys. Process maps and value stream mapping are two advanced Six Sigma tools often used in the define phase. Some Six Sigma teams start using run charts to begin establishing a baseline in the define phase; run charts are covered in Chapter 35, on the measure phase.

In this section, we'll cover three additional tools common to the define phase: Stakeholder Analysis, the In and Out of the Box method, and the Is/Is Not matrix.

#### Stakeholder Analysis

A stakeholder analysis is a quick way to identify how various people within an organization relate to a project and how the team should keep them informed. Start the analysis with a grid drawn over an x and y axis. The vertical axis represents how much power a person has in the organization. The horizontal axis represents how much interest a person has in the team's project. Stakeholder analysis works best when teams run it on a whiteboard or large flip chart. Draw the basic diagram, as seen in the figure below, in a large format.

Give the team sticky notes. Ask them to write down possible stakeholders for a project or process. Stakeholders are anyone who has an interest in the project, who could benefit from the project's outcome, or who could be impacted by the work carried out through the project. Take a few minutes to discuss the names raised during brainstorming and discard any the team feels aren't really stakeholders.

Once the team decides who the stakeholders are, start placing the names on the chart according to power and interest. People with little power and little interest get placed in the bottom-left corner. People with more power and little interest go in the top-left corner; people with less power but a lot of interest go in the bottom-right corner, and people with high levels of both power and interest go in the top-right corner. A name can be placed higher and further right than the bottom-left corner if the stakeholder has a moderate amount of interest and power, but the team doesn't feel the person really crosses the line. Likewise, a name placed at the lower boundaries of any quadrant can show smaller amounts of power than those placed closer to the top of the box; stakeholder analysis lets teams prioritize stakeholders this way, even within the four quadrants.

Each of the diagram's quadrants loosely correlates with a type of stakeholder, providing some guidance on how the team can interact with each person or department listed.

**Top Left: Keep Satisfied**

Stakeholders who fall into the diagram's top-left quadrant have enough power to interfere with a project, but aren't extremely interested in the day-to-day results. The team should make sure these individuals stay satisfied, however they interact with the project. Teams may also consult them at various points during the project. For example, a team working to solve shipping problems at a warehouse might need to consult purchasing managers at some point, because the team identifies a need for a different type of packing tape. Purchasing leaders have power over sourcing the resource, but may not have great interest in the project overall.

Sometimes, teams may identify a stakeholder who falls into this section and realize it would be valuable for the team if that particular stakeholder were more invested in the project. Six Sigma leaders can work with stakeholders to try to move someone from the low-interest categories into the high-interest ones — this is a political tactic some teams use to strengthen support for a project.

**Bottom Left: Minimal Effort**

Stakeholders who fall into the bottom-left quadrant have the least significant connection to a project. Teams will mostly communicate general information about a project through newsletters or email to these stakeholders. Although these stakeholders require minimal effort from teams, some situations may exist where teams want to move stakeholders out of this box and into the bottom-right box.

**Bottom Right: Keep Informed**

Individuals in the bottom-right box have a strong connection or interest in the project, but don't have access to power to support projects from a resource standpoint. These stakeholders may include employees in departments related to the process being improved, or subject-matter experts who'll be consulted on individual aspects of a project.

Although stakeholders in the bottom-right quadrant generally can't bring resources, they can act in support of a project, often in the form of goodwill ambassadors.

**Top Right: Key Player**

Individuals in the top-right quadrant are key players with respect to a process, or executive leaders with the ability to allocate resources to a project. These are the individuals teams will report to at various tollgates; the executives responsible for a process's or project's ultimate success often show up in the top-right quadrant.

#### In and Out of the Box Method

The In and Out of the Box method is a quick, easy method that helps teams define a project's or process's scope. Start by drawing a large box on a whiteboard or flip chart. Provide pens and sticky notes for the team. Ask the team to write down elements of the process being worked on, including resources, activities, and people. Each item should be written on its own sticky note. Work together to build as complete a list as possible, placing the sticky notes on a wall or table as you go. Make sure everyone on the team understands there are no wrong answers and that the exercise's first phase doesn't call for discussion. By brainstorming items with little or no discussion, teams can capture more ideas, leading to a more accurate picture of what's in and out of scope.

Once a comprehensive list has been made, start working as a team to assign each item a place inside, on the line, or outside the box. Items outside the box are those that will be considered out of the project's scope. Teams may place items outside the box because they don't have access to or control over them, don't have time to work on them, or were specifically instructed not to include the item in the project.

Items placed inside the box are considered within the project's scope. These should be process elements the team can reasonably expect to influence. If the team still isn't sure where an item fits, it should place it on the line. Items that stay on the line may be ones the team expects to be able to impact, but for which the team leader needs to seek permission or assistance from someone outside the team.

Once every item has been placed on the line, inside, or outside the box, review the placements as a team and make any changes. Document the exercise by photographing the diagram or recreating it on a computer. The team can look back at the diagram when trying to control scope creep or consider who to turn to for help with a project.

#### Is/Is Not Matrix

The Is/Is Not matrix is another quick brainstorming tool teams can use to define scope. It can also be used to help define some of the information needed for a problem statement. The matrix works by considering specific things about the process or project and arriving at both "is" and "is not" answers. For example, if a Six Sigma team is tasked with determining why the ovens at a particular plant aren't heating to the proper temperatures, it might create an Is/Is Not matrix like the one below.

### Define Tollgate Checklist

A successful define phase ends with all of the following deliverables:

- A comprehensive project statement
- A team charter
- An understanding of the process and a project diagram or map
- An understanding of the Voice of the Customer
- A definition of what success will look like, agreed on by team members and any sponsors or executive leaders

**Team Charter Template**

A basic one-page team charter template includes the following fields: Project Name; Team Members (Name, Role, Time Commitment); Sponsor; CTQs; Financial Drivers; Internal Customers; External Customers; Non-Customer Stakeholders; In Scope; Out of Scope; Problem Statement; Objective/Goal; and Project Timeline.
