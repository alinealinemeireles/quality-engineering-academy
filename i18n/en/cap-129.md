## Chapter 129: Six Sigma in Construction

### Benefits of Using Six Sigma in the Construction Sector

**Reducing Labor Costs**

Beyond doing good work, the ultimate goal of any construction job is finishing on time. Running past schedule has many ramifications, like additional costs and a damaged reputation for the contractor. An organized workflow is needed to make sure the job gets done on time, if not sooner.

Bringing in Six Sigma can help contractors and construction companies streamline the planning process. Gathering data from previous construction jobs is a vital element in building plans for future projects. When every team member is in a role they enjoy and excel at, the whole operation benefits. Work gets done more safely, more efficiently, and with more care.

Of course, it falls to the project's leadership team to work out the ideal solution that will put these pieces in place. Six Sigma will not only help with data collection, it can also recommend new planning ideas that will let contractors schedule jobs more efficiently. That added efficiency will boost productivity, not to mention the quality of the work.

Beyond that, seeing the proper alignment of a workforce can highlight areas where labor is no longer needed. Maybe there are some redundant processes, or maybe the work simply gets finished faster because it's better organized. Either way, these savings can help the business buy new machinery or offer employees more flexible schedules. The surplus money can also encourage the contractor to offer lower prices for better-quality work.

**Reducing Injuries**

Safety is paramount in the construction sector. And while no one wants to get hurt, it's an unfortunate reality that comes with the territory. All the safeguards in the world can't prevent every injury, but it's the construction company's duty to try to minimize risk wherever possible.

Injuries impact a construction team in many ways. First and foremost, suffering an injury is devastating for the employee, who now has to miss work and may face a lengthy recovery period. That employee also has to fight for workers' compensation benefits, which isn't always an easy task. The injury also has a ripple effect on other team members, who may feel a bit less enthusiastic about engaging in the riskier parts of their job. It also affects the contractor, who not only has to soothe the injured employee's concerns, but must also find an equally effective replacement, one who can work with the existing team. After all, the job has to be finished by the guaranteed completion date, regardless of who got hurt.

Using Six Sigma can help construction companies go beyond simply installing safeguards. Now, the company and the Six Sigma team can work together to rework processes as much as possible, in order to mitigate the amount of risk taken on by construction workers. As is the case with every Six Sigma project, data analysis will be paired with insightful feedback provided by the very workers who carry out dangerous tasks every day.

Because of the sector's nature, not every risky activity can be removed. However, the company can bring in Six Sigma to make the work as safe as possible for its employees.

**Creating Defined Processes**

One of the most frustrating aspects of managing construction is that most people on a given construction crew have different work backgrounds. That means each of them learned to do their job differently. While that might not seem like a big deal individually, having an entire crew of people who work differently from one another can create enormous headaches when it comes to establishing processes.

This disconnect can also result in jobs getting finished improperly, with the mistakes only becoming apparent when it's too late to fix them. Every construction worker has their own way of doing their job, but they don't always communicate those methods to their coworkers. In addition, there are sometimes language barriers, particularly between an English-speaking contractor and a Spanish-speaking crew. When these communication breakdowns are underestimated, the real message doesn't get through, creating the possibility of defects or diminished employee productivity.

These conflicts highlight the need for defined processes, ones that are largely well established. When employees know exactly what's expected of them, and know exactly how to carry out each task, it's much easier for them to finish the task in a way that satisfies management. In addition, establishing processes this way makes it easier for contractors to bring in new labor after someone leaves a project.

Building a new process isn't always easy to do, especially when there are deadlines to meet. Fortunately, Six Sigma allows the needed analysis and reworking to happen. Temporary dips in productivity are offset by periods of sustained efficiency. Solutions that may take time to implement come with supporting data, showing why the change is necessary. Most importantly, a new process, one everyone involved will benefit from, gets created, generating a workflow that removes many of the barriers standing in the way of maximum efficiency.

### Challenges of Using Six Sigma in the Construction Sector

**Machinery Cost**

Six Sigma's ability to pinpoint the core of a flawed process and suggest workable solutions is unmatched. The only problem is when the bottleneck turns out to be a piece of outdated machinery. That puts the entire project, as well as the company that commissioned it, in a tough spot.

For the company, it's a hard pill to swallow. Management was expecting to get an actionable plan, one that would help the company better structure its construction jobs and cut costs. Instead, the leadership team gets a bill for a new machine, one that could cost tens of thousands of dollars. And while the new equipment would help a great deal, it simply isn't an option for a construction company without the cash on hand.

This puts the business in a no-win situation. They either spend money they can't afford to spend on a new piece of machinery, or they go without the new equipment and keep operating at a suboptimal level. On top of that, the company has to deal with disillusioned employees, who thought they were getting a great solution, only to find the status quo will stay in place. This can have a knock-on effect on morale, potentially making productivity even lower than it was before the project started.

Six Sigma projects always aim to deliver the best possible solution at the lowest cost. Unfortunately, sometimes the ultimate solution is one that's cost-prohibitive for the business.

**Difficulty Testing New Processes**

Even the best Six Sigma processes go through a great deal of testing, as well as a period of expected inefficiency while the solution is being rolled out. While this can work in most sectors, it's impossible to pull off in construction.

Construction jobs come with high costs and big expectations. For the price paid, the person hiring the construction crew wants the job done their way, as fast as possible. That person wouldn't be too happy to learn the construction crew was testing out a new process, as a result of its recent Six Sigma initiative. In fact, they'd probably be quite quick to hire an entirely new crew.

In such a high-stakes business, no one wants to be the guinea pig, and understandably so. This puts extra pressure on the Six Sigma project team, as well as on the contractor and its employees. Whatever solution comes out of the project, it has to work, and it has to work the first time. If the solution fails, the project has failed. Worse still, a failed solution can't simply be undone. It may require additional work, which comes with significant costs, not to mention the headache of dealing with an angry customer.

Wherever possible, a contractor should arrange for a reworked process to be tested on a small scale, before making it part of the regular routine. That alone may cost money, but it will give the team some peace of mind, before it's fully rolled out.

**Rising Prices**

Sometimes, Six Sigma can have an unintended adverse effect on a company. A business uses Six Sigma to improve its processes; as a result, additional quality measures get put in place, to make sure the work gets done quickly and effectively the first time. These additional parts of the process, while helpful for ensuring quality and efficiency, force the company to raise the cost of the product or service.

In many cases, the recipient of that product or service is happy to pay the extra money, knowing they'll get the best value for it. However, it's not always that simple in construction. People want good work done, but they also want to pay as little as possible. In the end, money is usually the deciding factor.

A construction company that brings in Six Sigma and fixes its flawed processes should expect to find itself in a great position. And yet, it might find itself unable to attract new business, because of the new price tag attached to its services. Even if the company does better work than ever, and its employees are happy and safe, no one wants to hire that construction crew simply because someone else will do the job for less money.

It's an unfair reality of the business. However, becoming a more efficient, safer construction company is its own reward. Over time, enough work will come in to make up for being passed over by cost-conscious customers. That said, the initial rejection is bitterly disappointing for companies that worked hard to improve their processes.

### Six Sigma Case Study: Concrete Panel Production

A construction company was hired to build 405 villas in Saudi Arabia. Since the project required the company to build so many identical structures in a short period, it was decided that pre-built wall and floor panels would be used to construct the villas. A year into the project, the company realized it was 25 percent behind schedule. A Six Sigma project was carried out to examine why the project had fallen so far behind its expected productivity level.

The project's Define process showed the project's focus should be the number of panels shipped to the job site each day. The initial measurement indicated 18 panels were being delivered, while the project's goal was to raise that number to 75 panels a day.

The company had many potential factors for change; these factors spanned the scope of the entire project. Rather than one massive change dictating the project's future, the company and the Six Sigma team made several smaller changes, aimed at increasing the production and delivery of the concrete panels.

Project management changed, with four foremen hired to oversee six production areas each. In addition, a full-time project quality engineer was brought on, to make sure the panels' quality stayed consistent. Defects in the panels themselves would only add more delays and costs to the project.

To build the panels faster, the company brought in the use of a ready-mix concrete truck, ready to pour at any time. Cranes and a concrete bucket were also purchased, to help speed up panel production. Most importantly, a night shift was added, to help the company get back on schedule.

In addition, the inventory system was reworked. The stockyard was reorganized, to make it easier for employees to find the parts they needed. At the same time, the inventory level was cut to a maximum of 250 units per item. Electronic inventory management was also put in place, so the company could easily reorder materials as needed.

Finally, the company took time to clearly define the process, the stakeholders at each part of the process, and who was responsible for each aspect of production. The company also started measuring cycle times, to bring in benchmarking and measure productivity.

Many changes were needed to make this process workable, both in terms of making it efficient and getting back on the established schedule. Fortunately, the changes proved highly effective. By the project's end, the company had hit its goal of delivering 75 panels a day to the job site. In addition, internal control charts proved the process was, in fact, under control, confirming the solution would be sustainable for the foreseeable future.

### Tips for Using Six Sigma in the Construction Sector

Given the varied nature of the projects it takes on, construction may not seem like the most obvious sector to benefit from Six Sigma. However, its need for constant precision and productivity makes the construction sector a good candidate for the process improvements Six Sigma is known for.

While the company in the case study used its Six Sigma project to make sweeping changes to its process, that's often the wrong approach to take. Seeking out specific improvements that will drive a big change with minimal effort is the best way to bring in Six Sigma, particularly in a high-pressure, results-driven sector like construction.

In some cases, a Six Sigma project will indicate that investing in machinery is needed to improve efficiency and reduce defects. However, there are often other solutions that can improve workflow without requiring that large a capital investment. These alternative fixes may not deliver the improvements new machinery would have brought, but they may come at a much more reasonable cost.

Every construction process is different, and even the best Six Sigma process improvement may not be able to be replicated across other functions. However, there are always tips and positive developments to take away from a process-improvement initiative. Finding ways to fold the improvements made via Six Sigma into other processes can help the company become more well-rounded and productive, which results in a better experience for everyone.
