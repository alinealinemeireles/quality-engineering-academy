## Chapter 61: Measurement System Precision: Repeatability, Reproducibility, and Graphical and Manual Gage R&R Methods

### Repeatability and reproducibility: two distinct sources of variation

Repeatability is the variability observed when the same operator measures the same part, repeatedly, with the same instrument, under identical conditions. Since everything else is held constant — same operator, same part, same instrument, same method — any remaining variation between these repeated measurements is attributed to the instrument or measurement equipment itself. For that reason, repeatability is also called, in the MSA literature, equipment variation (EV). The main error sources feeding repeatability include mechanical wear on the instrument, the instrument's resolution or discrimination capability (that is, the smallest difference it's capable of detecting), small variations in how the same part is positioned or held at each new reading, vibration or electronic noise in the sensor, and the inherent variability of the physical measurement principle used.

Reproducibility, meanwhile, is the additional variability that appears when different operators measure the same part, with the same instrument, under conditions that should be identical in every way except the operator. Since the part and instrument are the same, any additional variation observed between operators is attributed to the differences between them — which is why reproducibility is also called appraiser variation (AV). Typical error sources for reproducibility include differences in training and experience between operators, differences in how the part or instrument is handled (reading angle, applied force, movement speed), differences in visual acuity or in interpreting analog scales, fatigue, and even differences in posture or work environment between shifts.

Added together, repeatability and reproducibility make up what's called gage R&R, or total measurement-system variation. When this combined variation is small relative to the process's natural (part-to-part) variation or relative to the specified tolerance, the measurement system is said to be capable of fulfilling its role; when it's large, the measurement system is "masking" or "inflating" the process's real variation, compromising any control, acceptance, or improvement decision based on that data.

Chapter 35 of this manual already presents a complete numeric example, worked through the analysis of variance (ANOVA) method, of a Gage R&R study, including the same data set worked in a spreadsheet, Power BI, Minitab, Python, and R — for that step-by-step breakdown with numbers, reading that chapter is recommended. Here, the focus is complementary: visual diagnostic graphical methods, the simpler manual calculation methods (the range method and the average and range method), and interpreting the results in terms of %GRR and the number of distinct categories.

### Graphical diagnostic methods

Before — or alongside — any numeric calculation, a Gage R&R study's data should be examined visually. Three charts are particularly useful for this purpose: the average chart by operator, the range chart by operator, and the operator correlation (scatter) chart.

**Average chart by operator.** In this chart, the average of each part's repeated measurements is calculated for each operator, and these average values are plotted on a control chart, with a separate panel for each operator, all sharing the same control limits (calculated from the average repeatability range). This chart's purpose is twofold. First, it reveals the measurement system's ability to discriminate between different parts: since parts normally differ from each other more than the measurement error, it's desirable for most points to fall outside the control limits — which, in this specific context, is a positive sign, not a sign of an out-of-control process as it would be on a traditional process control chart. If, instead, most points fall within the control limits, that indicates the instrument has trouble telling one part from another, because the instrument's own variation is on the same order of magnitude as the variation between parts. Second, by comparing different operators' panels side by side, you can check whether the average patterns are similar between them: if the operators agree well with each other, the rise-and-fall patterns of the per-part averages should look similar from one panel to the next (though with a constant vertical shift if there's some bias between operators); if the patterns are very different, that suggests reproducibility problems or even an interaction between operator and part (that is, operators who err differently depending on which part they're measuring).

**Range chart by operator.** This chart plots, for each part and each operator, the range between that operator's repeated measurements of that part, again with one panel per operator and common control limits based on the overall average range. This chart focuses exclusively on repeatability: each point reflects how consistent a single operator was at repeatedly measuring the same part. Every point is expected to stay within the control limits — that indicates the operators are consistent in using the instrument. Points outside the control limits on the range chart are a genuine warning sign (unlike the average chart): they indicate a special cause of variation in repeatability, like operator fatigue, lack of training, an instrument with mechanical play, or a part that's particularly hard to measure consistently (for example, because it has an irregular surface or is flexible). Comparing the range panels across different operators also lets you identify whether a specific operator is systematically less consistent than the others, which points directly to a need for additional training.

**Operator correlation (scatter) chart.** This third chart is built differently from the previous two: in it, the average (or an individual reading) of each part obtained by one operator is plotted on the horizontal axis, and the average (or reading) of the same part obtained by another operator is plotted on the vertical axis — one point for each part shared between the two operators. When there are more than two operators, a matrix of scatter plots can be built, comparing each pair of operators. If the two operators agree perfectly with each other, every point should fall exactly on the 45-degree line (the line where the value read by one operator is identical to the value read by the other). A constant, parallel shift of all the points relative to that line (a band of aligned points, but shifted up or down from the diagonal) indicates a systematic bias between the two operators — one of them consistently reads higher or lower than the other, but both agree on which parts are bigger or smaller. A wide scatter of points around the 45-degree line, with no constant-shift pattern, indicates a more serious lack of repeatability or reproducibility — the operators not only disagree on level, but also don't consistently agree on the relative ranking of the parts. This chart is particularly useful for communicating, in a simple, visual way, agreement problems between operators to audiences unfamiliar with statistics, and is often the first visual clue that a significant operator-by-part interaction will be found in a more formal analysis.

### Simplified manual methods: the range method and the average and range method

Before statistical software capable of running an ANOVA in a few clicks became widespread, Gage R&R studies were typically run through manual calculations based on ranges, since they're simple to carry out and interpret, even without advanced statistical knowledge. Two of these manual methods remain useful today, especially when a quick field estimate is wanted without needing to set up a full experiment: the range method and the average and range method.

**The range method.** This is the simplest, fastest of the Gage R&R methods, suited to a preliminary or quick check of the measurement system's combined variation, when there's no need (or no time/resources) to separate repeatability from reproducibility. Its typical execution involves just two operators and a small number of parts (often five), with each part measured only once by each of the two operators — that is, with no replicates. For each part, the absolute difference (the range) between operator 1's reading and operator 2's reading is calculated; then the average of these ranges across all the parts is calculated. This average range is then divided by a tabulated statistical constant (which depends on the number of parts used and is analogous to the d2 factors used in control charts), resulting in a combined estimate of the repeatability and reproducibility standard deviation — without, however, letting you isolate how much of that variation comes from the equipment and how much from the operators. Because of this limitation, the range method is suited only for a quick screen ("does the measurement system look grossly adequate or grossly inadequate?"), not for a definitive diagnosis or formal decisions about accepting a measurement system — for that, you should turn to the average and range method or ANOVA.

**The average and range method.** This method, more complete than the simple range method, is the one that lets you break total variation down into its component parts — repeatability, reproducibility, and part-to-part variation — using only averages and ranges, without needing the full apparatus of an analysis of variance. Its typical procedure involves multiple operators (generally two or three), multiple parts (generally between five and ten, chosen to represent the process's normal variation range) and multiple replicates per part and operator (generally two or three repeated measurements). From this data, three key ranges are calculated: the average range of each part's replicates for each operator (which feeds the repeatability estimate), the range between each operator's overall averages (which feeds the reproducibility estimate), and the range between each part's overall averages (which feeds the part-to-part variation estimate). Each of these ranges is divided by an appropriate tabulated statistical constant (depending on the number of replicates, operators, or parts, respectively), converting the range into a standard-deviation estimate. From these three standard-deviation estimates — usually labeled EV (equipment variation, repeatability), AV (appraiser variation, reproducibility), and PV (part variation, part-to-part variation) — the measurement system's combined variation, gage R&R, is calculated as the square root of the sum of the squares of EV and AV, and the study's total variation, TV, as the square root of the sum of the squares of gage R&R and PV.

This method is essentially equivalent, in spirit, to the "tabular method" (based on ranges) presented in greater algebraic detail elsewhere in this manual: both use ranges instead of sample variances to estimate the variation components, and both share the same limitation — being unable to separately estimate a possible interaction effect between operator and part (that is, the possibility that a specific operator errs differently depending on which part they're measuring). When that interaction matters, only the ANOVA method — covered in detail, with a complete numeric example, in Chapter 35 of this manual — is able to isolate and quantify it.

### Interpreting the results: %GRR and number of distinct categories (ndc)

Once the EV, AV, PV estimates are obtained (or, equivalently, the repeatability, reproducibility, and part-to-part variation variance components, in the case of an ANOVA), the final step of any Gage R&R study is translating those numbers into a practical conclusion about the measurement system's adequacy. The two most widely used metrics for this purpose are the Gage R&R percentage (%GRR) and the number of distinct categories (ndc).

%GRR expresses the measurement system's variation as a proportion of the total variation observed in the study — or, alternatively, as a proportion of the tolerance specified for the characteristic, when the goal is assessing the measurement system's adequacy for inspecting and accepting parts against a specification, rather than for statistical process control purposes. In the first case (total variation), it's calculated as:

%GRR = 100 × (Gage R&R / Total variation)

Where total variation combines the measurement system's own variation with the actual variation among the sampled parts. In the second case (tolerance-based variation), gage R&R (multiplied by a factor, typically 6 or 5.15 standard deviations, to cover most of the measurement error's distribution) is compared to the width of the specification range (upper limit minus lower limit) — a calculation equivalent in spirit to the precision-to-tolerance ratio (PTR) mentioned elsewhere in this manual.

Regardless of which base is used in the denominator, practical interpretation follows, by broad industry consensus, the following acceptance criteria:

- %GRR below 10%: the measurement system is considered excellent, fully capable of supporting process-control and product-acceptance decisions.
- %GRR between 10% and 30%: the measurement system is considered conditionally acceptable — the decision to accept it or not depends on the specific application, how critical the measured characteristic is, the cost and difficulty of improving the measurement system, and broader economic considerations. In critical applications (for example, safety characteristics), even values in this range may be considered unacceptable; in less critical applications, they may be tolerated until an improvement becomes viable.
- %GRR above 30%: the measurement system is considered unacceptable. It introduces excessive variation or uncertainty into the collected data, compromising any decision based on it, and needs to be improved (through recalibration, replacing the instrument, retraining operators, revising the measurement method, or standardizing procedures) before being used for control or acceptance.

The number of distinct categories (ndc) is a metric that complements %GRR, expressing the measurement system's ability to distinguish different levels of variation among the parts — in other words, how many "categories" or "classes" of size the system can reliably discriminate within the process's variation range. It's calculated, approximately, as:

ndc = 1.41 × (PV / Gage R&R)

rounding the result down to the nearest integer (truncating the decimal part). An ndc of 1 means the measurement system can't even distinguish two different parts from each other — every part looks the same to the instrument's eye, making it useless even for simple sorting between "large" and "small." An ndc of 2 allows, at most, sorting parts into two categories (for example, below and above average), which already enables some use for binary classification (pass/fail), but not for statistical process control, which requires the ability to detect finer variations in the process distribution. The reference widely adopted across industry is that a measurement system should show an ndc of 5 or greater to be considered adequate for statistical process control, since that minimum value ensures enough resolution that the measurement system doesn't "hide" real, meaningful changes in the process distribution behind its own noise.

It's worth noting that %GRR and ndc measure slightly different, though related, aspects of a measurement system's adequacy, and so should always be assessed together: it's possible, though uncommon, for a system to show an acceptable %GRR and still have an insufficient ndc (or vice versa), especially when the variation among the parts sampled in the study is atypically small or atypically large relative to the process's real variation in production — another reason why carefully selecting the parts used in a Gage R&R study, so they faithfully represent the process's normal variation range, is a critical step in planning the study, not a secondary detail.
