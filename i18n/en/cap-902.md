## Chapter 902: Capability Studio — Cp, Cpk, Pp, Ppk in Practice

### The engineering question

*The customer requires Cpk ≥ 1.33. The report says Cpk 1.41 and Ppk 0.92. Which of the two numbers
will they use to reject the lot — and which one should I use to decide what to do on Monday?*

They'll use **Ppk**, because it's the one that describes what actually ships out the door. You'll
use **the gap between the two**, because that's what tells you where the problem is. A capability
report that shows only one of the four indices isn't a capability report.

### The four indices, without the confusion

Hover over each row of the table to see what it means in practice.

| Index | Formula | Sigma used | Answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cp** | $\frac{USL - LSL}{6\hat{\sigma}_{short}}$ | short-term | Does the process **fit** within the tolerance? |
| **Cpk** | $\min\!\left(\frac{USL-\mu}{3\hat{\sigma}_{short}}, \frac{\mu-LSL}{3\hat{\sigma}_{short}}\right)$ | short-term | And is it **centered** in it? |
| **Pp** | $\frac{USL - LSL}{6 s}$ | long-term | Does it fit, **accounting for everything** that happened? |
| **Ppk** | $\min\!\left(\frac{USL-\mu}{3s}, \frac{\mu-LSL}{3s}\right)$ | long-term | It's what **the customer receives** |

The distinction that resolves 90% of the confusion:

- $\hat{\sigma}_{short}$ comes **from within the subgroups** — via the mean range
  ($\bar{R}/d_2$) or the moving range ($\overline{MR}/1.128$ for individuals). It's the variation
  the process has when nothing changes.
- $s$ is the **standard deviation of the entire sample**
  ($\sqrt{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2/(n-1)}$). It includes shift changes, raw-material lot changes,
  operator changes, tool wear.

Which is where this reading pays off:

That's a factor of 43.

## 2 · Why 1.33 isn't an arbitrary number

```plotly
cap-cpk-ppm
```

The scale is logarithmic because it has to be: every 0.33 of Cpk is worth roughly **one order of
magnitude** in defective parts. This is what's behind the requirements that show up in contracts:

- **Cpk ≥ 1.00** — the process "fits," at 2,700 PPM. Barely accepted anywhere anymore.
- **Cpk ≥ 1.33** — 63 PPM. The reference requirement in industrial supply.
- **Cpk ≥ 1.67** — 0.3 PPM. Safety characteristics, automotive, aerospace.
- **Cpk ≥ 2.00** — "six sigma" in the strict sense, before the 1.5σ shift.

> **Watch out for the 1.5σ shift.** The classic "6σ = 3.4 DPMO" conversion already includes an
> assumed 1.5σ shift in the long-term mean. The PPM figures in the chart above are calculated
> **without** that shift — they're instantaneous performance. Mixing the two conventions in the
> same report is the most common source of sterile arguments in quality meetings.

## 3 · Same Cp, very different Cpk

Four processes, the same tolerance. Hover over each curve:

```plotly
cap-cp-vs-cpk
```

Case **B** is the most common in industry and the easiest to fix: the machine is capable, it's
just poorly centered. A setup adjustment restores the Cpk. Case **C** is the most expensive:
no adjustment helps, because the variation is intrinsic — it requires changing the tool, fixture,
material, or method.

**The decision rule that follows from this:**

1. Calculate Cp. If Cp < 1.33, **don't waste time centering** — the tolerance doesn't fit.
   Attack the spread.
2. If Cp ≥ 1.33 but Cpk < 1.33, it's a **centering** problem. It's the cheap fix. Solve it first.
3. Only then compare Cpk with Ppk to find out whether there's instability to deal with.

## 4 · Where to gain Cpk: center it or reduce the variation?

The map below shows Cpk as a function of two quantities normalized by the half-tolerance: how far
off-target the process is (horizontal axis) and how much it varies (vertical axis).

```plotly
cap-cpk-heat
```

Read it like a topographic map. If you're at a point with **nearly horizontal contour lines**,
moving sideways (centering) barely raises the Cpk — you have to go down (reduce σ). If the
contours are nearly vertical, centering solves it. Across most of the practical region, **going
down costs more but pays more.**

## 5 · Cpk vs Ppk: the gap always has a name

A hundred and twenty parts, four shifts. Within each shift the process is stable; between shifts,
it isn't.

```plotly
cap-curto-longo
```

The short-term sigma only "sees" the variation within a shift, so the Cpk is optimistic. The
long-term sigma sees the steps between shifts, and the Ppk drops. **The gap between the two
indices is the cost of instability** — and in this case it has a name, a time, and an owner.

The classic mistake is reporting the Cpk to the customer because it's the bigger number. The
symmetric, and more serious, mistake is reporting the Ppk and closing the subject — losing the
information that the process *is capable*, it's just not under control.

> **Golden rule.** Capability only means something once the process is in **statistical
> control**. Calculating Cpk on an unstable process is measuring a patient's average temperature
> as they swing between 35 °C and 41 °C: the number exists, and it describes no one.

## 6 · The full calculation, in Python and R

This is the block that should go into the report: the four indices, the two sigmas, the PPM, and
the normality test — because **every index above assumes a normal distribution**.

```py-r
--- python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Shaft diameter (mm). Specification 35.00 +/- 0.50
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
turno = np.repeat(np.arange(4), 30)
x = 35.0 + np.array([0.0, 0.10, -0.06, 0.16])[turno] + rng.normal(0, 0.09, 120)

LSL, USL, TARGET = 34.50, 35.50, 35.00

# --- the two sigmas ----------------------------------------------------------
MR = np.abs(np.diff(x))
sigma_short = MR.mean() / 1.128          # d2 = 1.128 for n = 2
sigma_long = x.std(ddof=1)
mu = x.mean()

def indices(mu, s):
    Cp  = (USL - LSL) / (6 * s)
    Cpk = min(USL - mu, mu - LSL) / (3 * s)
    Cpm = (USL - LSL) / (6 * np.sqrt(s**2 + (mu - TARGET)**2))   # penalizes deviation from target
    ppm = (stats.norm.cdf(LSL, mu, s) + stats.norm.sf(USL, mu, s)) * 1e6
    return Cp, Cpk, Cpm, ppm

Cp, Cpk, Cpm, ppm_c = indices(mu, sigma_short)
Pp, Ppk, _,   ppm_l = indices(mu, sigma_long)

# --- normality: without this, the indices are worthless ---------------------
ad = stats.anderson(x, dist="norm")
normal = ad.statistic < ad.critical_values[2]          # 5% level

print(f"n = {len(x)}   mean = {mu:.4f} mm   target = {TARGET}")
print(f"sigma short = {sigma_short:.4f}   sigma long = {sigma_long:.4f}   "
      f"ratio = {sigma_long/sigma_short:.2f}")
print()
print(f"  Cp  = {Cp:5.2f}      Cpk = {Cpk:5.2f}      Cpm = {Cpm:5.2f}   (short-term)")
print(f"  Pp  = {Pp:5.2f}      Ppk = {Ppk:5.2f}                        (long-term)")
print()
print(f"  Expected PPM (short) = {ppm_c:9.1f}")
print(f"  Expected PPM (long)  = {ppm_l:9.1f}   <- what the customer receives")
print(f"  Observed PPM         = {((x < LSL) | (x > USL)).mean()*1e6:9.1f}")
print()
print(f"Anderson-Darling A2 = {ad.statistic:.3f} "
      f"(5% critical = {ad.critical_values[2]:.3f}) -> "
      f"{'normal' if normal else 'NOT normal: use the Clements or Johnson method'}")
print()
if Cp - Cpk > 0.15:
    print(f"DIAGNOSIS: off-center. Cp - Cpk = {Cp-Cpk:.2f}. "
          f"Adjusting the mean by {TARGET - mu:+.3f} mm restores Cpk = {Cp:.2f}.")
if Cpk - Ppk > 0.15:
    print(f"DIAGNOSIS: unstable. Cpk - Ppk = {Cpk-Ppk:.2f}. "
          f"There are special causes between subgroups - check SPC before touching the machine.")

# --- normality ---------------------------------------------------------------

cat(sprintf("sigma short = %.4f   sigma long = %.4f   ratio = %.2f\n\n",
            sigma_short, sigma_long, sigma_long / sigma_short))

            curto["Cp"], curto["Cpk"], curto["Cpm"]))
cat(sprintf("  Pp  = %5.2f      Ppk = %5.2f                        (long-term)\n\n",

if (curto["Cp"] - curto["Cpk"] > 0.15)

              curto["Cp"] - curto["Cpk"], TARGET - mu))

              curto["Cpk"] - longo["Cpk"]))

--- dax
// Capability measures in Power BI
// 'Measurements' table with columns [Value], [Date], [Shift]; parameters in 'Spec'

Mean = AVERAGE ( Measurements[Value] )

SigmaLong = STDEV.S ( Measurements[Value] )

// Short-term sigma via moving range
SigmaShort =
VAR WithMR =
    ADDCOLUMNS (
        Measurements,
        "@MR",
        VAR d = Measurements[Date]
        VAR prev = CALCULATE ( MAX ( Measurements[Value] ), Measurements[Date] < d )
        RETURN ABS ( Measurements[Value] - prev )
    )
RETURN DIVIDE ( AVERAGEX ( WithMR, [@MR] ), 1.128 )

Cp  = DIVIDE ( [USL] - [LSL], 6 * [SigmaShort] )

Cpk = DIVIDE ( MIN ( [USL] - [Mean], [Mean] - [LSL] ), 3 * [SigmaShort] )

Ppk = DIVIDE ( MIN ( [USL] - [Mean], [Mean] - [LSL] ), 3 * [SigmaLong] )

// Traffic light for the KPI card
StatusCpk =
SWITCH (
    TRUE (),
    [Cpk] >= 1.67, "Excellent",
    [Cpk] >= 1.33, "Conforming",
    [Cpk] >= 1.00, "Marginal",
    "Not capable"
)
```

## 7 · When the distribution isn't normal

It's more common than people admit: runout, roughness, flatness, concentricity, and anything
**bounded on one side by zero** is rarely normal. Applying the formulas above in those cases
produces optimistic Cpk values — sometimes by a factor of two.

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Moderate skew, known cause | Box-Cox or Johnson transform, then normal indices |
| Zero-bounded characteristic (runout, form) | **Clements** method with the 0.135% and 99.865% percentiles |
| Identified distribution (Weibull, lognormal) | Percentiles of the fitted distribution itself |
| Attribute data (pass/fail) | Don't use Cpk. Use **PPM** and a p or np chart |

The generalized definition, which works for any distribution, replaces $6\sigma$ with the interval
between percentiles:

$$P_{pk} = \min\left(\frac{USL - x_{0.5}}{x_{99.865} - x_{0.5}},\ \frac{x_{0.5} - LSL}{x_{0.5} - x_{0.135}}\right)$$

For the normal distribution, this formula reduces exactly to the classic one — it's the same
thing written more generally. Chapter 72 of the manual covers this case in detail.

## 8 · The checklist before signing off on a report

1. Is the process in **statistical control**? (chart before index, always)
2. Is the data **normal**? Was it tested, and is the test in the report?
3. Is the **subgroup rational**? (Chapter 66 — this decision determines the short-term sigma)
4. Are there **at least 100 measurements**, preferably 125+, covering shifts and lots?
5. Was the measurement system validated? A **Gage R&R above 30%** invalidates any Cpk — the
   measured variation belongs to the instrument, not the process.
6. Are the **four indices** reported, not just the most favorable one?
7. Is the **specification correct** and the currently effective drawing revision?
8. Does the report state **which sigma was used** for each index?

Point 5 is the one most often ignored: if the Gage R&R consumes 30% of the tolerance, the observed
$\sigma$ already contains $\sigma_{measurement}$, and the process's real Cpk is **better** than
calculated. Correcting for it is simple arithmetic:

$$\sigma_{process} = \sqrt{\sigma_{observed}^2 - \sigma_{measurement}^2}$$

## 9 · Two quick readings: the garage and the Sixpack

Two ways to explain Cp/Cpk without a formula in sight — useful for a meeting with people who don't
live in statistics day to day.

**The car-in-the-garage analogy.** Cp measures whether the car fits; Cpk measures whether it fits
*in the right spot*.

| Scenario | Cp | Cpk | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wide garage, car well centered | high | high | Excellent — clearance on both sides |
| Wide garage, car pulled up against one side | high | low | The process has capacity to spare, it's just poorly positioned — a centering adjustment fixes it |
| Tight garage, car centered | ≈ 1 | ≈ 1 | It fits, but with no margin — any extra variation hits the wall |
| Narrow garage | low | low | It doesn't fit, no matter the position — the problem is the spread, not the centering |

It's the same reading as the table in section 1, just without a chart — it works in any hallway
conversation.

**What Minitab's Capability Sixpack shows, panel by panel.** Anyone using Minitab doesn't see the
indices in isolation — they see six charts side by side, and each answers a different question:

| Panel | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| X̄ chart (or I, for individuals) | Is the process centered and stable over time? |
| R chart (or MR) | Is the within-subgroup spread under control? |
| Capability histogram + normal curve | Is the shape of the data compatible with the spec limits? |
| Normal probability plot | Is the data reasonably normal? (Anderson-Darling, section 7) |
| Last 20 subgroups | Is there a recent drift that the full chart dilutes? |
| Within/overall capability | Cp/Cpk (short-term) side by side with Pp/Ppk (long-term) |

The design point behind the Sixpack is exactly this chapter's argument: **never show the index
without showing the chart next to it**. A pretty Cpk next to an out-of-control chart is the most
reliable sign that something is wrong with the report, not the process.

**A fifth letter that shows up in an automotive context: Cm and Cmk.** Before the process enters
production, many standards (IATF, VDA) require a **machine capability** study — few parts, very
short term, controlled conditions — separate from the process capability study.

| Index | What it assesses | Typical part count | When it's used |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cm / Cmk** | The machine alone, in the best possible scenario | 50–100 consecutive parts | New-machine approval, before any shift/lot variation enters the picture |
| **Cp / Cpk** | The process already in normal operation, but short-term | 25 subgroups × 5 parts (125 measurements) | Stabilized process, routine decision |
| **Pp / Ppk** | Actual performance over time, all sources of variation included | 100+ measurements, covering shifts and lots | PPAP, customer report |

The progression matters: **Cm/Cmk assesses the machine; Cp/Cpk assesses the process; Pp/Ppk
assesses what the customer actually receives.** Approving the machine doesn't replace validating
the process, and validating the process short-term doesn't replace measuring actual performance
over time — they're three different questions, answered at three different points in the APQP
cycle.

### Common mistakes

1. **Calculating capability on an unstable process.** Mistake #1, and the most expensive.
2. **Using `std()` without `ddof=1`** (or `n` instead of `n-1`). Underestimates sigma and inflates
   every index. In R, `sd()` already uses n−1; in Python, `numpy.std()` uses n by default.
3. **Confusing $\bar{R}/d_2$ with $s$.** They're different things and answer different questions.
4. **Reporting Cpk when the customer asked for Ppk.** Or, worse, switching between the two
   depending on convenience.
5. **Ignoring the target.** A process within tolerance but far from the target has a good Cpk and
   a bad Cpm — and whoever assembles the part downstream notices the difference.
6. **Applying indices to non-normal data** without checking.
7. **Forgetting the measurement system.** See point 5 above.
