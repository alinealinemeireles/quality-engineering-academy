## Chapter 138: Model Validation, Calibration, Drift, and Model Risk
### The engineering question

> *"The model has 96% accuracy on validation. Can I let it decide on part approval? And how will I
> know, six months from now, whether it's still working?"*

When a statistical or machine-learning model starts making or supporting quality decisions, it
becomes **part of the control system** — and it becomes subject to the same requirements as any
measurement instrument: documented validation, monitoring, acceptance criteria, and a reaction
plan. This is the bridge between data science and ISO/IEC 42001 (Chapter 149).

---

## 138.1 Validation: what quality requires and data science usually forgets

**1. Temporal split, not random.** If the data has temporal structure (and process data always
does), a random split lets the model see the future. The correct split is to train up to T and test
after T. A random split systematically produces optimistic metrics.

**2. Split by group.** If there are multiple measurements of the same part, lot, or machine, every
observation from that group must end up on the same side of the split. Otherwise the model
"recognizes" the group instead of learning the pattern — the most common form of leakage in
industrial data.

**3. Nested CV for hyperparameter selection.** Using the same set to choose hyperparameters and to
estimate performance biases the estimate. The inner loop selects; the outer loop estimates. Without
this, the performance estimate is optimistic in an unquantified way.

**4. The metric has to reflect the real cost.** In an inspection with 2% bad parts, a model that
approves everything has 98% accuracy and zero value. Useful metrics: sensitivity at a fixed
specificity, expected cost per part, or the precision-recall curve.

**5. An honest baseline.** Compare against what exists today: the current rule, the human inspector,
or the trivial model. A model that doesn't beat the baseline shouldn't go to production, no matter
how sophisticated it is.

---

## 138.2 Calibration: the property almost nobody checks

A model is **calibrated** when, among the cases it assigns probability p, roughly a fraction p
actually occurs.

**Discrimination and calibration are independent properties.** AUC measures only ranking and is
invariant to any monotonic transformation of the probabilities. A model can have an AUC of 0.95
(ranks nearly perfectly) and predict 0.90 where the true frequency is 0.50.

**When calibration matters more than AUC:** whenever the predicted probability feeds an economic
calculation. "Inspect if P(NC) × cost_NC > cost_inspection" only works if P(NC) is a genuine
probability.

**How to measure it:**

- **Calibration curve (reliability diagram):** group by predicted probability and compare against
  the observed frequency in each group.
- **Brier score:** mean squared error of the probability. Decomposes into calibration + refinement.
- **ECE (Expected Calibration Error):** weighted average of the absolute deviation per group.

**How to fix it:** *Platt scaling* (logistic regression on the output) or **isotonic regression**
(monotonic, nonparametric), both fitted on a calibration set **separate** from the training set.

---

## 138.3 The decision threshold comes from the cost, not from 0.5

The default 0.5 threshold amounts to assuming the two errors cost the same. In a quality
inspection, they almost never do.

With C_FN = the cost of letting a bad part through and C_FP = the cost of rejecting a good part,
the threshold that minimizes the expected cost is:

$$ p^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}} $$

If letting one through costs €400 and wrongly rejecting one costs €12, then p* = 12/412 ≈
**0.029**. You reject any part with more than a 2.9% probability of being bad. Using 0.5 would be
an expensive mistake — and it's what happens by default in practically every library.

---

## 138.4 Drift: the model degrades on its own

A model in production degrades because the world changes, not because the code changes.

| Type | Definition | Industrial example |
|---|---|---|
| **Covariate drift** | P(X) changes; P(Y\|X) stays the same | New supplier with different raw material |
| **Concept drift** | P(Y\|X) changes | A new specification changes what "nonconforming" means |
| **Label drift** | P(Y) changes | A process improvement lowers the base defect rate |
| **Upstream data drift** | A pipeline change | Sensor recalibrated, unit changed, field renamed |

**Detection without labels** (the normal case — labels arrive late or never):

- **PSI (Population Stability Index)** per variable. Rule of thumb: < 0.10 stable; 0.10-0.25
  investigate; > 0.25 material drift.
- **KS test or Wasserstein distance** between the reference distribution and the current one.
- **Monitor the distribution of the predictions**, not just of the inputs.
- **A control chart on the model's metrics** — SPC applied to the model, closing the loop.

---

## 138.5 Model risk: the model as a piece of the quality system

A model that makes decisions about product is a control. At minimum, it requires:

1. **Model inventory** — which models exist, where, who's accountable for each one.
2. **Documented validation** — data, method, metrics, known limitations, acceptance criterion.
3. **Change control** — versioning of code, data, and model; which changes require formal
   revalidation.
4. **Continuous monitoring** — drift, performance, and alarms with a defined reaction plan.
5. **Human oversight** — who can override the model, with what authority and what record.
6. **Traceability** — being able to reconstruct why a part was rejected on day X by version Y.
7. **Fallback plan** — what happens if the model fails or is retired.

This list isn't optional good practice: it's the translation, into quality terms, of the
requirements of ISO/IEC 42001 and the guidance of ISO/IEC 23894.

---

## 138.6 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Random split with temporal data | Optimistic performance, fails in production | Temporal split |
| Observations from the same lot in both train and test | Group leakage | Split by group |
| Optimizing accuracy with imbalanced classes | A model that approves everything | Cost-linked metric |
| Using the default 0.5 threshold | Expected cost not minimized | p* = C_FP/(C_FP+C_FN) |
| Trusting AUC when the probability itself is used | Wrong economic calculation | Check and fix calibration |
| Not monitoring drift | Silent degradation | PSI/KS + control chart |
| Changing the model without revalidating | An untraceable change in the control system | Formal change control |

def logistica(Xtr, ytr, iters=60, ridge=1e-6):
    Xa = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(Xtr)), Xtr])
    beta = np.zeros(Xa.shape[1])
    for _ in range(iters):
        eta = Xa @ beta
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(eta, -35, 35)))
        W = np.clip(p * (1 - p), 1e-9, None)
        H = Xa.T @ (Xa * W[:, None]) + ridge * np.eye(Xa.shape[1])
        g = Xa.T @ (ytr - p)
        passo = np.linalg.solve(H, g)
        beta += passo
        if np.max(np.abs(passo)) < 1e-9:
            break
    return beta

def isotonica(x, yv):
    o = np.argsort(x); xs, ys = x[o], yv[o].astype(float)
    niveis = ys.copy(); pesos = np.ones(len(ys))
    i = 0
    while i < len(niveis) - 1:

            novo = (niveis[i]*pesos[i] + niveis[i+1]*pesos[i+1]) / (pesos[i] + pesos[i+1])

            niveis = np.delete(niveis, i+1); pesos = np.delete(pesos, i+1)

            while i > 0 and niveis[i-1] > niveis[i]:

    limites = np.cumsum(pesos).astype(int)
    x_nos = np.array([xs[min(l, len(xs)-1) - 1] for l in limites])
    return x_nos, niveis

def psi(ref, atual, n_bins=10):
    bordas = np.quantile(ref, np.linspace(0, 1, n_bins + 1))

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Data split.**
*You have 50,000 measurements from 500 lots, gathered over 18 months. How do you split them for
validation?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Two constraints at once**, and both must be respected:

1. **Temporal:** train on the first ~12 months, validate on months 13-15, test on months 16-18.
   This reproduces the real situation: the model predicts the future using information from the past.
2. **By group:** no lot can have measurements in two sets. Measurements from the same lot share raw
   material, setup, and conditions — the model would memorize the lot's signature.

Fortunately the two constraints are compatible: splitting by the lot's production date automatically
satisfies the group split, as long as the cut is made **at the lot level** rather than the
measurement level.

**What a random split by measurement would do:** measurements from lot 237 would end up in both
train and test at once. The model would learn "lots with this sensor signature have this defect
rate" — an excellent test metric, zero value on a new lot.

**For the final result, use walk-forward validation:** train on [0,12], test on [13]; train on
[0,13], test on [14]; and so on. This gives several performance estimates and reveals whether the
model is degrading over time — information a single split hides.

**Also important:** check whether the base defect rate changes over the 18 months. If the process
improved, the test set has fewer positives and its metrics aren't comparable to the training set's.

</details>

**E2 — Calibration vs discrimination.**
*Model A has AUC 0.92 and ECE 0.15. Model B has AUC 0.88 and ECE 0.02. Which do you choose?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**It depends on how the output will be used** — and this is exactly the question you should ask
before choosing the metric.

**If the output is a ranking** (inspect the 100 most suspect parts per shift, with fixed capacity),
what matters is discrimination. **Model A.** The absolute probability is irrelevant when the
decision is "the top 100."

**If the output feeds an economic calculation** (inspect if P(NC)×€400 > €12), the probability has
to be reliable. **Model B.** An ECE of 0.15 means the probabilities are systematically off by
~15 percentage points, which destroys the calculation.

**But the better answer is: don't choose — calibrate A.**

Calibration (Platt or isotonic) is a **monotonic** transformation of the output. So:
- **it doesn't change the AUC** (which only depends on the ranking);
- **it drastically reduces the ECE**.

The result is a model with AUC 0.92 and an ECE close to 0.02 — better than both. It costs a separate
calibration set (it can't be the training set or the final test set) and about ten lines of code.
The cell above demonstrates this numerically.

**The rule:** discrimination is a property of the model, hard to improve. Calibration is a property
of the output scale, almost always easy to fix. Don't trade the first for the second.

</details>

**E3 — Economic threshold.**
*A nonconforming part that reaches the customer costs €2,000 in warranty and reputation. Rejecting a
good part costs €15 (scrap). What's the threshold? How does it change if the good part can be
reworked for €3?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Case 1 — reject = scrap:**

p* = C_FP/(C_FP + C_FN) = 15/(15 + 2000) = **0.00744**

You reject any part with more than a **0.74%** probability of being nonconforming. An extremely
low threshold — because the cost asymmetry is extreme (133:1).

**Case 2 — a good part can be reworked for €3:**

p* = 3/(3 + 2000) = **0.00150**

An even lower threshold (0.15%): since the cost of erring on the safe side dropped from €15 to €3,
it pays to be even more cautious. **Reducing the cost of the false positive lets you increase
protection.**

**The engineering implication is the exercise's main lesson:** investing in making the false
positive cheap — a rework line, a quick second check — lets you lower the threshold and catch more
bad parts, without raising the total cost. That's often an investment with a much better return
than improving the model.

**Mandatory checks before applying this:**

1. **Is the model calibrated?** With thresholds this low (0.15%), small calibration errors in the
   left tail change the rejection rate a lot. Check calibration specifically in the threshold's
   region, not on average.
2. **What's the resulting rejection rate?** If p* = 0.0015 leads to rejecting 40% of production, the
   model doesn't discriminate well enough for this application, and the conclusion is that you need
   more information (more sensors, better measurement), not a different threshold.
3. **Are the costs right?** It's worth redoing this with C_FN of €1,000 and €4,000 — if the optimal
   threshold doesn't change the operational decision much, uncertainty in the costs isn't critical.

</details>

**E4 — Revalidation.**
*Define the criteria that require formal revalidation of an inspection model.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The criterion should be written into the model's control plan **before** the model goes to
production, with numeric thresholds and a named owner.

**Mandatory revalidation triggers**

*Deliberate changes*
- Any change to the training code, the input variables, or the hyperparameters.
- Retraining with new data, even without changing the method (the resulting model is a different model).
- A change to the decision threshold.
- A change in the upstream pipeline: new sensor, recalibration, unit or schema change.

*Process changes*
- New raw-material supplier, new mold, new machine, recipe change.
- A change to the specification or the definition of nonconformance (**concept drift** by
  definition — what the model learned to predict is no longer what matters).
- A new product or variant not represented in the training data.

*Monitoring signals*
- PSI > 0.25 in any input variable or in the distribution of the predictions.
- Performance below the acceptance criterion in two consecutive windows.
- Calibration drift: ECE above the defined threshold.
- A customer complaint attributable to a part the model approved.

*Schedule*
- A minimum periodic revalidation (e.g., annual), regardless of triggers — because the absence of
  an alarm might mean the monitoring itself has stopped working.

**What revalidation has to produce:** a new report with data, period, metrics, a comparison against
the previous version, a decision (keep / retrain / retire), and approval from whoever has the
authority. Without this, there's no traceability and the management system's change-control clause
isn't satisfied.

**Normative link:** this set is the translation, for a quality case, of the change control and
monitoring required by ISO/IEC 42001 and recommended by ISO/IEC 23894 (Chapter 149).
