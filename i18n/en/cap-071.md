## Chapter 71: Capability and Performance: Cp, Cpk, Pp, Ppk, Cpm
### The engineering question

*Can this process meet the specification — and does it actually meet it?*

These are two different questions. The first is about **potential**; the second is about **actual performance**. Confusing them is the most expensive mistake in this subject, because it leads to certifying as capable processes that produce scrap.

### The rule that structures this entire subject

> **Capability shouldn't be calculated before statistical stability has been demonstrated.**

The required sequence is:

$$\textbf{MSA} \;\rightarrow\; \textbf{Stability} \;\rightarrow\; \textbf{Distribution} \;\rightarrow\; \textbf{Capability} \;\rightarrow\; \textbf{Performance}$$

**Why this order, step by step:**

1. **MSA first.** The observed variation is $\sigma^2_{total} = \sigma^2_{process} + \sigma^2_{measurement}$. If the measurement system contributes 30% of the variation, the calculated $C_p$ belongs to the process+measurement combination, not to the process alone. Improving the process won't move the number.
2. **Stability next.** A $C_{pk}$ calculated over a process with special causes is a statistic of a distribution that doesn't exist. It doesn't meaningfully describe the past, nor does it predict the future.
3. **Distribution after that.** Every conventional index assumes normality when converted into ppm. Without that assumption, the index still calculates, but the conversion to a defect rate comes out wrong — sometimes by orders of magnitude.
4. **Only then the indices.**

### Verifying stability before capability

Step 2 above — stability — isn't a formality: it's what guarantees that the distribution the indices are about to be calculated from actually exists. There are three complementary fronts for checking it.

**Control charts.** The appropriate chart depends on the nature of the data and the subgroup size: X-bar/R or X-bar/S for subgroups larger than 1 (R for small subgroups, S for larger ones, see Part VI), and I-MR (individuals and moving range) when no rational subgroup is possible, as with unit measurements spaced out over time. The reading looks for patterns that indicate a special cause: points beyond the limits, continuous trends (drift), systematic alternation between high and low values, periodic cycles, and atypical clustering near one of the limits — the same Nelson/Western Electric rules covered in Chapter 67.

**Normality.** Most classic capability formulas assume an approximately normal distribution. Verifying this combines formal tests — Shapiro-Wilk (more sensitive with small and moderate samples, used in the worked example below) or Anderson-Darling (gives extra weight to the tails, which matters because that's exactly where ppm gets decided) — with graphical inspection via a normal probability plot and a histogram overlaid with the estimated density. The assessment isn't binary: small deviations from normality, common in industrial practice, don't necessarily invalidate the classic indices, as long as the limitation is acknowledged. When a characteristic is physically incapable of being normal, the problem isn't sample size — it's the chosen model (Chapter 72).

**Trend analysis.** Complements the chart by identifying gradual changes still within the control limits, but that slowly alter the process's behavior over time: tool wear, an instrument gradually losing calibration, a reagent degrading, or an operator's practice changing over the course of a shift. This kind of drift is better caught by time-series analysis on the chart's own signal than by a single point-in-time hypothesis test.

Ignoring any of these three fronts has a recurring consequence: when the within-subgroup standard deviation (used in Cp/Cpk) is much smaller than the overall standard deviation (used in Pp/Ppk) because of undetected drift between subgroups, the Cp/Cpk pair creates a false impression of high capability — the process looks potentially very good, but the actual performance, captured by Pp/Ppk, is much worse. This is exactly the symptom the note on the Cp/Pp ratio, in the diagnostic table further below, was designed to catch.

### The indices, with explicit denominators

**Capability (short-term, potential)** — uses $\hat{\sigma}_{within}$, estimated **from within the subgroups**:

$$\hat{\sigma}_{within} = \frac{\bar{R}}{d_2} \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{\bar{s}}{c_4} \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{\overline{MR}}{d_2}\;(n=1)$$

$$C_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{6\hat{\sigma}_{within}} \qquad C_{pk} = \min\left(\frac{USL-\mu}{3\hat{\sigma}_{within}},\; \frac{\mu-LSL}{3\hat{\sigma}_{within}}\right)$$

**Performance (long-term, actual)** — uses $\hat{\sigma}_{overall}$, the sample standard deviation of **all** the data:

$$P_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{6\hat{\sigma}_{overall}} \qquad P_{pk} = \min\left(\frac{USL-\mu}{3\hat{\sigma}_{overall}},\; \frac{\mu-LSL}{3\hat{\sigma}_{overall}}\right)$$

**Taguchi indices** — penalize deviation from the **target** $T$, not just from the specification:

$$C_{pm} = \frac{USL-LSL}{6\sqrt{\sigma^2 + (\mu - T)^2}} \qquad C_{pmk} = \frac{\min(USL-\mu,\ \mu-LSL)}{3\sqrt{\sigma^2 + (\mu-T)^2}}$$

**One-sided** — when only one limit exists (roughness, contamination, minimum strength). Report $C_{pu}$ or $C_{pl}$ on its own; **don't** invent the missing limit.

$$C_{pu} = \frac{USL - \mu}{3\sigma} \qquad C_{pl} = \frac{\mu - LSL}{3\sigma}$$

### Sources of variation and how to read the number

Before interpreting any index, it's worth recalling where the variation it measures comes from. Every process carries natural dispersion resulting from factors intrinsic to the system — the so-called 6M's: **machine** (wear, play, vibration), **material** (variation between raw-material lots), **method** (procedural differences), **manpower** (variation between operators), **environment** (temperature, humidity, external vibration), and **measurement** (the measurement system itself, covered in MSA Step 1). When these factors stay stable over time, the variability is predictable and repeatable — that's exactly the condition Step 2 (stability) verifies before any calculation.

Once calculated, the indices' absolute reading follows bands widely used in industrial practice — none of them is a statistical law, and all should be read together with the diagnostic table below and the confidence interval discussed further on:

| Cpk | Typical reading |
|---|---|
| < 1.00 | Typically incapable process — a measurable share of production falls outside the specification |
| 1.00 – 1.33 | Operating at the edge; elevated risk of nonconformance under any additional drift |
| ≥ 1.33 | Common acceptance criterion in many industrial programs — not universal (see the PPAP note further below) |
| ≥ 1.67 | Robust, repeatable operation, typical of safety-critical characteristics |

A point $C_{pk}$ of 1.33 calculated on a small sample can, in practice, have a true value as low as 1.00 — the table above is only useful read together with $n$ and the confidence interval.

### The diagnostic table that's rarely taught

The indices are more useful read **together** than in isolation:

| Observed pattern | Diagnosis | Action |
|---|---|---|
| $C_p \approx C_{pk}$, both high | Centered, capable process | Maintain; monitor |
| $C_p$ high, $C_{pk}$ low | **Off-center.** The variation is fine | Adjust the mean — usually cheap and fast |
| $C_p$ low, $C_p \approx C_{pk}$ | **Excessive variation**, centered | Reduce variation — DMAIC/DOE project |
| $C_p \gg P_p$ | Variation **between** subgroups: drift, shifts, lots | Investigate the source; the chart should have caught it |
| $C_{pk}$ high, $C_{pm}$ low | Within spec but far from the **target** | Relevant if there's a quadratic loss (assemblies, adjustments) |
| All high but there are complaints | Suspect the **MSA** or the distribution | Go back to step 1 |

**Note on the $C_p/P_p$ ratio.** This ratio is the most direct way to quantify how much variation is escaping between subgroups. Ideally close to 1. Values above 1.3 indicate instability the control chart should be flagging.

### Worked example: complete analysis with confidence intervals

```py-r
--- python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

LSL, USL, ALVO = 9.80, 10.20, 10.00
n_sub, tam_sub = 30, 5

# Processo: sigma dentro do subgrupo pequeno, mas com deriva entre subgrupos
sigma_within = 0.035
deriva = np.linspace(0, 0.045, n_sub) + rng.normal(0, 0.012, n_sub)
dados = np.array([rng.normal(10.02 + deriva[i], sigma_within, tam_sub)
                  for i in range(n_sub)])
todos = dados.ravel()

# --- Passo 1: MSA (assumido feito; ver PARTE VI) ---
grr_pct = 12.0
print(f"PASSO 1 — MSA: %GRR = {grr_pct:.1f}%  "
      f"({'aceitável' if grr_pct < 10 else 'condicionalmente aceitável (10-30%)'})")
print(f"   Nota: a variação de medição inflaciona sigma. "
      f"sigma_processo ≈ sigma_observado × sqrt(1 - {grr_pct/100:.2f}²) "
      f"= {np.sqrt(1-(grr_pct/100)**2):.3f} × sigma_observado\n")

# --- Passo 2: estabilidade ---
d2, A2 = 2.326, 0.577                       # n = 5
xbar = dados.mean(axis=1); R = dados.max(axis=1) - dados.min(axis=1)
xbb, Rb = xbar.mean(), R.mean()
lsc_x, lic_x = xbb + A2*Rb, xbb - A2*Rb
fora = int(((xbar > lsc_x) | (xbar < lic_x)).sum())
# regra 3 de Nelson: 6 pontos consecutivos a subir
dif = np.diff(xbar)
tend = sum(1 for i in range(5, len(dif)) if np.all(dif[i-5:i+1] > 0))
print(f"PASSO 2 — Estabilidade:")
print(f"   Pontos fora dos limites: {fora}/{n_sub}")
print(f"   Sequências de 6 pontos crescentes (regra 3): {tend}")
estavel = (fora == 0 and tend == 0)
print(f"   => Processo {'ESTÁVEL' if estavel else 'INSTÁVEL — há deriva'}")
if not estavel:
    print("      Os índices abaixo são calculados na mesma, mas com a ressalva")
    print("      de que Cp/Cpk descrevem um processo que não é reprodutível.\n")

# --- Passo 3: distribuição ---
W, p_sw = stats.shapiro(todos)
print(f"PASSO 3 — Normalidade (Shapiro-Wilk): W = {W:.4f}, p = {p_sw:.4f}"
      f"  => {'compatível com normal' if p_sw > 0.05 else 'REJEITA normalidade'}\n")

# --- Passo 4 e 5: índices ---
sigma_w = Rb / d2
sigma_o = todos.std(ddof=1)
mu = todos.mean()

Cp  = (USL - LSL) / (6*sigma_w)
Cpu = (USL - mu) / (3*sigma_w); Cpl = (mu - LSL) / (3*sigma_w)
Cpk = min(Cpu, Cpl)
Pp  = (USL - LSL) / (6*sigma_o)
Ppk = min((USL - mu)/(3*sigma_o), (mu - LSL)/(3*sigma_o))
Cpm = (USL - LSL) / (6*np.sqrt(sigma_o**2 + (mu - ALVO)**2))
Cpmk = min(USL-mu, mu-LSL) / (3*np.sqrt(sigma_o**2 + (mu-ALVO)**2))

# Intervalos de confiança a 95% (aproximação de Kushler-Hurley para Cpk)
N = len(todos)
ic_cp = (Cp*np.sqrt(stats.chi2.ppf(0.025, N-1)/(N-1)),
         Cp*np.sqrt(stats.chi2.ppf(0.975, N-1)/(N-1)))
se_cpk = np.sqrt(1/(9*N) + Cpk**2/(2*(N-1)))
ic_cpk = (Cpk - 1.96*se_cpk, Cpk + 1.96*se_cpk)

print("PASSO 4/5 — Índices:")
tab = pd.DataFrame([
    ("Cp   (potencial, curto prazo)", Cp,  f"[{ic_cp[0]:.2f} ; {ic_cp[1]:.2f}]"),
    ("Cpk  (real, curto prazo)",      Cpk, f"[{ic_cpk[0]:.2f} ; {ic_cpk[1]:.2f}]"),
    ("Pp   (potencial, longo prazo)", Pp,  ""),
    ("Ppk  (real, longo prazo)",      Ppk, ""),
    ("Cpm  (Taguchi, vs alvo)",       Cpm, ""),
    ("Cpmk (Taguchi, unilateral)",    Cpmk, ""),
], columns=["Índice", "Valor", "IC 95%"])
tab["Valor"] = tab["Valor"].round(3)
print(tab.to_string(index=False))

print(f"\n   sigma_within  = {sigma_w:.4f}  (R-barra/d2)")
print(f"   sigma_overall = {sigma_o:.4f}  (desvio-padrão de todos os dados)")
print(f"   Rácio Cp/Pp   = {Cp/Pp:.2f}")

# --- Diagnóstico automático ---
print("\nDIAGNÓSTICO:")
if Cp/Pp > 1.3:
    print(f"   • Cp/Pp = {Cp/Pp:.2f} >> 1 : existe variação ENTRE subgrupos "
          f"não capturada dentro deles (deriva).")
if Cp - Cpk > 0.15:
    desloc = abs(mu - (USL+LSL)/2)
    print(f"   • Cp ({Cp:.2f}) > Cpk ({Cpk:.2f}) : processo DESCENTRADO em "
          f"{desloc:.4f} ({desloc/sigma_w:.2f} sigma).")
    Cpk_se_centrado = Cp
    print(f"     Centrando a média, Cpk subiria para ≈ {Cpk_se_centrado:.2f}.")
if Cpm < Cpk - 0.1:
    print(f"   • Cpm ({Cpm:.2f}) < Cpk ({Cpk:.2f}) : afastado do ALVO "
          f"(média {mu:.4f} vs alvo {ALVO}).")

# --- ppm esperado ---
print("\nTAXA DE DEFEITO ESPERADA (assumindo normalidade):")
for nome, s in [("curto prazo (within)", sigma_w), ("longo prazo (overall)", sigma_o)]:
    ppm = (stats.norm.sf((USL-mu)/s) + stats.norm.cdf((LSL-mu)/s)) * 1e6
    print(f"   {nome:<24}: {ppm:10.1f} ppm")
obs = ((todos > USL) | (todos < LSL)).mean()*1e6
print(f"   {'observado na amostra':<24}: {obs:10.1f} ppm  (n = {N})")

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 4))
axes[0].plot(xbar, "o-", ms=4, color="#00688f")
axes[0].axhline(lsc_x, color="r", ls="--"); axes[0].axhline(lic_x, color="r", ls="--")
axes[0].axhline(xbb, color="g"); axes[0].set_title("Carta X-barra — verificar estabilidade PRIMEIRO")
axes[1].hist(todos, bins=22, density=True, alpha=0.65, color="#6fa8be", edgecolor="white")
xs = np.linspace(min(todos.min(), LSL)-0.02, max(todos.max(), USL)+0.02, 300)
axes[1].plot(xs, stats.norm.pdf(xs, mu, sigma_w), lw=2, label="within (curto prazo)")
axes[1].plot(xs, stats.norm.pdf(xs, mu, sigma_o), lw=2, ls="--", label="overall (longo prazo)")
for v, c, l in [(LSL,"red","LSL"), (USL,"red","USL"), (ALVO,"green","Alvo")]:
    axes[1].axvline(v, color=c, ls="-" if c=="red" else ":", lw=1.5)
    axes[1].text(v, axes[1].get_ylim()[1]*0.95, l, ha="center", fontsize=8)
axes[1].legend(fontsize=8); axes[1].set_title(f"Cp={Cp:.2f}  Cpk={Cpk:.2f}  Pp={Pp:.2f}  Ppk={Ppk:.2f}")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

--- python
%%R
set.seed(2026)
LSL <- 9.80; USL <- 10.20; ALVO <- 10.00
n_sub <- 30; tam <- 5; sigma_w_real <- 0.035
deriva <- seq(0, 0.045, length.out = n_sub) + rnorm(n_sub, 0, 0.012)
dados <- t(sapply(1:n_sub, function(i) rnorm(tam, 10.02 + deriva[i], sigma_w_real)))
todos <- as.vector(dados)

d2 <- 2.326
Rb <- mean(apply(dados, 1, function(x) max(x) - min(x)))
sigma_w <- Rb / d2
sigma_o <- sd(todos); mu <- mean(todos)

Cp  <- (USL - LSL) / (6*sigma_w)
Cpk <- min((USL - mu)/(3*sigma_w), (mu - LSL)/(3*sigma_w))
Pp  <- (USL - LSL) / (6*sigma_o)
Ppk <- min((USL - mu)/(3*sigma_o), (mu - LSL)/(3*sigma_o))
Cpm <- (USL - LSL) / (6*sqrt(sigma_o^2 + (mu - ALVO)^2))

cat(sprintf("sigma_within = %.4f | sigma_overall = %.4f\n", sigma_w, sigma_o))
cat(sprintf("Cp = %.3f | Cpk = %.3f | Pp = %.3f | Ppk = %.3f | Cpm = %.3f\n",
            Cp, Cpk, Pp, Ppk, Cpm))
cat(sprintf("Racio Cp/Pp = %.2f %s\n", Cp/Pp,
            ifelse(Cp/Pp > 1.3, "-> variacao ENTRE subgrupos", "-> ok")))
cat(sprintf("ppm esperado (longo prazo) = %.1f\n",
            1e6*(pnorm(LSL, mu, sigma_o) + pnorm(USL, mu, sigma_o, lower.tail = FALSE))))
cat(sprintf("Shapiro-Wilk p = %.4f\n", shapiro.test(todos)$p.value))

### On the confidence interval — and why it matters for PPAP

$C_{pk}$ is an **estimate**, and a noisy one. With $n = 30$, the 95% confidence interval for a point estimate $C_{pk}$ of 1.33 is approximately $[1.00 ; 1.66]$.

The practical consequence is serious: **a customer requiring $C_{pk} \geq 1.33$ with 30 parts is, in fact, accepting processes whose true $C_{pk}$ could be 1.00.** Some customers and organizations adopt sample-size and minimum-index criteria for initial process studies/PPAP, but **there's no single universal value applicable to every program**. The criteria should be set by the process design, the customer/OEM, the control plan, and the specific PPAP/IATF requirements.

Reporting $C_{pk}$ without $n$ and without a confidence interval is, strictly speaking, reporting a number with no statistical meaning.

### Common mistakes

1. **Calculating capability on an unstable process.** Mistake number one. The index becomes an average of different states.
2. **Reporting only $C_{pk}$.** Without $C_p$, you can't distinguish off-centering from excess variation — and these are problems with completely different costs and solutions.
3. **Confusing $C_p$ with $P_p$.** Many software packages and spreadsheets use the sample standard deviation and call it $C_p$. Always check which sigma is in the denominator.
4. **Converting $C_{pk}$ into ppm without checking normality.** With skewed data, the error can be one or two orders of magnitude — see the next chapter.
5. **Ignoring the MSA.** With a %GRR of 30%, about 9% of the observed variance comes from the instrument. The process's real $C_p$ is better than what's calculated, and no work on the process will move it up.
6. **Automatically applying the "1.5σ shift."** It's a historical Six Sigma convention for relating short-term and long-term, not a law. If you have long-term data, use $P_{pk}$ and skip the convention.
7. **Inventing the missing limit on a one-sided specification.** Report $C_{pu}$ or $C_{pl}$.
8. **Using small samples for the capability estimate.** They generate artificial fluctuation that can falsely suggest incapacity or excess capacity — always compare against the confidence interval, not just the point value.
9. **Not standardizing the data collection method.** Different operators, mixed collection times, equipment changes, or inconsistent periodicity introduce variability that doesn't come from the process, but from how it was measured.

### Using capability to prioritize improvements

Beyond deciding whether a process can be released, the indices are useful for prioritizing where to invest continuous-improvement effort across multiple processes or characteristics. A practical rule used in project selection:

- **$C_{pk} < 1.00$** → immediate action; a natural candidate for a DMAIC Improve project, with short-term containment while the root cause is investigated.
- **$C_{pk}$ between 1.00 and 1.33** → significant improvement opportunity; priority depends on customer impact, the cost of nonconformance, and regulatory criticality.
- **$C_{pk} \geq 1.33$** → monitor via the control chart; not a priority candidate for a new project.

Prioritization should also look at the relationship between short and long term, not just the isolated $C_{pk}$ value: when $C_p$ is adequate but $P_p$ is insufficient, the problem is stability or standardization, not intrinsic capability — the right project targets the process, not the product. When $C_p$ itself is already low, the problem lies in intrinsic variation and tends to require revisiting design parameters, method, equipment, or raw material — a DOE project (Part VIII) is often the path.

### Capability's role across the DMAIC cycle

The indices aren't an isolated calculation — they run through the whole DMAIC cycle (Part IV):

- **Measure** — $C_{pk}$ and $P_{pk}$ quantify initial performance (the baseline) and translate variation and centering into a single metric comparable across projects.
- **Analyze** — comparing short and long term ($C_p$ vs. $P_p$, $C_{pk}$ vs. $P_{pk}$) points to whether the dominant cause is intrinsic variation or a lack of control/stability, guiding which analysis tools come next.
- **Improve** — recalculating the indices after actions are implemented is the objective test that the change worked, not the team's opinion.
- **Control** — ongoing monitoring of capability, together with the control chart, ensures the gain achieved is sustained over time, instead of disappearing within a few weeks.

The same logic underpins process validation (IQ/OQ/PQ, Chapter 97) and integration with SPC (Part VI): capability is the quantitative evidence that an already-qualified process keeps operating within what was promised.
