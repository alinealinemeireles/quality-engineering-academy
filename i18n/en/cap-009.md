## Chapter 9: Approaching the Problem

### Problem Functions: y = f(x)

Since Six Sigma approaches things with a statistical mindset, it considers every problem to be a function. Using mathematical symbols, that looks like:

The statement y=f(x) can be used in two ways. First, it's a general roadmap for stating a problem. Y (the problem) happens because some X (input or cause) is happening. In reality, Y usually happens because of some group of causes or inputs, which means there'll be more than one X.

The idea can also be applied to specific processes and outcomes within the problem. As you get more and more granular, the y=f(x) concept becomes increasingly mathematical; in many cases, you can graph the relationship between the outcome (y) and the input (x).

To understand the concept of thinking about problems as a function, let's look at a problem that could happen at a large HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) service provider. A service team manager found that service calls are taking much longer than expected; in fact, her five team members take, on average, 1.75 times as long as other company service reps to handle every kind of call.

To find out what might be causing this situation, the manager researches the problem by talking with the reps, talking with customers, and shadowing random calls with all five reps. She makes the following observations:

- One rep is a native of the area the team serves, which means he personally knows many of the customers. This results in friendly conversations that stretch out the service time.
- One rep is giving homeowners very in-depth explanations and education about HVAC issues, sometimes beyond anything the homeowner would ever need to know about their HVAC unit.
- One rep is new to the job and takes longer to complete each task because they're unsure of the work, need to double-check their work, or call another rep with questions.
- The other two reps do the work at times consistent with the company averages.

The manager distills this data down into two general causes for the problem:

- Excessive talking (reps one and two)
- Inadequate training

The problem can now be stated as a function:

**Extra time = f(excessive talking, inadequate training)**

Extra time is a function of excessive talking and inadequate training.

The manager now has two root causes to address. The example is simple, but it illustrates the basic concept of defining a y=f(x) relationship for a problem and its causes. It isn't always this easy to run the research and analysis to find the relationship, but the relationship is always present.

A few other examples of y=f(x) relationships include:

- Low customer satisfaction with burger flavor is a function of a miscalibrated griddle.
- Low employee morale is a function of a poor time-off approval system.
- Customer wait time is a function of technology distractions for employees.

### The 5 Whys

Data analysis is one of the best ways to validate a y=f(x) assumption, but teams familiar with processes can often arrive at a few basic relationships through a process known as the 5 Whys. This is a brainstorming tool that asks increasingly granular "why" questions about a problem or process, seeking to understand the root cause or the real problem. The 5 Whys can be used to define a problem or to start looking for causes.

For example, consider the burger example above. Teams handling a customer satisfaction problem might start doing so because feedback forms showed lower-than-normal satisfaction with food quality last week.

The team first asks: Why are customers unhappy with the food?

Looking at feedback tied to orders, the team notices customers who rate the food poorly are mostly customers who ordered some kind of burger. The answer to the first question is that customers are unhappy with the food because they're unhappy with the burgers.

Why are customers unhappy with the burgers?

The team looks at the written feedback on the forms or talks directly with customers and finds many customers felt their burgers were undercooked. The new answer is that customers are unhappy with the burgers because the meat is undercooked.

Why is the meat undercooked?

An investigation in the kitchen reveals the griddle isn't properly calibrated and is producing inconsistent results. At this point, you already have the y=f(x) relationship, but the team could keep asking questions.

Why isn't the griddle properly calibrated?

Further investigation shows the morning shift, responsible for calibrating the griddle, has a new cook. During training, education on how to perform this function was skipped. The griddle isn't properly calibrated because the responsible employee wasn't properly trained. Now the team has a specific cause and a solution: train the griddle cook.

In a Six Sigma setting, the team could keep going with one more question: Why wasn't the griddle cook properly trained? That could lead to developing a consistent training policy, so the problem doesn't happen the next time a new cook is hired.

In the burger example, it only took four "why" questions to get to the root of the problem, and a fifth question started pointing toward long-term controls or solutions. It isn't always this easy; the tool is called the 5 Whys because it often leads to answers within five questions. However, teams might need to ask a dozen questions if they start at a very high level and work through a complex process.

#### When to Use the 5 Whys

One benefit of the 5 Whys is that it costs your team only a small amount of time — a team familiar with a process can run a complete 5 Whys session in under an hour if a facilitator keeps things focused. Because of its simplicity, the 5 Whys tool can be used for almost any problem. Use it to address a problem raised by team members, to address a problem noticed by a supervisor, or to address the vague sense that a problem exists when no one has been able to pin down exactly what's wrong. At minimum, a 5 Whys session facilitates communication and thinking.

In a Six Sigma project setting, the 5 Whys are generally deployed when processes involve human interactions or people-driven inputs, though they can be an effective starting point for brainstorming on any process.

#### Running a 5 Whys Session

Since a 5 Whys session generally relies on input from subject-matter experts, gather people who are close to the process. On a whiteboard or web-conferencing screen, display a basic statement of the problem as you understand it. This statement won't be as detailed as the statements we'll discuss in the next section — a 5 Whys session is often one of the tools used to arrive at that detailed statement.

Examples of statements you might see in a 5 Whys session include:

- Customers aren't satisfied with the produce selection
- Customers are receiving late orders
- The printing process is resulting in too many defects
- Lead times in the bottling process are excessive
- Employees aren't satisfied with vacation scheduling

These are all fairly general statements that only say something about defects or dissatisfaction. Start by asking the highest-level possible "Why?" question about the statement. "Why aren't employees satisfied with vacation scheduling?" Write that question down.

The team works together to provide a high-level answer to the question. Employees aren't satisfied with vacation scheduling because it's rare to get exactly the requested time off. Write the answer below the question, then create the highest-level possible "Why?" question about the new answer.

"Why can't employees get their first choice of vacation time?" Maybe the answer is that supervisors take so long to approve vacation requests that other employees have also requested off for the same period, making it hard for supervisors to accommodate everyone.

The next question is "Why are supervisors taking too long to approve requests?" The answer might be that the time-off request system is too complicated, so supervisors put off approvals until they have plenty of time to manage them.

"Why do supervisors see the system as complicated?" Because there are wait times when switching between screens, and every approval requires a large number of clicks and entries.

Now the team has a root cause: the system itself is inefficient, which leads to problems further down the line. If the team can fix the programming issues and encourage supervisors to approve vacation requests faster, employee morale might improve.

### Building a Problem Statement

A Six Sigma improvement project generally starts with a formal project statement. This is different from the basic statements used to kick off a 5 Whys session. A strong problem statement is similar to a 30-second elevator pitch, the kind executives and salespeople around the world use to hook customers or investors on an idea. The problem statement, like that pitch, provides enough information for a busy executive to understand what the issue is and why an improvement effort is needed.

Project statements should include:

- Where and when the problem was logged or was occurring
- A measure of the problem's magnitude, preferably tied to some cost
- A brief description of the problem that can be understood by professionals not closely familiar with the process (avoid too many niche terms and acronyms if you're presenting information to professionals outside the niche)
- A brief indication of the metric used to measure or describe the problem

#### Example of a Strong Problem Statement

*In the first quarter, the California distribution center shipped 108,000 packages. Of those packages, 15,000 were returned, resulting in a 13.8% return rate. The return rate is above the accepted rate of 7% and cost the company an additional $372,000 for the quarter. Over the course of the year, the current process could result in additional costs of over $1.4 million.*

This problem statement covers all the basic information:

- When? During this year's first quarter.
- Where? The California distribution center
- What? Returns
- How many? 15,000, or 6.8% above expected
- What's the magnitude? The cost could be $1.4 million a year

The problem statement doesn't talk about solutions or provide too much detail. This is a strong problem statement because it answers every basic question and provides a meaningful reason for leadership to invest interest: $1.4 million a year is a big loss.

#### Example of a Weak Problem Statement

*The Canton, Ohio bakery is producing undercooked bread. Customer dissatisfaction with the bread is resulting in returns and bad word of mouth. The bread should be baked at 350 degrees for 40 minutes.*

This statement introduces a problem, but it doesn't provide detail on when the problem happened, how it was measured, and what the real magnitude is. The problem statement also starts drifting into possible root causes by including how the bread should be baked; the problem statement isn't the place to start that kind of analysis.

This statement could be better phrased as:

*In November and December 2014, customer dissatisfaction complaints were traced back to bread baked at the Canton, Ohio facility. The facility produced 300,000 loaves during that period and received 50,000 complaints of undercooked bread. Bread returns and lost sales related to the quality issue are estimated at $125,000 a month.*

#### Writing Your Own Problem Statement

When you start writing problem statements, it's sometimes harder than you'd expect to fit all the information into a few sentences. To avoid leaving out information, it helps to use a checklist and think of yourself as a "problem statement journalist."

When journalists write a story, they seek to answer specific questions: What happened? Who did it happen to? When did it happen? Why should the audience care?

The same goes when you're writing a problem statement. Follow the problem statement checklist:

- Where did the problem occur?
- When did the problem occur?
- What process did the problem involve?
- How is the problem measured?
- How much is the problem costing (in money, time, customer satisfaction, or another critical metric)?

Use the checklist to build the problem statement, then ask yourself: could someone answer every checklist question using just your problem statement? Before presenting your statement to a boss or other decision-maker, test it out on a colleague or someone not as familiar with the issue as you are.

Here are two problem statements. See if you can answer every checklist question using just the information provided.

**Problem Statement 1**

*The call center in Jacksonville, Florida handled 36,000 calls in February 2015. Of those calls, 8,000 had an average speed of answer (ASA) above the 15 seconds required by contract. Those 8,000 service level agreement violations resulted in $200,000 in costs.*

**Problem Statement 2**

*The call center in Ohio has a service level agreement problem that's costing approximately $9,000 a day.*

Problem statement 1 answers every checklist question:

- Where did the problem occur? Jacksonville, Florida
- When did the problem occur? February 2015
- What process did the problem involve? Answering phone calls
- How is the problem measured? Average speed of answer
- How much is the problem costing? $200,000 a month

Problem statement 2 doesn't answer every question:

- Where did the problem occur? Ohio
- When did the problem occur? Unknown
- What process did the problem involve? Unknown
- How is the problem measured? Unknown
- How much is the problem costing? $9,000 a month

Problem statement 2 would benefit from adding a location, a specific reference to a process, and a specific metric.

#### Problem Statements Lead to Goal Statements

Another way to know you have a strong problem statement is that you can build a general project objective or goal statement directly from the problem statement. Consider the two examples above. The team working with problem statement 1 could create a goal that says:

*The goal is to reduce speed-of-answer SLA violations at the Jacksonville call center by 50% within three months. The potential savings for the company is $100,000 a month.*

The team working with problem statement 2 couldn't build a goal statement with that level of detail. They could only say they hope to reduce service level agreement violations at the facility.

Specific problem and goal statements are fundamental to Six Sigma project success for several reasons. First, being as specific as possible sets proper expectations. In the first example, leadership has a specific expectation for the project: the team will work to reduce average speed of answer, and success is a 50% reduction. No one will expect the team to solve another problem, like customer satisfaction with phone operators. That's out of scope for this project.

In the second situation, the problem and goal statements aren't specific enough. Which SLA violations is the team addressing? What exactly does success look like? Is the team expected to reduce costs completely? Not being specific enough sets you up for failure. Leadership might expect you to handle service level agreements related to how reps route calls, while you intend to only handle service level agreements related to how quickly calls are answered. Leadership might think success is a 75% reduction in costs, when you intend to work toward a 25% reduction.

Building strong problem statements sets a solid foundation for the rest of your project, gives the team a beacon when they get overwhelmed with information, and reduces the chance of scope creep and misunderstandings.

### Scope and Scope Creep

Scope is the definition of what's included — and what isn't included — in an improvement process or project. You start defining scope with your problem statement. The information you include in the statement offers clues about what you'll work on, and the goal statement provides proper boundaries for the work to be done.

Six Sigma projects aren't eternal initiatives, though the improvement culture that comes from Six Sigma is. That means your individual project needs a specific goal, one that's challenging but achievable. Once that goal is reached, the project is finished and you start looking for a new problem to improve.

Scope creep happens when teams seek to make endless improvements to a process, try to reach unrealistic goals, or start reaching into processes or problems that are outside the original scope. For example, consider the problem statement from one of our examples in this chapter:

*In the first quarter, the California distribution center shipped 108,000 packages. Of those packages, 15,000 were returned, resulting in a 13.8% return rate. The return rate is above the accepted rate of 7% and cost the company an additional $372,000 for the quarter. Over the course of the year, the current process could result in additional costs of over $1.4 million.*

A related goal statement might be:

*The goal is to reduce the return rate to the accepted 7% and save the company $372,000 per quarter.*

Within this project's scope are the processes related to shipping and returns only to the extent they impact the return process. At some point, the team might come across a packaging process using too much material, costing the company an additional $50,000 a month. Unless the packaging process is causing the returns — which isn't likely in this situation — that issue isn't within the team's scope, and they shouldn't try to fix it. The team can, however, note the issue or report it so a future project might be launched to address the problem.

It takes discipline and organization to handle only what's within a project's scope. Understanding the relationship between problems and inputs and knowing how to build a strong problem statement are the first steps toward controlling an improvement process.
