## Chapter 174: Cross-Correlation and Leading Indicators

<h3 id="the-engineering-question">The engineering question</h3><p><em>This week's sales "announce" production two weeks from now — or is that just an impression from someone who already knows what happened afterward, looking at two overlapping charts? And if two series genuinely move together, which one comes first?</em></p>
<p>A "leading indicator" only deserves that name if it survives a formal test — not a visual inspection of two charts that seem to track each other. This chapter covers the tools that make that check rigorous: lagged variables as an explicit model input, the cross-correlation function to find at what lag two related series connect, and a formal — not just visual — test of predictive precedence.</p><h3 id="lagged-variables-lag-features-vs-simple-moving-average">Lagged variables (lag features) vs. simple moving average</h3><p>A <strong>lagged variable</strong> (lag feature) is simply the value of a series at an earlier point in time, used as a predictor at the current point:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="X^{(k)}_t = X_{t-k}"></div>
<p>This is conceptually different from a <strong>moving average</strong>, which smooths several previous points into a single aggregate value (<span class="math-inline" data-math="\bar{X}_{t-k:t-1}"></span>). A moving average is useful for removing short-term noise, but it <strong>blurs the exact timing</strong> of the signal — if the goal is to measure how far ahead an event in <span class="math-inline" data-math="X"></span> precedes an event in <span class="math-inline" data-math="Y"></span>, a lagged variable preserves that specific-lag information, and a moving average destroys it. In a predictive model (like those in <a href="#/aula/cap-111">Chapter 111</a>), specific lag features — not moving averages — are the right input when the question is "what did I already know <span class="math-inline" data-math="k"></span> periods ago that helps predict today?".</p><h3 id="what-actually-counts-as-a-leading-indicator">What actually counts as a leading indicator</h3><p>Formally: <span class="math-inline" data-math="X"></span> is a leading indicator of <span class="math-inline" data-math="Y"></span> at lag <span class="math-inline" data-math="k"></span> if the past value of <span class="math-inline" data-math="X"></span> at <span class="math-inline" data-math="t-k"></span> helps predict <span class="math-inline" data-math="Y_t"></span> in a way that's consistent and statistically detectable — not just a visual coincidence in one stretch of the chart.</p>
<p>The standard formal test for that question is <strong>Granger causality</strong>: you compare a model that predicts <span class="math-inline" data-math="Y_t"></span> using only <span class="math-inline" data-math="Y"></span>'s own past against a model that also includes <span class="math-inline" data-math="X"></span>'s past; if the second model predicts significantly better (an F-test on the reduction in residual sum of squares), <span class="math-inline" data-math="X"></span> is said to "Granger-cause" <span class="math-inline" data-math="Y"></span>.</p>
<p><strong>Watch the name.</strong> Granger "causality" is, in fact, a test of <strong>predictive precedence</strong>, not physical causality in the sense of <a href="#/aula/cap-137">Chapter 137</a> (DAGs, causality, and experimentation). Two series can Granger-cause each other simply because both respond to a common unobserved cause — the test doesn't distinguish that. Treat a significant Granger result as evidence that it's worth investigating the underlying business mechanism, not as proof that the mechanism exists.</p><h3 id="cross-correlation-function-ccf">Cross-correlation function (CCF)</h3><p>The <strong>cross-correlation function</strong> (CCF) generalizes autocorrelation (already covered in <a href="#/aula/cap-055">Chapter 55</a>) to two different series, computing the correlation between <span class="math-inline" data-math="X"></span> and <span class="math-inline" data-math="Y"></span> at each lag <span class="math-inline" data-math="k"></span>:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="r_k(X, Y) = \frac{\sum_t (X_t - \bar{X})(Y_{t+k} - \bar{Y})}{\sqrt{\sum_t (X_t - \bar{X})^2 \sum_t (Y_t - \bar{Y})^2}}"></div>
<p>Unlike the ACF (which only has one "side," since a series always correlates with itself symmetrically), the CCF has two sides: positive and negative lags tell different stories about which series comes first. A correlation peak at lag <span class="math-inline" data-math="k &gt; 0"></span> suggests that values of <span class="math-inline" data-math="X"></span> anticipate future values of <span class="math-inline" data-math="Y"></span>; the reverse holds for <span class="math-inline" data-math="k &lt; 0"></span>. No clear peak at any lag, with values close to zero throughout the function, is evidence that the two series simply aren't related over time — don't force a leading-indicator reading where the CCF shows no signal.</p><h3 id="interpreting-who-leads-whom-production-pulled-vs-pushed-by-sales">Interpreting who leads whom: production ‘pulled’ vs. ‘pushed’ by sales</h3><p>In a manufacturing setting, the practical reading of a CCF peak usually translates into one of two opposite operational narratives:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Production "pulled" by sales</strong> — sales lead production (peak at a positive lag): the plant reacts to already-confirmed demand, adjusting the volume produced a few weeks later. Useful for capacity planning from the sales funnel.</li>
<li><strong>Production "pushed" toward sales</strong> — production leads sales (peak at a negative lag): volume is decided by an independent production plan, and the resulting inventory is what determines how much is later available to sell. In that case, "predicting sales from production" is the more defensible directional reading, not the reverse.</li>
</ul>
<p>The two narratives carry completely different management implications — the first suggests investing in demand forecasting to size production; the second suggests the real bottleneck is production capacity, not demand. That's why confirming the <em>direction</em> of the lag (via CCF and Granger, not by assumption) is a consequential engineering decision, not just a statistical detail.</p><h3 id="when-this-becomes-a-multivariate-model">When this becomes a multivariate model (and why it usually doesn't need to)</h3><p>When more than two related series exist — for example, sales, production, and inventory level, all influencing each other — the natural extension is a <strong>VAR</strong> (vector autoregression) model, where each series is modeled as a function of the past of all the others simultaneously. That's the standard treatment in the multivariate forecasting literature for this scenario.</p>
<p>In quality and manufacturing practice, though, VAR rarely pays for the extra effort: when there's only <strong>one</strong> clear precedence relationship between two series (like the sales &rarr; production pair in this chapter), a simple dynamic regression — the target series predicted from its own past plus the relevant lags of the leading series, identified via the CCF — is more interpretable, easier to audit, and, with the 1-to-2-year data history typically available, usually about as accurate as a full VAR. Treat VAR as a tool to look for outside this course only once the number of mutually dependent series grows beyond two or three.</p><h3 id="worked-example-sales-as-a-leading-indicator-of-production">Worked example: sales as a leading indicator of production</h3><div class="codetabs"><div class="ct-bar" role="tablist"><button type="button" class="ct-tab on" role="tab" aria-selected="true" id="ct05c3f568-t0">Python</button><button type="button" class="ct-tab" role="tab" aria-selected="false" id="ct05c3f568-t1">R</button></div><div class="ct-pane" role="tabpanel" aria-labelledby="ct05c3f568-t0"><div class="codeblock" data-lang="python"><div class="codebar"><span class="lang">Python</span><button class="btn-copy" type="button">Copy</button></div><pre><code class="language-python">import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import ccf, grangercausalitytests

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
n = 104  # ~2 years of weeks

# Today's sales "push" production 2 weeks later (true lag = 2)
vendas = 500 + 40 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * np.arange(n) / 13) + rng.normal(0, 15, n)
producao = np.empty(n)
lag_real = 2
producao[:lag_real] = 480 + rng.normal(0, 15, lag_real)
producao[lag_real:] = 0.85 * vendas[:-lag_real] + 90 + rng.normal(0, 12, n - lag_real)

d = pd.DataFrame({"vendas": vendas, "producao": producao})

# --- Step 1: cross-correlation (positive = production follows sales) ---
correl = ccf(d["producao"] - d["producao"].mean(), d["vendas"] - d["vendas"].mean())
top_lags = pd.Series(correl[:15]).sort_values(ascending=False).head(3)
print("3 largest cross-correlations (index = lag in weeks):")
print(top_lags.round(3))

# --- Step 2: formal Granger causality test (does sales lead production?) ---
resultado = grangercausalitytests(d[["producao", "vendas"]], maxlag=4, verbose=False)
print("\nGranger test -- does sales lead production?")
for lag in range(1, 5):
    p_valor = resultado[lag][0]["ssr_ftest"][1]
    print(f"  lag {lag} week(s): p-value = {p_valor:.4f}"
          f"  {'(significant)' if p_valor &lt; 0.05 else '(not significant)'}")</code></pre></div></div><div class="ct-pane" hidden role="tabpanel" aria-labelledby="ct05c3f568-t1"><div class="codeblock" data-lang="r"><div class="codebar"><span class="lang">R</span><button class="btn-copy" type="button">Copy</button></div><pre><code class="language-r">set.seed(2026)
