## Chapter 62: Sample Integrity: Random Sampling, Selection Bias, and Chain of Custody

### Why sample integrity matters

Products and samples are always exposed to risks of contamination, deterioration, swapping, or misuse between the moment they're selected and the moment they're actually inspected or tested. This matters especially when sampling serves purposes like qualification programs, customer evaluation, or destructive/nondestructive testing, where a single test's result can determine whether an entire lot is accepted or rejected, a contractual dispute, or even a regulatory decision. A famous, instructive case, often cited as an analogy outside the industrial context, involves criminal trials in which DNA-test evidence was challenged by the defense precisely because it couldn't be proven that the biological sample stayed free of contamination at every moment between collection and analysis. Although extreme, this example nicely illustrates why carefully thought-out controls are needed whenever a sample stands in, for decision purposes, for a larger universe than itself.

In industrial practice, sample integrity is generally discussed across three complementary fronts: the adequacy of the sampling technique itself (avoiding selection bias), documented control over lots, changes, and configurations, and the physical sample's chain of custody.

### Proper random sampling and sources of selection bias

Every acceptance sampling plan starts from the assumption that the sample of n units was randomly selected from the lot — that is, that every unit in the lot had a known, typically equal, chance of being selected. This assumption is what lets you calculate acceptance probabilities using the binomial, Poisson, or hypergeometric distribution; without genuine randomness in the selection, these formulas simply don't apply to the actual case, even though they remain correct in the abstract.

In practice, the biggest threat to randomness usually isn't a sophisticated statistical error, but selection bias stemming from operational convenience. It's extremely common, for example, for an inspector to always pull sample units from the top of a box, the front of a pallet, or the first units produced in a shift — simply because they're the easiest to reach. If, for any reason tied to the production process or logistics handling, these units aren't representative of the rest of the lot (for example, if the last parts produced in a shift tend to show more defects from operator fatigue, or if units at the bottom of a box suffered more shipping damage), the sample will be systematically biased, and the decision about the lot — accept or reject — will no longer reflect the true quality of the lot as a whole.

To mitigate this risk, forming the lots themselves already needs careful thought: a lot meant for an acceptance sampling plan should be as homogeneous as possible, avoiding mixing, within a single lot, units produced by different machines, different shifts, or different production lines — mixtures like this make any sample, no matter how randomly it's selected, poorly informative about the real origin of any nonconformances. Forming large, well-defined lots, with packaging and identification that make random selection easier, is also what lets you use larger samples at a reasonable cost and, in turn, get more discriminating OC curves. Beyond that, whenever feasible, it's preferable to use a truly random, documentable selection method (for example, random numbers applied to numbered positions in the lot) rather than informal convenience rules, and to train inspectors to recognize and avoid the most common convenience patterns — which is, in essence, a practical extension, within the acceptance sampling context, of the random and stratified sampling precautions studied more generally in statistics.

### Lot, change, and configuration control

A second front for sample integrity involves the documented traceability of what's being sampled, especially when the product is manufactured in lots or batches, as opposed to discrete production of individual parts. A lot, in this broader sense (which covers operations like mixing, heating, distilling, and other comparable operations), needs to have logged, in a batch diary or log, what quantities and materials went in, in what order, and at what point in time — since the moment each input is added (heat, pressure, reagent) is often just as important as the quantity added. When tests are needed to verify whether the lot developed the desired properties over time, those test results need to be unambiguously tied to the corresponding physical lot and to every other record for that lot. In the end, each lot should receive an identification code separate from every other lot, printed or stamped on the corresponding packaging, drums, bottles, or pallets; and, when the product is sold, it's often necessary to also maintain a link between the lot number and the customer who received it, so the lot can be traced across the entire distribution chain — which is essential, for example, in the event of a recall.

A second, complementary technique is change control, aimed at relatively simple to moderately complex products that undergo minor changes over time without the product's name or model changing — as happens with appliances and computers that get small revisions under the same model name, identified only by a version number. For warranty, repair, and replacement purposes, every time the product is changed needs to be logged. A common practice is classifying changes into three categories by urgency: emergency change, when a hazardous condition has been found in the current version and the fix can't wait; priority change, when there's a solid economic reason to implement the change quickly, but life and property aren't at immediate risk (for example, a change that cuts energy consumption or maintenance needs); and routine change, for changes that need to be made, but with no urgency — typically to accommodate newly designed parts or give the product a bit more functionality, without justifying an entirely new model.

Finally, configuration control is an extension of change control aimed at complex products, made up of several units and subassemblies, like aerospace vehicles and defense systems, whose field versions gradually evolve as new engines, avionics systems, or hydraulic systems get installed on already-existing units. Managing these ongoing field modifications is, in practice, an adaptation of materials-resource-planning techniques to tracking product configurations, and requires extensive documentation — generally both computerized database records and physical backup copies, often kept at more than one location, given how critical this information is to the product's safety and maintenance across its entire life cycle.

### Chain of custody

While lot, change, and configuration control handles the documented traceability of the product a sample was drawn from, chain of custody handles the physical traceability of the sample itself, from the moment it's pulled from the lot to the moment it's finally tested, archived, or discarded. The central idea is simple to state and, at the same time, demanding to practice: at any moment, it should be possible to say, with documented evidence, who had the sample in hand, where it was physically stored, and that no opportunity for contamination, substitution, damage, or tampering went unlogged between collection and the final result.

A well-built chain of custody generally includes, at minimum: a unique, unambiguous identification for each sample, traceably linked to the lot and the exact point of collection (date, time, location, person responsible for collection); a log that travels with the physical sample, signed or otherwise confirmed at every transfer of responsibility — from whoever collected it to whoever transported it, from whoever transported it to whoever stored it, from whoever stored it to whoever tested it; storage and transport conditions appropriate to the sample's nature, so as to preserve the characteristic that will be assessed (temperature, humidity, light exposure, vibration, among others, as applicable); and, where applicable, tamper-evident packaging or seals, which make any attempt at unauthorized opening or substitution visible before testing.

Chain of custody matters especially in three situations that come up often in a quality engineer's work: destructive testing, in which the sample is consumed by the test itself and therefore can't be re-examined later if there's doubt about its identity or original state; contractual disputes or warranty claims, where a sample's test result can determine financial liability between supplier and customer, and the validity of that result depends on proving the tested sample was, beyond any reasonable doubt, the same sample drawn from the lot in question; and regulatory or legal contexts, where sample traceability might be required as formal evidence, and a gap in the chain of custody can invalidate a test result regardless of its technical quality.

Together, well-executed random sampling, documented lot/change/configuration control, and physical chain of custody form the three layers that ensure the acceptance probability calculated by an OC curve, a Z1.4 plan, or a Z1.9 plan — all discussed in earlier chapters — actually corresponds to the reality of the lot being judged, rather than a distorted or compromised version of it.

### Systematic and cluster sampling

The previous sections covered simple random and stratified sampling. Two designs widely used in industrial inspection and auditing remain: systematic sampling and cluster sampling.

**Systematic sampling.** Instead of drawing each unit independently, the first unit is randomly selected within the first interval, and then every k-th unit is taken from there on:

$$k = \frac{N}{n}, \qquad \text{selected units: } r,\; r+k,\; r+2k,\; \dots$$

where $r$ is randomly chosen between $1$ and $k$. This is the basis of many in-line acceptance sampling plans (for example, "inspect 1 in every 20 units on the conveyor") because it's simple to run without a prior count of the entire lot.

**The risk that makes it dangerous: periodicity.** If the process has a cyclic pattern with the same period (or a multiple) as $k$ — for example, a defect that repeats with every shift change, or a variation that follows the cycle of a 20-position rotary machine — systematic sampling can systematically select the same phase of the cycle every time, producing an artificially homogeneous sample and completely masking the problem. **Before adopting systematic sampling, check whether the process has a known periodicity** (shift change, mold/cavity rotation, maintenance cycles) and, if it does, choose $k$ so it doesn't coincide with that period, or prefer simple random sampling instead.

**Cluster sampling.** Instead of sampling individual units, the population is divided into natural groups (clusters) — boxes, pallets, a shift's lots, a chain's stores — and a subset of entire clusters is drawn, then all (or nearly all) the units within them are inspected. This is the right choice when listing or accessing individual units is expensive, but accessing an entire cluster is cheap — for example, opening a pallet already gives you access to every box on it, but listing every unit on every pallet in the warehouse beforehand isn't practical.

| | Stratified sampling | Cluster sampling |
|---|---|---|
| Divides the population into | **Internally homogeneous** groups, heterogeneous from each other | **Internally heterogeneous** groups (mini-replicas of the population), similar to each other |
| Sampling happens | **Within** each group | **Entire** (or nearly entire) groups |
| Effect on precision | Generally **increases** precision for the same n | Generally **reduces** precision for the same n (units within the same cluster tend to resemble each other) |
| When to use | Known subpopulations with different variance | Cost of accessing individual units is high |

Cluster sampling's loss of precision is measured by the **design effect** ($DEFF$), which compares the variance obtained against what simple random sampling of equal size would produce; $DEFF > 1$ is the typical situation and indicates a larger $n$ is needed to compensate for the internal similarity within clusters.
