## 150.1 The case

**Metalúrgica Aurora, S.A.** — 340 employees, a Tier 1 supplier of suspension components to two
automakers. IATF 16949 certified. Four situations converge in the same quarter:

1. **Complaints rising.** Field complaints went from 42 to 118 PPM in six months. Sales leadership
   attributes it to a "more demanding" customer; production attributes it to the raw material.
2. **Unstable supplier.** The single-source supplier of 34CrMo4 micro-alloyed steel had two delivery
   delays and an uncommunicated change to the rolling process.
3. **Machine degrading.** Press PR-04 shows increasing vibration and three unplanned stoppages in
   eight weeks.
4. **AI model in production.** A visual inspection model, in production for 14 months, was validated
   using material from the old supplier and has never been revalidated.

**Stated objective (what turns these events into risks):** keep customer PPM < 50, OTIF ≥ 98%, zero
safety recalls, and program contribution margin ≥ 22%.

## 150.3 Evaluation criterion

The exercise is **not** complete when a number exists. It's complete when the engineer can answer,
in writing and defensibly:

1. **Why each technique was chosen**, and why not the alternatives.
2. **What assumptions were made** — in particular about independence, barrier effectiveness, and
   distributions — and which of them are verifiable.
3. **What data supports** each number, and which is declared judgment.
4. **What uncertainty remains**, and what decision would change if the uncertainty resolved
   unfavorably.
5. **How the risk will be monitored**, with what indicator, what threshold, and what reaction plan.
6. **How this integrates into the quality system** — control plan, audit, corrective action,
   management review.

> **A risk report that presents numbers without answering these six questions is not a risk
> analysis. It's a spreadsheet with apparent authority.**

## 150.4 The mistake this case was built to expose

The four situations are **not independent**. The supplier's process change (situation 2) is
plausibly the common cause of:

- the rise in complaints (situation 1) — material with different properties;
- the press degradation (situation 3) — harder material increases the load on the tool;
- the AI model's failure (situation 4) — surface appearance different from the training material.

Treating them as four separate risks, each with its own action plan, is the central mistake — and
it's what happens in most organizations, because each situation arrives through a different
channel, at a different department.

The code cell quantifies the difference between the two readings.

def perda_agregada(riscos, correl=0.0, n_sim=N_SIM, rng=rng):
    """Simula a perda anual. correl = correlacao entre os indicadores de ocorrencia."""
    k = len(riscos)
    if correl > 0:
        R = np.full((k,k), correl); np.fill_diagonal(R, 1.0)
        L = np.linalg.cholesky(R)
        Z = (L @ rng.standard_normal((k, n_sim))).T
        U = stats.norm.cdf(Z)
    else:
        U = rng.random((n_sim, k))
    total = np.zeros(n_sim)
    for j, r in enumerate(riscos):
        ocorre = U[:, j] < r["p"]
        # severidade lognormal com media = perda declarada, cv = 0,7
        sg = np.sqrt(np.log(1+0.7**2)); mu = np.log(r["perda"]) - sg**2/2
        sev = rng.lognormal(mu, sg, n_sim)
        total += ocorre * sev
    return total

def es(L, q=0.95):
    v = np.percentile(L, 100*q)
    return (L[L>v].sum()/len(L) + v*((L<=v).mean()-q)) / (1-q)

### How this case should be evaluated

The case has no "single solution." It has **quality criteria for the analysis**. A piece of work is
considered complete when it answers the six questions in section 150.3. Below, the evaluation grid.

<details><summary><b>Detailed evaluation grid</b></summary>

| Criterion | Insufficient | Sufficient | Good |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Link to objectives** | Lists events | Ties each risk to an objective | Quantifies the impact on the objective |
| **Choice of techniques** | Uses FMEA for everything | Chooses appropriately | Justifies the choice and the rejected alternatives |
| **Dependence** | Assumes independence | Recognizes the common cause | Quantifies the effect on the tail |
| **Barrier effectiveness** | Assumes values | Uses measured values where they exist | Designs the challenge test for the missing ones |
| **Quantification** | Only probability × impact | Aggregate distribution | Tail, CVaR, and sensitivity |
| **Uncertainty** | Presents point estimates | Presents intervals | Identifies what would change the decision |
| **KRIs** | Names indicators | Defines thresholds | Justifies the thresholds by ARL and response time |
| **QMS integration** | Mentions ISO 9001 | Lists connections | Specifies where each element fits in |

</details>

<details><summary><b>The seven mistakes that fail the analysis</b></summary>

1. **Treating the four situations as independent risks.** It's the central mistake the case was
   built to expose. Four separate, expensive, partially redundant action plans, instead of an €8,000
   contractual clause that attacks the common cause.

2. **Reporting only the expected loss.** The mean is nearly insensitive to dependence; the tail
   isn't. An analysis that reports only the mean concludes dependence doesn't matter — and concludes
   wrongly.

3. **Using assumed barrier effectiveness.** With assumed effectiveness, any treatment portfolio
   looks sufficient, and the ROI calculation of any reinforcement comes out negative. It's the most
   common way to produce the wrong conclusion with correct arithmetic.

4. **Concluding the system is robust from the FTA's order-6 cut.** Six simultaneous failures look
   improbable — until you recognize that three of the barriers share a common cause.

5. **Not questioning the initial attribution.** "The customer got more demanding" and "it's the raw
   material" are hypotheses, not facts. Without data, the analysis inherits the bias of whoever
   reported it.

6. **Setting KRI thresholds without justification.** A threshold that fires too often gets ignored; one
   that fires too late is useless. Both are worse than having no KRI, because they create the
   illusion of vigilance.

7. **Ending with numbers instead of decisions.** The product of a risk analysis is a prioritized
   treatment portfolio, with owners, deadlines, KRIs, and a connection to the quality system.

</details>

1. **Verify the common-cause hypothesis with data** (Chapter 137). Is the rolling change really the
   cause? A DiD comparing lots of old and new material, or a time-series analysis with a breakpoint
   at the change date, tests the hypothesis instead of assuming it.

2. **Design the AI model's challenge test** (Chapters 138, 147). How many standard parts are needed
   to estimate sensitivity with enough precision for the decision? Stratify by old/new material.

   compare the effect on the CVaR. The difference is the measure of the risk of underestimating the

   breakdown. Compare the value of predictive vs. preventive vs. corrective maintenance.

6. **Complete BIA** (Chapter 148) for the steel disruption, with a threshold-based impact curve and
   an economic comparison of resilience strategies.

7. **Expected value of perfect information** about the cause of the complaints. What's it worth to
   know for certain whether it's the material? If it exceeds the cost of the study, run the study.
