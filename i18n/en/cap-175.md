## Chapter 175: ANOVA Gage R&R: the Method the AIAG MSA Recommends

<h3 id="the-engineering-question">The engineering question</h3>
<p><em>The Range method from <a href="#/aula/cap-061">Chapter 61</a> says the measurement system is approved. But two inspectors disagree more systematically on large parts than on small ones — a pattern the Range method, by construction, can't see. Is the system really approved?</em></p>
<p>Maybe not. Inspector×part interaction — when the disagreement between inspectors isn't constant, but varies with the part being measured — is invisible to the Average-and-Range method, and it's exactly what the ANOVA method was designed to capture. It's no coincidence that the AIAG's current MSA manual recommends ANOVA as the preferred method — and it's exactly the method the <em>manufacturing-performance-analytics</em> project implements (Notebook, Part 9.2).</p>
<h3 id="why-aiag-recommends-anova-over-range">Why AIAG recommends ANOVA over the Range method</h3>
<p>The Average-and-Range method, covered in <a href="#/aula/cap-061">Chapter 61</a>, was born in an era when doing ANOVA by hand was impractical — it's a method designed to be computed with a ruler and paper. That comes at a statistical cost: the Range method <strong>doesn't model the inspector×part interaction</strong>, because it computes repeatability and reproducibility as separate components, with no cross term.</p>
<p>The AIAG <em>Measurement Systems Analysis</em> (MSA) manual, in its 4th edition, is explicit: the ANOVA method is preferred because (1) it accounts for inspector×part interaction, information the Range method simply discards, and (2) it's more precise in estimating variance components, especially with small samples of parts or inspectors. With any modern computing tool — Python, Excel, Minitab — there's no practical reason to keep using the Range method as the primary method; it remains useful as a quick check or in settings without access to statistical software.</p><h3 id="the-two-factor-anova-model">The two-factor ANOVA model for R&amp;R</h3>
<p>An ANOVA-based Gage R&amp;R study treats each measurement as the result of a model with two crossed factors — Part and Inspector — plus their interaction:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="y_{ijk} = \mu + P_i + I_j + (PI)_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ijk}"></div>
<p>where <span class="math-inline" data-math="P_i"></span> is the part effect, <span class="math-inline" data-math="I_j"></span> the inspector effect, <span class="math-inline" data-math="(PI)_{ij}"></span> the interaction, and <span class="math-inline" data-math="\varepsilon_{ijk}"></span> the repeat error (repeatability). The two-way ANOVA table with interaction breaks the total sum of squares down into those four sources, and the variance components follow from them. In the code of the <em>manufacturing-performance-analytics</em> project (Notebook, Part 9.2), these components appear under the names <code>var_repeatability</code>, <code>var_reproducibility</code>, <code>var_part</code>, and <code>var_grr</code>:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Repeatability (<code>var_repeatability</code>)</strong> — from the residual variation (<span class="math-inline" data-math="\varepsilon"></span>): when the instrument measures the same part with the same inspector, how much does it repeat?</li>
<li><strong>Reproducibility (<code>var_reproducibility</code>)</strong> — from the inspector effect and the inspector×part interaction combined: when different inspectors measure the same part, how much do they disagree?</li>
<li><strong>Part-to-part variation (<code>var_part</code>)</strong> — from the part effect: how much the parts actually differ from each other (the "good" variation, the one the measurement system is supposed to be able to detect).</li>
<li><strong>Total GRR (<code>var_grr</code>)</strong> — the sum of repeatability and reproducibility: <code>var_grr = var_repeatability + var_reproducibility</code>.</li>
</ul>
<p>The final indicator, %GRR, compares the measurement standard deviation with the study's total variation:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\%GRR = 100 \times \frac{\sqrt{\text{var\_grr}}}{\sqrt{\text{var\_grr} + \text{var\_part}}}"></div>
<p><strong>Acceptance criterion (AIAG):</strong> %GRR &lt; 10% — acceptable system; 10–30% — conditionally acceptable, depending on the application and the cost of improving it; &gt; 30% — unacceptable.</p><h3 id="worked-example-two-factor-anova">Worked example: a two-factor ANOVA with interaction, in the same format as the practical project</h3><div class="codetabs"><div class="ct-bar" role="tablist"><button type="button" class="ct-tab on" role="tab" aria-selected="true" id="ctbe4f76f5-t0">Python</button><button type="button" class="ct-tab" role="tab" aria-selected="false" id="ctbe4f76f5-t1">R</button></div><div class="ct-pane" role="tabpanel" aria-labelledby="ctbe4f76f5-t0"><div class="codeblock" data-lang="python"><div class="codebar"><span class="lang">Python</span><button class="btn-copy" type="button">Copy</button></div><pre><code class="language-python">import numpy as np

pecas = [f"P{p:02d}" for p in range(1, 11)]
inspetores = ["Insp1", "Insp2", "Insp3"]

    for insp in inspetores:
        # interaction: Insp3 has a bias that GROWS with part size (not constant!)
        vies_interacao = 0.03 * (valor_real_peca[i] - 50.0) if insp == "Insp3" else 0.0
        for rep in range(2):
            medido = valor_real_peca[i] + vies_interacao + rng.normal(0, 0.15)
            registos.append({"PartId": peca, "Inspector": insp, "Rep": rep, "MeasuredValue": medido})

df = pd.DataFrame(registos)

modelo = smf.ols("MeasuredValue ~ C(PartId) + C(Inspector) + C(PartId):C(Inspector)", data=df).fit()
anova_tab = sm.stats.anova_lm(modelo, typ=2)
print("Two-way ANOVA table with interaction:")
print(anova_tab.round(4), "\n")

n_inspetores = df["Inspector"].nunique()
n_pecas = df["PartId"].nunique()
n_reps = df.groupby(["PartId", "Inspector"]).size().iloc[0]

ms_interacao = anova_tab.loc["C(PartId):C(Inspector)", "sum_sq"] / anova_tab.loc["C(PartId):C(Inspector)", "df"]
ms_inspector = anova_tab.loc["C(Inspector)", "sum_sq"] / anova_tab.loc["C(Inspector)", "df"]
ms_part = anova_tab.loc["C(PartId)", "sum_sq"] / anova_tab.loc["C(PartId)", "df"]

var_interaction = max(0.0, (ms_interacao - ms_erro) / n_reps)
var_inspector = max(0.0, (ms_inspector - ms_interacao) / (n_pecas * n_reps))
var_reproducibility = var_inspector + var_interaction
var_part = max(0.0, (ms_part - ms_interacao) / (n_inspetores * n_reps))

var_grr = var_repeatability + var_reproducibility
var_total = var_grr + var_part

print(f"Repeatability (var_repeatability)      : {var_repeatability:.5f}")
print(f"Reproducibility (var_reproducibility)  : {var_reproducibility:.5f}  "

print(f"Part-to-part (var_part)                : {var_part:.5f}")
print(f"Total GRR (var_grr)                    : {var_grr:.5f}")

print(f"\n%GRR = 100 x sqrt(var_grr) / sqrt(var_total) = {pct_grr:.1f}%")

<p>A measurement system with a high %GRR doesn't just produce noisy readings — it <strong>directly contaminates</strong> the process-capability reading from <a href="#/aula/cap-071">Chapter 71</a>. The total observed variance in a capability study is, by construction, the sum of the true process variance and the measurement-system variance:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\sigma_{Observed}^2 = \sigma_{Process}^2 + \sigma_{Measurement}^2"></div>
<p>If <span class="math-inline" data-math="\sigma_{Measurement}"></span> is a meaningful fraction of <span class="math-inline" data-math="\sigma_{Observed}"></span>, the Cpk calculated from the data <strong>understates</strong> the process's real Cpk — because the Cpk formula's denominator uses the observed standard deviation, inflated by measurement noise. In practice, that means a genuinely capable process can look incapable just because the measurement system is poor — and the wrong corrective action (touching the process, which was fine all along) fixes nothing, because the problem was in the gauge, not in the part.</p>
<p>That's why the correct methodological sequence is always: <strong>validate the measurement system first (this chapter), calculate capability second (Chapter 71).</strong> Doing it the other way around means building a capability conclusion on a foundation that may already be contaminated.</p><h3 id="proposed-exercise">Proposed exercise</h3>

<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Sticking with the Range method "because it's simpler."</strong> With a statistical library at hand, the simplicity of the calculation stopped being a meaningful practical advantage — and the cost of missing the inspector×part interaction is real.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring a significant interaction p-value.</strong> A significant interaction is, in itself, an engineering finding: it means at least one inspector has a method or training problem specific to certain conditions, not a general bias.</li>
<li><strong>Calculating capability before validating the measurement system.</strong> As explained above, a Cpk computed on an unvalidated measurement system can be systematically understated.</li>
<li><strong>Mistaking a negative estimated variance for a calculation error.</strong> With little data, it's common for a mean-square subtraction to yield a slightly negative interaction-variance estimate — the convention is to truncate at zero (as the code above does), not a bug.</li>
</ol>
