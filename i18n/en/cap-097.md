## Chapter 97: Verification and Validation

### Design Verification

Design verification, per ISO 9000:2026's definition, is "confirmation, through the provision of objective evidence, that specified requirements have been fulfilled." In other words, verification is a series of assessments that make sure the item was built according to the design and according to the specifications. Objective evidence can be the result of an inspection or can come from other forms of determination, like performing alternative calculations or reviewing documents. There are four potential verification methods, each suited to different situations:

- **Demonstration**, one of the simplest methods, in which the product or process is operated and visual confirmation is performed to verify the design requirements were met. Demonstration is appropriate only when quantitative evidence isn't required for verification, and can use models, simulations, mockups, or the final product itself.
- **Inspection**, another simple means of verification, in which physical characteristics, materials, or parts are examined to make sure requirements were met. Inspection requires the least resources and is typically used to verify a physical design characteristic. Worth remembering: quality can't be inspected into a product; inspection only determines whether quality already exists, and the real solution for high rejection volumes isn't improving inspection, but improving the design or the manufacturing process.
- **Analysis**, which uses mathematical models and other analytical techniques, like modeling and simulation, to determine whether the design meets requirements.
- **Testing**, a planned experiment or activity to determine whether requirements are met under controlled conditions. This is the verification method requiring the most resources, and its statistical analysis leans heavily on design-of-experiments and hypothesis-testing tools covered in this manual's quantitative methods unit.

These verification activities should occur throughout a product's or process's entire review process, not just at a single isolated point on the schedule.

### Design Validation

Design validation is, per the same ISO 9000:2026 standard, "confirmation, through the provision of objective evidence, that the requirements for a specific intended use or application have been fulfilled." In other words, validation makes sure the product or system operates as expected in its intended environment and as required by the customer — validation's central question isn't "did we build the item right according to the drawing?" (that's verification's question), but rather "did we build the right item for its intended use?" Objective evidence for validation can be the result of a test or some other form of determination; test methods, sample sizes, and acceptance criteria should be clearly specified during a product's or process's conceptual planning, and the design should be validated at each design review phase.

When designing validation tests, it's important to build in factors like dimensional wear, material fatigue, and assembly-process variation; variation of critical characteristics across the whole tolerance range; contamination; environmental aging and extreme environmental conditions; and extreme customer use, like maximum loads or long-duration cycles.

### Installation, Operational, and Process Qualification (IQ, OQ, PQ)

In heavily regulated industries — pharmaceuticals, medical devices, food, and others where patient or consumer safety directly depends on the manufacturing process's consistency — process and product validation is usually formalized in three sequential, documented stages, each with its own protocol, acceptance criteria, and report:

- **Installation Qualification (IQ)** is performed to establish that every system and piece of equipment was installed correctly, per the manufacturer's specifications — confirming, for example, that the right machine was installed in the right location, with the correct utility connections, calibrations, and settings.
- **Operational Qualification (OQ)** is performed to establish that the equipment's control limits meet their respective requirements — that is, that the equipment functions correctly within the specified operating ranges, testing individual process parameters under worst-case conditions wherever applicable.
- **Process Qualification (or Performance Qualification, PQ)** is performed to establish that the process consistently produces acceptable products under normal operating conditions. Unlike OQ, which tests the equipment in isolation, PQ assesses the complete process under real production conditions, repeated multiple times, to demonstrate reproducibility.

An additional stage, often used as a final step, is **Process Performance Qualification (PPQ)**, which demonstrates that the validated processes produce a product meeting its specifications — this phase covers the entire manufacturing process as an integrated system, not just an isolated sub-step. Together, IQ, OQ, and PQ (with PPQ as the final consolidation) form the backbone of the process validation required by regulatory agencies like the U.S. FDA before a manufacturing process can be cleared for commercial distribution; the logic is always the same — first it's proven the right equipment was installed (IQ), then that it operates correctly within the specified parameters (OQ), and finally that the process as a whole produces consistent, acceptable results over time (PQ).

Test failures, whether discovered during verification, validation, or the IQ/OQ/PQ stages, should be carefully examined to determine the failure modes involved; unanticipated failure modes should be fed back into the design FMEA, and corrective actions and design improvements should be pursued whenever test results fail to meet established quality targets.
