## Chapter 180: Six Sigma Yield Metrics: DPU, DPMO, Sigma Level, FPY/FTY, and RTY

<h3 id="the-engineering-question">The engineering question</h3>
<p><em>Line A has a 2% reject rate at final inspection. Line B, in a process with five inspection stations in series, also has a 2% reject rate at final inspection. Do the two lines have the same quality performance?</em></p>
<p>Almost certainly not — and the reason is that "rejects at final inspection" is the wrong metric for comparing processes with different numbers of defect opportunities and different numbers of stations in series. This chapter covers the set of indicators Six Sigma uses precisely to make processes comparable regardless of their complexity: DPU, DPMO, sigma level, FPY/FTY, and RTY.</p>
<h3 id="dpu-defects-per-unit">DPU: defects per unit</h3>
<div class="math-block" data-math="DPU = \frac{\text{Total number of defects found}}{\text{Number of units inspected}}"></div>
<p>DPU counts <strong>defects</strong>, not defective units — a distinction that matters. A unit can have more than one defect; DPU captures that, a simple "rejected units / produced units" rate doesn't. A unit with three defects and a unit with one defect count equally toward a binary reject rate, but count differently — more informatively — toward DPU.</p><h3 id="dpmo-and-sigma-level">DPMO and sigma level: normalizing to compare different processes</h3>
<p>DPU alone still doesn't let you compare two processes with different numbers of <strong>defect opportunities per unit</strong> (a circuit board with 200 solder joints has 200 opportunities per unit; a simple machined part might have 3). DPMO fixes that:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="DPMO = \frac{\text{Total number of defects}}{\text{Units} \times \text{Opportunities per unit}} \times 1,000,000"></div>
<p>DPMO is the defect rate normalized per million opportunities — comparable between processes with completely different opportunity counts. From DPMO, you convert to <strong>sigma level</strong>, using the area under the normal curve (with the conventional Six Sigma 1.5σ shift, which assumes the process mean drifts over time relative to what a short-term snapshot measures):</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\text{Sigma Level} = \Phi^{-1}\left(1 - \frac{DPMO}{1,000,000}\right) + 1.5"></div>
<p>where <span class="math-inline" data-math="\Phi^{-1}"></span> is the inverse of the standard normal cumulative distribution function. A "6 sigma" process has DPMO ≈ 3.4 — the methodology's reference benchmark, corresponding to fewer than 3.4 defects per million opportunities once the 1.5σ shift is accounted for.</p><h3 id="fpy-fty">FPY / FTY: first-pass yield</h3>
<div class="math-block" data-math="FPY = \frac{\text{Units good on the first pass, no rework}}{\text{Units entering the process}}"></div>
<p><em>First Pass Yield</em> (also called <em>First Time Yield</em>, FTY) measures the fraction of units that go through a station or process <strong>with no rework or correction whatsoever</strong>. It's a stricter metric than the final approval rate, because a unit that needed rework and then passed inspection counts as "approved" in a simple approval rate, but doesn't count as "good on the first pass" in FPY — and the cost of rework, even when invisible in the final approval rate, is real (see Cost of Quality, <a href="#/aula/cap-007">Chapter 7</a>).</p><h3 id="rty-rolled-yield">RTY: rolled yield, and why it's theoretical</h3>
<p>When a process has several stations in series, <strong>Rolled Throughput Yield</strong> (RTY) is the product of each station's FPY:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="RTY = FPY_1 \times FPY_2 \times \cdots \times FPY_n"></div>
<p>RTY answers the question the final reject rate, on its own, doesn't: what's the probability that a unit makes it through the <strong>entire</strong> process with no rework, at any station? That's why two processes with the same final reject rate can perform radically differently — a process with five stations in series, each at 98% FPY, has RTY = 0.98⁵ ≈ 90.4%, even though the final defect looks small.</p>
<p><strong>The important caveat:</strong> RTY calculated as a product of FPYs is <strong>theoretical</strong> — it assumes each station is statistically independent of the others, and that rework caught at one station can't be traced back to where the defect actually originated. In real manufacturing, that's rarely true: a defect introduced at station 1 might only get caught at station 4, and rolled-up RTY doesn't distinguish "the defect was born here" from "the defect was merely caught here." When full traceability exists (product → lot → station — see <a href="#/aula/cap-099">Chapter 99</a>), it's possible to calculate a <strong>traceable</strong> RTY, physically tied to the defect's real origin — more work to build, but more correct for improvement-investment decisions, because it points to the station that actually causes the problem, not just the one that catches it.</p><h3 id="worked-example-from-dpu-to-rty">Worked example: from DPU to RTY, on the same production line</h3><div class="codetabs"><div class="ct-bar" role="tablist"><button type="button" class="ct-tab on" role="tab" aria-selected="true" id="ct4b64aa9e-t0">Python</button><button type="button" class="ct-tab" role="tab" aria-selected="false" id="ct4b64aa9e-t1">R</button></div><div class="ct-pane" role="tabpanel" aria-labelledby="ct4b64aa9e-t0"><div class="codeblock" data-lang="python"><div class="codebar"><span class="lang">Python</span><button class="btn-copy" type="button">Copy</button></div><pre><code class="language-python">import numpy as np

<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Using the final reject rate as a proxy for whole-process quality.</strong> As the example shows, it hides intermediate rework in processes with multiple stations.</li>
<li><strong>Confusing DPU with the defective-unit rate.</strong> A unit with multiple defects correctly inflates DPU, but counts as "one" defective unit in a binary rate — the two metrics answer different questions.</li>
<li><strong>Forgetting the 1.5σ shift when converting DPMO to sigma level — or applying it without knowing why it's there.</strong> It's a convention from the classic Six Sigma methodology (Motorola), not a universal mathematical property; some contexts report a "short-term" sigma level without the shift — always state which one you're using.</li>
<li><strong>Treating theoretical RTY as real RTY without checking the independence assumption between stations.</strong> If a defect at one station raises the probability of a defect at the next one (dependence), the theoretical RTY calculated as a simple product understates or overstates the real yield, depending on the direction of the dependence.</li>
</ol>
