## Chapter 152: Compliance Risk, Personal Data, and AI Regulation
### The engineering question

> *"Compliance risk belongs to legal, not to me. Or does it?"*

Both. The legal obligation belongs to the organization; the **ability to meet it** almost always
depends on processes, data, and controls that fall under quality engineering's responsibility. A
legal traceability requirement is fulfilled by the traceability system, not by a legal opinion.

This chapter treats compliance risk with the same quantitative discipline as the previous ones: how
to identify it, how to quantify something whose consequence is a fine or a sales ban, and how to
design controls that hold up under scrutiny.

---

## 152.1 The reference normative framework

| Reference | Subject | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| **ISO 37301:2021** | Compliance management systems | Certifiable; high-level structure |
| **ISO 37001:2025** | Anti-bribery management systems | Certifiable; new 2025 edition |
| **GDPR (EU 2016/679)** | Personal data protection | Directly applicable EU regulation |
| **LGPD (Law 13.709/2018, Brazil)** | Personal data protection | Federal law |
| **Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 — AI Act** | AI systems | In force since 08/01/2024; phased application (see 152.5) |
| **Sector legislation** | Product, environment, safety | Varies by jurisdiction and product |

> **The distinction that structures this chapter:** *compliance* is the obligation; the
> **compliance management system** is the mechanism by which the organization makes sure it meets
> it in a demonstrable way. ISO 37301 doesn't create obligations — it organizes the capability to
> meet them.

---

## 152.2 Identifying obligations: the compliance obligations register

The starting point isn't a list of risks. It's a list of **obligations**, and most organizations
don't have it in a usable form.

Every obligation should have, at minimum:

| Field | Why |
|---|---|
| Source (law, regulation, contract, voluntary code) | Traceability and verification of currency |
| Jurisdiction and scope | An EU obligation doesn't apply to production outside the EU |
| Concrete requirement | What it demands operationally, not a legal citation |
| Affected process | Where it's fulfilled |
| Control that ensures compliance | How it's fulfilled |
| Evidence of compliance | How it's demonstrated to a regulator |
| Owner | Who's accountable |
| Consequence of non-compliance | Fine, sales ban, personal liability |
| Review date | Legislation changes; the register goes stale silently |

**The most common structural mistake:** keeping the register in legal language. A requirement
written as *"ensure compliance with Article 5(1)(e)"* isn't actionable by anyone in the process. It
has to be translated into *"X data is deleted N months after Y; the control is Z; the evidence is
log W"*.

---

## 152.3 Quantifying compliance risk

The consequence of non-compliance has very different components, and aggregating them into one is
the mistake that makes the quantification useless:

| Component | Nature | How to estimate |
|---|---|---|
| **Fine** | Defined by law, frequently as % of turnover | Legal framework + history of decisions |
| **Remediation cost** | Process correction, notifications, external audit | Project budget |
| **Disruption** | Sales ban, withdrawal, certification suspension | BIA (Chapter 148) |
| **Reputation** | Customer loss, cost of capital | Difficult; scenarios |
| **Personal liability** | Executives | Not monetizable — changes the appetite |

**The characteristic that sets compliance risk apart:** the **regulatory component has an upper
bound defined by law** (for example, a maximum percentage of annual turnover), which allows the
maximum exposure to be calculated with a precision unusual for other risks. The code cell uses that
to build the exposure distribution.

**The characteristic that makes it hard:** the probability of detection by the regulator isn't
independent of the organization's behavior. A complaint, a whistleblower, or a media incident change
it discontinuously. It's a risk with **context dependence**, not a stationary Poisson process.

## 152.4 Personal data in quality engineering

The point that surprises engineers: **quality data contains personal data far more often than
assumed.**

| Where | Personal data | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| SPC and inspection records | Operator identification | Used for individual evaluation with no declared legal basis |
| Visual inspection images | Hands, badges, faces in the background | Processing of biometric data with no legal basis |
| Gage R&R studies | Performance by appraiser | Covert performance evaluation |
| Training records | Individual results | Excessive retention |
| Traceability systems | Who performed each operation | Indefinite retention "for safety" |
| Internal complaints and whistleblowing | Whistleblower identity | Retaliation risk; specific legal protection |

**The operational principles engineering has to apply:**

1. **Minimization** — collect only what's needed for the declared purpose. An MSA study needs to
   distinguish appraisers, not identify them: **A, B, C** works just as well as names, and
   eliminates most of the risk.
2. **Purpose limitation** — data collected for MSA can't be used for performance evaluation without
   a legal basis and prior notice.
3. **Storage limitation** — define and **enforce** retention periods. "We keep everything" is a
   nonconformance, not a precaution.
4. **Pseudonymization by default** in quality analytics.
5. **Declared legal basis** for every processing activity.

> **The rule of thumb that solves 80% of the problem:** if the analysis doesn't need to know **who**,
> don't record **who**. Replacing names with codes at the point of collection eliminates the risk at
> zero analytical cost.

## 152.5 The AI Act: timeline and what it means for quality

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 **entered into force on August 1, 2024** and applies in **phases**. Note
the distinction: *entering into force* (the act becomes legally existent) and *becoming applicable*
(the obligations become enforceable) are different things, and confusing the two is common.

**Timeline of the Regulation's adopted text (Art. 113):**

| Date | What becomes applicable |
|---|---|
| February 2, 2025 | Prohibited AI practices (Chapter II); AI literacy obligations |
| August 2, 2025 | General-purpose AI (GPAI) models; governance; sanctions |
| **August 2, 2026** | General rule — most provisions, subject to the specific exceptions in Article 113 |
| **December 2, 2027** | High-risk AI systems classified under Article 6(2) / **Annex III** — Chapter III, Sections 1–3 |
| **August 2, 2028** | High-risk AI systems embedded in regulated products under Article 6(1) / **Annex I** — Chapter III, Sections 1–3 |

> **Current status after the AI Omnibus amendment:** the dates above reflect the amended Regulation applicable on the review date. Always verify the consolidated text in EUR-Lex before making a contractual or compliance decision, especially where later guidance, delegated acts, or harmonised standards affect classification or conformity assessment.

**What matters to a quality engineer:**

- An **internal inspection** model that decides part approval is not, by itself, a high-risk system
  under the regulation — but it **can be** if it's embedded in a product covered by Annex I (for
  example, a safety component of a machine or a medical device). Classification depends on the
  **product and the use**, not the technology.
- Even when the regulation doesn't apply, the controls it requires — risk management, data
  governance, technical documentation, event logging, human oversight, accuracy, and robustness —
  **are exactly the Chapter 149 controls**. An organization that implements ISO/IEC 42001 well is
  substantially prepared, whatever the legislative outcome.
- The **AI literacy** obligation applies to whoever operates the systems — which is a **competence**
  requirement, and therefore falls under the quality management system's competence clause. This
  obligation has already been applicable since February 2025.

## 152.6 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Treating compliance as legal's exclusive concern | Obligations with no operational control | A register with process, control, and owner |
| Keeping the obligations register in legal language | No one in the process can apply it | Translate into operational requirements |
| Not reviewing whether obligations are still current | Compliance with a repealed rule | Review date and responsible party |
| Assuming quality data has no personal data | Processing with no legal basis | Inventory of processing activities |
| Recording identity when it isn't needed | Unnecessary risk | Pseudonymize at collection |
| Indefinite retention "for safety" | Nonconformance | Defined, enforced retention periods |
| Classifying AI by technology instead of use | Wrong classification under the AI Act | Classify by product and purpose |
| Quantifying compliance with a 5×5 matrix | Real exposure invisible | Model fine, remediation, and disruption separately |

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Obligations register.**
*Build the register entry for the "one step back, one step forward" traceability obligation.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Source** | Sector regulation applicable to the destination market (identify article and edition) |
| **Jurisdiction** | Market where the product is placed — can differ from the production country |
| **Legal requirement** | Identify who it was received from and who it was supplied to |
| **Operational requirement** | *"For any shipped lot, identify within ≤ 4 h: raw-material lot(s), production date/time, and destination customer(s)"* |
| **Affected process** | Receiving, production, shipping |
| **Control** | Parametrized query in the system, tested quarterly with a real lot chosen at random |
| **Evidence** | Traceability test record, with elapsed time and completeness |
| **Owner** | Quality Director |
| **Consequence** | Expanded recall; sanction; supply suspension |
| **Review** | Annual, or on legislative change |

**The element that distinguishes a good register from a decorative one:** the **periodic
traceability test**. Having the system isn't the same as having the capability. The only way to know
whether traceability works is to pick a lot at random and time how long it takes to trace it in both
directions — exactly like testing a backup restore (Chapter 148).

**Methodological note:** the operational wording includes a **deadline** (4 h). Without a deadline,
"we can trace it" is always true given enough time — and in a recall situation, time is precisely
what's missing.

</details>

**E2 — Quantifying exposure.**
*How do you quantify the risk of a regulatory nonconformance?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Decompose into four components**, because they have different distributions and treatments:

1. **Fine** — has a **known legal ceiling** (a percentage of turnover). Model it as a fraction of
   that ceiling, with an asymmetric distribution: maximum fines are rare and reserved for aggravated
   cases. The existence of the ceiling is what makes this risk unusually quantifiable.
2. **Remediation** — occurs **even without a fine**, and is frequently the largest contributor to the
   mean. It's the cost of fixing what should have been done beforehand.
3. **Disruption** — sales ban or withdrawal. Rare, but dominates the tail. Estimate with the BIA
   (Chapter 148).
4. **Reputation and personal liability** — not credibly monetizable. Treat by **scenarios** and
   recognize they change the **appetite** (Chapter 143), not the calculation.

**Two particularities that set this risk apart:**

- **The probability of detection isn't stationary.** A complaint, a whistleblower, or a media
  incident change it discontinuously. Modeling it as constant underestimates the exposure — and
  underestimates it most precisely when something has already gone wrong.
- **The probability of non-compliance is controllable.** That's where the compliance management
  system acts, and it's that reduction that gets compared against the system's cost. The cell
  quantifies that return.

**What to report to leadership:** expected exposure, CVaR 95%, maximum legal exposure, and the
comparison of the latter against absorption capacity. If the maximum exposure exceeds capacity, the
appetite has to be zero — and that justifies the investment without further discussion.

</details>

**E3 — Personal data in quality data.**
*Inventory the personal data in the quality systems and propose minimization.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The cell builds the inventory. The result typically surprises: **half or more of the personal-data
processing activities in quality systems are unnecessary** for the analytical purpose.

**The three eliminable cases, and the action:**

- **Operator ID in SPC records** → replace with **station ID**. In process-variation analysis, the
  operator is rarely the unit of analysis; the station and shift are enough. If the goal is to study
  variation between operators, then it is needed — but that's an MSA study, with a declared purpose
  and a defined term.
- **Inspection images with people in the background** → **automatic cropping** of the region of
  interest before storage. Besides eliminating the personal data, it reduces file size and improves
  model performance.
- **Appraiser name in Gage R&R** → code as **A/B/C at collection**. Zero analytical loss.

**The three that remain, and their treatment:**

- **Execution traceability** — required by regulation in several sectors. Set a retention period
  aligned with the legal obligation, not indefinite.
- **Training records** — needed to demonstrate competence. Retention limited to the required period.
- **Internal complaints** — the whistleblower's identity has specific legal protection. Restricted
  access, access logging, and formal anti-retaliation protection.

**The methodological point, and it's the most important one:** minimizing **at collection** is
trivial; minimizing **afterward** is very hard, because the data has already spread across reports,
exports, backups, and downstream systems. The decision not to record costs a configuration line; the
decision to retroactively depersonalize costs a project.

</details>

**E4 — Internal audit and the discovery channel.**
*Why is an internal audit program that "never finds anything" a problem?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Because the same nonconformance costs **several times more** depending on who discovers it — and the
cell quantifies it.

**The cost hierarchy by discovery channel:**

1. **Self-detection with voluntary disclosure** — the cheapest. Practically every sanctioning regime
   provides mitigation for self-detection, disclosure, and cooperation. And there's no external
   reputational damage.
2. **Customer audit** — contractual damage and possible supply suspension.
3. **Regulator inspection** — sanction with no mitigation; broadened subsequent scrutiny.
4. **Whistleblower or media coverage** — the most expensive, and cumulative with the previous ones.

**That's why an internal audit program that never finds anything isn't protecting the
organization.** It's guaranteeing that the next finding comes from outside, through the most
expensive channel.

**The four signs of a decorative audit program:**

- **Zero nonconformances** across several cycles. Statistically implausible in a real organization.
- **Always the same minor observations** — the auditor avoids the difficult topics.
- **Auditors with no independence** from the area being audited.
- **The audit verifies the control exists, not that it's effective** — it's the mistake Chapter 147
  covers: "does the procedure exist?" instead of "under what conditions does it stop working, and
  did those conditions occur?"

**What turns a weak program into a useful one:**

- **Audit by risk**, not by a uniform rotating calendar: spend time where the risk is greatest.
- **Audit barrier effectiveness** (Chapter 147), with challenge tests where applicable.
- **Audit the escalation factors** (Chapter 144) — training, overload, deferred maintenance.
- **Measure the program's own effectiveness**: of the findings that came from outside last year, how
  many had internal audit already visited without finding anything? It's the most uncomfortable
  metric and the most useful one.

</details>
