## Chapter 167: Confounding: Why ANOVA Alone Can Mislead You

<h3 id="the-engineering-question">The engineering question</h3>
<p><em>The one-way ANOVA from <a href="#/aula/cap-050">Chapter 50</a> shows a statistically significant difference in reject rate between three machines. The obvious conclusion: swap out the worst machine. Except the "worst" machine is also the only one processing the plant's hardest product. Is the difference due to the machine, the product, or the two tangled together in a way ANOVA can't pull apart?</em></p>
<p>This is the problem of <strong>confounding</strong> — and it's also one of the most misused terms in applied statistics, because the same word is used for two technically different meanings that the literature rarely distinguishes explicitly. This chapter draws that distinction, and points to where to go deeper on each one.</p>
<h3 id="two-meanings-one-term">Two meanings, one term: the two senses of "confounding"</h3>
<p>The same statistical term covers two related but distinct situations that call for different tools:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Observational confounding</strong> (the sense this chapter focuses on, and the most common one in day-to-day production data): an uncontrolled background variable is correlated with both the "treatment" you want to evaluate and the outcome — and that correlation, if ignored, gets wrongly attributed to the treatment. This is the sense used in epidemiology and causal inference, covered in depth in <a href="#/aula/cap-137">Chapter 137</a> (causality, DAGs, quasi-experimental design).</li>
<li><strong>Experimental-design confounding</strong> (Montgomery's technical sense, <em>Design and Analysis of Experiments</em>, Ch. 7): <strong>deliberately</strong> confounding an interaction effect with the block effect, as a fractional-factorial strategy, to cut the number of experimental runs needed. Here "confounding" is a design choice, made on purpose and under the experimenter's control — covered in <a href="#/aula/cap-076">Chapter 76</a> (blocking) and the fractional factorials that follow.</li>
</ol>
<p>The crucial difference: in sense 2, the confounding is <strong>intentional and known</strong> — the experimenter knows exactly which effect is confounded with which block, because they designed the experiment that way. In sense 1, the confounding is <strong>accidental and often unknown</strong> until someone thinks to look for it — which is what makes it dangerous. The machine-and-hard-product example that opens this chapter is type 1: nobody designed the plant to confound machine with product, it just happened, because production routing isn't random.</p><h3 id="anatomy-of-observational-confounding">Anatomy of observational confounding</h3>
<p>The most common pattern in manufacturing data is <strong>1:1 confounding</strong>: a process variable (machine, shift, operator) is perfectly or near-perfectly correlated with another variable (product mix, raw material, customer) — because how production gets routed isn't random, it's decided by business rules (capacity, specialization, availability).</p>
<p>When that happens, a one-way ANOVA testing only "Machine" is, in practice, testing an indistinguishable blend of "machine effect" and "effect of the product that machine happens to process." Statistically, the degrees of freedom that would be needed to separate the two sources of variation simply don't exist in the data — that isn't a limitation of the test, it's a limitation of the available information. No statistical method, however sophisticated, recovers information the data doesn't contain.</p>
<p><strong>What to do, in increasing order of rigor:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Check before you test.</strong> Build a Machine × Product contingency table. If the correlation is close to 1:1, you already know, before running any ANOVA, that the two effects aren't statistically separable with this data.</li>
<li><strong>Extend the ANOVA to include the covariate.</strong> If there's some natural variation (products that run on more than one machine), a two-way ANOVA, or a regression/GLM controlling for the covariate (see <a href="#/aula/cap-051">Chapter 51</a>), can partially separate the two effects — provided there's enough variation to estimate both.</li>
<li><strong>State the limitation explicitly.</strong> When the confounding is total (perfect 1:1) and there's no way to redesign data collection, the correct conclusion is to report the association as "machine-and-product," not decompose what the data doesn't allow you to decompose — and, if possible, propose a future intervention (temporarily run the hard product on a different machine) that breaks the confounding.</li>

# The TRUE effect of machine C, on its own, is ZERO -- all of the observed
# difference comes from the product, not the machine. Let's see whether a
# naive ANOVA picks up on that.

maquinas = ["M-A", "M-B", "M-C"]
prob_produto_dificil = {"M-A": 0.10, "M-B": 0.10, "M-C": 0.95}   # near-1:1 confounding

    dificil = rng.random(n_por_maquina) &lt; prob_produto_dificil[maq]
    # reject rate: TRUE effect of the hard product = +4 pp; the machine, by itself, has no effect
    base = 0.05
    efeito_produto = np.where(dificil, 0.04, 0.0)
    ruido = rng.normal(0, 0.01, n_por_maquina)
    taxa_rejeitados = np.clip(base + efeito_produto + ruido, 0, 1)
    for d, t in zip(dificil, taxa_rejeitados):
        registos.append({"Maquina": maq, "ProdutoDificil": d, "TaxaRejeitados": t})

df = pd.DataFrame(registos)

print("Machine x Product contingency table (confounding visible before testing):")
print(pd.crosstab(df["Maquina"], df["ProdutoDificil"]), "\n")

grupos = [df.loc[df["Maquina"] == m, "TaxaRejeitados"] for m in maquinas]
f_ing, p_ing = stats.f_oneway(*grupos)
resultado = "SIGNIFICANT: looks like the machine matters" if p_ing &lt; 0.05 else "not significant"
print(f"  F = {f_ing:.2f}  p = {p_ing:.4f}  -&gt; {resultado}")

print("\nSTEP 2 -- two-way ANOVA, controlling for 'ProdutoDificil':")
df["ProdutoDificil"] = df["ProdutoDificil"].astype(int)
modelo = smf.ols("TaxaRejeitados ~ C(Maquina) + ProdutoDificil", data=df).fit()
tabela = sm.stats.anova_lm(modelo, typ=2)
print(tabela.round(4))

print("  shows 'Machine' stops being significant once 'ProdutoDificil' enters the")
print("  model: the real effect is the product's, not the machine's.")

<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Running the one-way ANOVA and stopping there.</strong> Without checking whether the tested factor is correlated with another process variable, the causal conclusion may simply be wrong.</li>
<li><strong>Mixing up the two senses of the term.</strong> Reading about "confounding" in a DOE textbook (Montgomery) and applying that intuition directly to an observational shop-floor problem creates a vocabulary mismatch — the experimental-design problem and the observational-data problem call for different tools.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming "controlling for the covariate" always fixes it.</strong> It only works if there's enough variation in the data (the two variables can't be 100% confounded) — see the exercise above.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring unmeasured confounding variables.</strong> Statistical control is only possible for variables that were actually recorded. A confounding variable that isn't in the dataset can't be controlled for — only discussed as a limitation.</li>
</ol>
