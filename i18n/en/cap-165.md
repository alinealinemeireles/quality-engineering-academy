## Chapter 165: One-Factor Designs — Randomized Blocks and Latin Squares

> **Editorial note.** Chapter written from scratch for the web edition, as a companion to Chapters 73-74. Those chapters cover the full factorial with multiple factors; this one covers the classic designs for **a single factor of interest**, in the presence of one or two nuisance sources of variation that need to be blocked — an explicit topic in the CSSBB BOK (VII.A.4).

### The engineering question

*You want to compare four raw-material suppliers. You know, from experience, that the production shift also affects the result — but the shift isn't what's being tested. How do you isolate the supplier's effect without the shift's variation confounding the conclusion?*

The wrong answer is to ignore the shift and hope "the average sorts itself out" — that simply pushes the shift's variation into the experimental error, making it harder to detect a real supplier effect. The right answer depends on how many nuisance sources of variation exist: one (the shift) calls for a randomized block design; two at the same time (say, shift **and** machine) may, under certain conditions, call for a Latin square.

### Completely randomized design (CRD)

The starting point is the **completely randomized design**, appropriate when the experimental units are homogeneous — that is, when there's no known, relevant nuisance source of variation to control for. Treatments are assigned to experimental units entirely at random, and the analysis is the one-way ANOVA already covered in Chapter 50:

$$y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \varepsilon_{ij}$$

where $\tau_i$ is treatment $i$'s effect and $\varepsilon_{ij}$ is random error. The CRD's practical limitation shows up exactly when the homogeneity assumption fails: if, for example, different raw-material lots react systematically differently to the same treatment, that difference goes entirely into the error term, making the test less sensitive to the treatment's real effect — a classic insufficient statistical power problem (Chapter 49).

### Randomized complete block design (RCBD)

When there's **one** known nuisance source of variation — shift, operator, raw-material lot, day of the week — and that source can be organized into homogeneous groups (blocks), the randomized complete block design (RCBD) reduces the variability that would otherwise fall entirely into the experimental error. Within each block, every treatment is applied and the order is randomized; between blocks, systematic variation is removed statistically, not physically. The model extends the CRD with an additional term:

$$y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \beta_j + \varepsilon_{ij}$$

where $\beta_j$ is block $j$'s effect. An RCBD can be read as an extension of the CRD to heterogeneous experimental units: instead of assuming the heterogeneity doesn't exist, it's explicitly modeled and removed from the error — which typically produces a smaller error-variance estimate and an F-test with more power to detect the treatment's real effect.

### Latin square: blocking two sources of variation at once

When there are **two** simultaneous nuisance sources of variation — for example, shift and machine — and both can be organized with the same number of levels as the treatment factor, the Latin square design blocks both at once, without multiplying the number of runs needed. A Latin square is an $n \times n$ array of $n$ different symbols, arranged so each symbol appears exactly once in each row and exactly once in each column. The square's rows and columns correspond to the two blocking factors; the symbols (A, B, C…) correspond to the treatment factor's levels.

A classic example: testing four lubricating oils (the treatment) using four drivers and four cars (the two blocking factors), so each driver uses each oil exactly once and each car receives each oil exactly once — controlling for both the driver's effect and the car's effect on the outcome at the same time, without needing $4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64$ runs (the full factorial), only 16.

The Latin square is therefore also called a **one-factor design**, because, despite involving three categorical variables (treatment and two blocks), the goal is measuring the effect of only one of them — the treatment — while the other two are just neutralized statistically. The model decomposes the total sum of squares into four parts:

$$SS_{Total} = SS_{Row} + SS_{Column} + SS_{Treatment} + SS_{Error}$$

**The essential condition, and the central limitation.** A Latin square is only valid when no interaction is expected between the row, column, and treatment factors — the design's construction explicitly **prevents** estimating any interaction, because there aren't enough degrees of freedom left for it. When there's reason to suspect a relevant interaction (for example, if the oil's effect depends on the specific car), the Latin square is the wrong choice, and a full factorial (Chapter 73), capable of estimating interactions, should be used instead.

### Worked example: RCBD and Latin square side by side

```py-r
--- python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

# ---------- RCBD: 4 suppliers (treatment), 3 shifts (block) ----------
fornecedores = ["S1", "S2", "S3", "S4"]
turnos = ["Morning", "Afternoon", "Night"]
efeito_turno = {"Morning": 0.0, "Afternoon": -1.2, "Night": 1.8}
efeito_fornecedor = {"S1": 0.0, "S2": 2.5, "S3": -1.0, "S4": 1.0}

linhas = []
for t in turnos:
    for f in fornecedores:
        strength = 100 + efeito_turno[t] + efeito_fornecedor[f] + rng.normal(0, 1.5)
        linhas.append({"shift": t, "supplier": f, "strength": strength})
rcbd = pd.DataFrame(linhas)

modelo_rcbd = smf.ols("strength ~ C(supplier) + C(shift)", data=rcbd).fit()
anova_rcbd = sm.stats.anova_lm(modelo_rcbd, typ=2)
print("STEP 1 -- RCBD: supplier (treatment) blocked by shift")
print(anova_rcbd.round(4))
print(f"\n  supplier p-value = {anova_rcbd.loc['C(supplier)', 'PR(>F)']:.4f}"
      f"  ({'significant effect' if anova_rcbd.loc['C(supplier)','PR(>F)'] < 0.05 else 'no evidence of effect'})")
print(f"  shift (block) p-value = {anova_rcbd.loc['C(shift)', 'PR(>F)']:.4f}"
      f"  (blocking the shift removed this variation from the error)\n")

# ---------- Latin square: 4 oils, 4 drivers, 4 cars ----------
oleos      = ["Oil_A", "Oil_B", "Oil_C", "Oil_D"]
motoristas = ["Driver_1", "Driver_2", "Driver_3", "Driver_4"]
carros     = ["Car_1", "Car_2", "Car_3", "Car_4"]

# standard 4x4 Latin square (each oil appears once per row and per column)
quadrado = [
    ["Oil_A", "Oil_B", "Oil_C", "Oil_D"],
    ["Oil_B", "Oil_C", "Oil_D", "Oil_A"],
    ["Oil_C", "Oil_D", "Oil_A", "Oil_B"],
    ["Oil_D", "Oil_A", "Oil_B", "Oil_C"],
]
efeito_oleo      = {"Oil_A": 0.0, "Oil_B": 1.5, "Oil_C": -2.0, "Oil_D": 0.8}
efeito_motorista = {m: rng.normal(0, 1.0) for m in motoristas}
efeito_carro     = {c: rng.normal(0, 1.0) for c in carros}

linhas = []
for i, m in enumerate(motoristas):
    for j, c in enumerate(carros):
        oleo = quadrado[i][j]
        consumo = 10 + efeito_oleo[oleo] + efeito_motorista[m] + efeito_carro[c] + rng.normal(0, 0.6)
        linhas.append({"driver": m, "car": c, "oil": oleo, "consumption": consumo})
ql = pd.DataFrame(linhas)

modelo_ql = smf.ols("consumption ~ C(oil) + C(driver) + C(car)", data=ql).fit()
anova_ql = sm.stats.anova_lm(modelo_ql, typ=2)
print("STEP 2 -- Latin square: oil (treatment), blocked by driver AND car")
print(anova_ql.round(4))
print(f"\n  oil p-value = {anova_ql.loc['C(oil)', 'PR(>F)']:.4f}"
      f"  ({'significant effect' if anova_ql.loc['C(oil)','PR(>F)'] < 0.05 else 'no evidence of effect'})")
print(f"  Runs used: {len(ql)}  (a full 4x4x4 factorial would need {4**3})")
```

**Engineering reading.** In Step 1, comparing the supplier's p-value with and without the shift block in the model (try removing `C(shift)` from the formula and recalculating) concretely shows the blocking's gain in statistical power: the same real difference between suppliers becomes easier to detect once the shift's variation is removed from the error. In Step 2, the Latin square tested four oils' effect while simultaneously controlling for driver and car using only 16 runs — a quarter of what a full factorial would require — at the cost of not being able to estimate whether the oil's effect depends on the specific car.

### Tools: Excel, Power BI, and Minitab

**Excel 365.** The Analysis ToolPak includes **Data → Data Analysis → ANOVA: Single Factor** (equivalent to the CRD) and **ANOVA: Two-Factor Without Replication**, which corresponds exactly to the RCBD — arrange the data with treatments in columns and blocks in rows (or vice versa), and Excel returns the full ANOVA table, including a separate F-test for treatment and for block. There's no native support for the Latin square; the practical route is to manually build the three sums of squares (row, column, treatment) from the marginal means and get the error by subtracting from $SS_{Total}$, or to use Python, R, or Minitab.

**Power BI (DAX).** Doesn't calculate ANOVA natively; the typical use is publishing the already-calculated results table (means by treatment, confidence intervals) for visual tracking, not for the calculation itself.

**Minitab** (current version — check minitab.com): **Stat → ANOVA → General Linear Model** directly accepts formulas with multiple categorical terms (`Response = Treatment Block` for the RCBD, `Response = Treatment Row Column` for the Latin square), returning the same sum-of-squares breakdown calculated in the Python example above — it's the most direct route when you don't want to build the formula by hand.

### Common mistakes

1. **Using a CRD when an obvious, known nuisance source of variation exists.** Letting that variation fall into the error needlessly reduces the test's power — blocking it would have been free, statistically speaking.
2. **Blocking a factor that could actually interact with the treatment.** Blocking assumes the block's effect is additive; if an interaction is reasonably suspected, a factorial that can estimate the interaction is more appropriate.
3. **Using a Latin square without confirming the two blocks' number of levels equals the treatment's number of levels.** It's a structural requirement of the design, not a suggestion.
4. **Interpreting the absence of an interaction term in the Latin square as proof the interaction doesn't exist.** The design simply doesn't have the degrees of freedom to estimate it — absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence.
5. **Randomizing the run order within a block inconsistently or without documenting it.** The blocking's statistical validity depends on correct randomization within each block, not just on the design's structure on paper.
