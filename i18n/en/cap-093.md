## Chapter 93: Quality Audits

### Types of Audits

Quality audits can be classified according to different criteria: the party conducting them, their scope and extent, and the audit method used. Generally speaking, three parties are involved in an audit: the organization requesting the audit (the client), the party conducting the audit (the auditor), and the organization being audited (the auditee).

When the auditor is an employee of the very organization being audited, the audit is classified as an internal quality audit; to preserve objectivity and minimize bias, internal auditors need to be independent of the activity being audited — that is, no one should audit their own work or an area under their own direct management. When, on the other hand, the auditors are employees of the client or of an independent or third-party organization contracted specifically for that purpose, the audit is classified as an external quality audit. In that case, the auditors are clearly independent of the auditee and are in a position to give the client an unbiased, objective assessment; this is the type of audit required to allow an organization to be listed in a qualified-supplier registry or to meet mandatory quality requirements, though the time and cost involved in an external audit tend to be significantly higher than in an internal audit.

This classification by relationship to the auditee is also frequently described in terms of "parties": a first-party audit is the internal audit an organization performs on itself; a second-party audit is one performed by a customer on a supplier (or by a representative hired by the customer), typically in the context of supplier qualification or monitoring; and a third-party audit is one conducted by an independent certification body, with no direct contractual relationship with either the client or the auditee, for the purpose of issuing a market-recognized conformance certificate — this is the type of audit that underpins ISO 9001 certification, for example.

Another way to classify quality audits is by scope and extent. An audit can be as broad as needed or requested by the client. The most comprehensive type is the quality system audit (or system/management audit), which examines the adequacy and effectiveness of the system as a whole, covering both the documentation and the practical implementation of the quality system. Reasons for initiating a system audit can range from assessing a potential supplier to verifying the organization's own system. Audits of specific system elements, processes, products, or services are also possible — these are limited in scope and typically referred to with a modifier preceding the term "quality audit." Examples include the process audit, which analyzes elements of a process and assesses its completeness, correctness, conditions, and likely effectiveness, verifying the adequacy and effectiveness of process controls established by procedures, work instructions, flowcharts, and training and process specifications; and the product audit, an examination of a specific product or service (hardware, processed material, or software) to assess its conformance to requirements — specifications, performance standards, and customer requirements. The product audit verifies whether the system and processes used to produce the item are capable of producing a conforming product, and is typically performed after the product has already passed final inspection; for that reason, it shouldn't be confused with the term "inspection," which deals with accepting or rejecting a lot or individual item — in a product audit, the already-approved item is examined in terms of form, fit, and function, as an additional, independent check on the system's effectiveness.

There are also specific audits aimed at regulatory compliance — the so-called compliance or regulatory audits —, conducted to verify conformance with legal and regulatory requirements applicable to the sector (like food safety, medical device, or environmental regulations), and registration/certification audits, a subtype of third-party external audit conducted specifically to support issuing or maintaining a formal certificate of conformance to a management-system standard.

The method by which the quality audit is conducted offers yet another way to classify it. Audits can be conducted by location or by function. A location-oriented audit provides an in-depth examination of every quality-related activity within a given physical site. In a function-oriented audit, a specific activity is examined at every location where it's performed — useful, for example, to verify whether the same calibration procedure is applied consistently across several of the same company's plants. It's worth noting these classifications aren't mutually exclusive, and in practice, overlaps between them are entirely possible: an audit can simultaneously be external, second-party, process-oriented, and conducted at a single location.

Consolidated normative references for management-system auditing detail guidelines for conducting these audits and typically list four fundamental purposes for quality audits: meeting certification requirements against a management-system standard; verifying conformance to contractual requirements; obtaining and maintaining confidence in a supplier's capability; and contributing to improving the management system.

### Roles and Responsibilities in Audits

Each of the three parties involved in an audit — the client, the auditor, and the auditee — plays a role that contributes to its success. The client is the party that initiates the audit, selects the auditor, and determines the reference standard to be used. The client, typically the end user of the audit results, determines the type of audit needed (system, process, product, etc.), as well as its timeframe and duration.

The selected auditor, whether an individual or a group, needs to adhere to the role of an independent third party — that is, the auditor must maintain objectivity and avoid bias in conducting the audit. The auditor must comply with any confidentiality requirements imposed by the auditee. An experienced individual is designated as the lead auditor to communicate the audit requirements, manage the audit activities, and report the results; rules, qualifications, and evaluation criteria for auditors are detailed in standards and reference guides specific to the field of management-system auditing.

Finally, the auditee is responsible for accommodating the audit, which involves giving the auditors access to the facilities involved and copies of all relevant documentation. The auditee is also expected to provide the necessary resources and select members of their team to accompany the auditors while the work is carried out.

### Audit Planning and Execution, Including Risk Assessment

Proper planning is a key factor in achieving an efficient audit. Planning must be conducted with the client's expectations in mind, including scope, depth, and timeframe. The lead auditor is responsible for planning and conducting these activities and must be formally authorized to do so.

Planning an audit, like any other activity, has to answer the questions of what, when, how, and who. That is: which quality-system elements will be audited? Against which reference document or standard will the quality system be audited? The answers to these two questions are determined by the client and must be clearly communicated to the auditee. A schedule of audit activities needs to be prepared and communicated to both the client and the auditee. It's the lead auditor's responsibility to inform the client of any delays, report their reasons, and update the audit completion date.

One step that deserves special attention in planning is risk assessment, which guides how audit time and depth are allocated. Instead of spreading effort evenly across the whole scope, mature audit planning prioritizes higher-risk areas — processes historically prone to nonconformances, characteristics critical to safety or product performance, areas with recent changes in personnel, process, or supplier, and areas where prior audits identified relevant findings not yet fully resolved. This risk assessment also informs the composition of the audit team: processes requiring specialized technical knowledge (like critical welding processes, embedded software, or regulated processes) may call for auditors with specific technical competence in that area, not just generic auditing competence.

The audit conduct method must also be addressed in planning. Working documents need to be prepared, including checklists of elements to examine, questions to ask, and activities to monitor. There are various references that provide generic checklists usable as templates; however, it's preferable to design a checklist tailored to the specific audit at hand and its particular objectives. Forms for collecting the auditors' observations and supporting evidence should also be included in the working document. These working documents are typically reviewed by an experienced auditor and approved by the lead auditor before implementation. It's recommended that the auditor explain the planned methods to the auditee — this helps the organization prepare better for the audit and reduces the apprehension normally associated with the process.

The question of who will examine specific elements, processes, or products addresses the qualifications and experience of the individual auditors (assessors) needed. Given the client's expectations, the lead auditor must distribute the various tasks among team members, taking into account the risk assessment performed earlier.

An audit is normally conducted in three stages. The first is a pre-analysis or opening meeting with the auditee, which marks the start of the process: at this meeting, the lead auditor introduces the team members to the auditee's senior management and explains the audit's objectives and the methods that will be used. The auditee is represented by selected members of the organization who facilitate and assist with the process and submit a documented description of the quality system or the element to be examined. Questions about proprietary information are typically addressed and resolved before the audit actually begins.

The second stage involves a suitability audit of the documented procedures against the selected reference standard. Nonconformances observed at this stage must be reported to both the client and the auditee for immediate action; the audit process should pause to allow corrective measures where appropriate. For the third stage, the auditor examines the quality system's implementation in depth. The auditor keeps records of all observed nonconformances and the corresponding supporting data. The audit plan should include provisions allowing further investigation of leads suggesting nonconformances revealed by the collected data. The auditee's management must be informed of and acknowledge every nonconformance observed during the audit. This stage closes with a closing meeting with the auditee's management to present the findings; in some cases, the auditor may be asked to recommend corrective measures to improve the system, though it's always up to the auditee to plan and implement these measures in whatever way best suits the organization.

### Audit Report and Follow-Up, Including Corrective Action Verification

A final report is submitted to the client stating the audit facts and conclusions about the capability of the system, element, process, or product subject to the audit to meet quality objectives. Proper audit planning and execution make it easier to prepare this report and provide the data that support its conclusions. The lead auditor is responsible for the report's accuracy and the validity of its conclusions; the report must be submitted to the client, who in turn is responsible for providing a copy to the auditee.

The final audit report must include, at minimum, the following elements: type of audit conducted; audit objectives; identification of the parties involved (auditor, auditee, and third parties, where applicable); audit team members; critical nonconformances and other observations; audit standards and reference documents used; determination of the appropriate corrective action(s); audit duration; distribution and date of the audit report; audit results and recommendations; and audit-related records.

If the auditee initiates improvement efforts to correct the identified nonconformances, the three parties should agree on a follow-up audit to verify the results. The cycle of planning, auditing, reporting, and improving can repeat whenever the systems and/or requirements change. The results achieved provide a measure of the audit's effectiveness. Improvement efforts should also be directed at identifying and eliminating the root causes of reported nonconformances and at identifying the corrective action(s) to be taken. Root causes represent the primary reason behind a nonconformance or undesirable condition occurring, and these corrective actions can then be validated by running new tests, inspections, or even new audits — closing the loop, in this way, between the audit as a verification activity and continuous improvement as the quality system's ultimate goal.
