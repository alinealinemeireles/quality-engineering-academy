## Chapter 907: Cluster Analysis — Hierarchical and K-Means in Quality

### The engineering question

*I have 40 suppliers, each with a dozen quality indicators. Are there natural groups in there — or
am I just staring at a cloud of points and inventing patterns that don't exist?*

Cluster analysis is the family of statistical techniques that answers this question
systematically: it groups observations so that the ones within each group are similar to each
other and the ones in different groups are distinct, using only the measured variables — with no
response variable or predefined label steering the grouping. That's why it's called
**unsupervised learning**: unlike regression (Chapters 51-55) or classification, there's no
correct "y" to validate the result against. The group exists, or it doesn't, and it's up to the
analyst to decide whether it makes engineering sense — statistics alone never proves a cluster is
"real."

## 2 · The basis of it all: distance and standardization

Any clustering method starts by defining "similar" numerically. The most common choice is the
**Euclidean distance** between two points $\mathbf{x}_i$ and $\mathbf{x}_j$ with $p$ variables:

$$d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{p} (x_{ik} - x_{jk})^2}$$

**The step most people forget — and the one that distorts the result the most — is
standardization.** If one variable is "defect PPM" (0 to 50,000) and another is "Cpk" (0.5 to
2.0), the raw Euclidean distance is dominated almost entirely by the first, and the algorithm
effectively "sees" only that variable, ignoring the rest in practice. The standard fix is to
normalize each variable before computing distances:

$$z_{ik} = \frac{x_{ik} - \bar{x}_k}{s_k}$$

exactly the same standardization used for principal components in Chapter 56 — and that's no
coincidence: PCA and clustering share the same sensitivity to scale, which is why it's common to
chain them (see section 6).

## 3 · Hierarchical clustering and the dendrogram

The agglomerative algorithm starts with each observation as its own cluster and repeatedly merges
the two closest clusters, until only one remains. The result is a tree of merges — the
**dendrogram** — where the height of each merge represents the distance between the merged
clusters. Cutting the tree at a given height produces a partition into $k$ groups; different
cutting heights give different numbers of groups, all computed in a single pass.

The decision that most affects the result is the **linkage method** — how to measure the distance
between two *clusters* (not between two points):

| Linkage method | How it measures distance between clusters | Tendency |
|---|---|---|
| **Single (minimum)** | Smallest distance between any pair of points from the two clusters | Produces elongated/chained clusters; sensitive to outliers that "chain" distinct groups together |
| **Complete (maximum)** | Largest distance between any pair of points from the two clusters | Produces compact clusters of similar size |
| **Average** | Average of all pairwise distances between the two clusters | A compromise between single and complete |
| **Ward** | Minimizes the increase in within-cluster sum of squares when merging | The most common default in quality — produces compact clusters of comparable variance, and connects directly to the same "within-group sum of squares" logic from Chapter 54 |

**Practical rule:** start with Ward linkage and Euclidean distance on standardized variables.
Switch to single/complete only if you have a specific reason (e.g., a suspicion of elongated,
non-spherical clusters).

## 4 · K-means: once you're ready to decide k

The k-means algorithm starts from $k$ initial centers (random or chosen) and iterates two steps
until convergence:

1. **Assignment.** Each point is assigned to the nearest center (centroid).
2. **Update.** Each centroid becomes the average of the points currently assigned to it.

The criterion the algorithm minimizes is the **within-cluster sum of squares** (WCSS):

$$WCSS = \sum_{c=1}^{k} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in C_c} \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c \rVert^2$$

**How to choose $k$.** Two complementary techniques, neither sufficient on its own:

- **Elbow method.** Run k-means for several values of $k$ and plot WCSS against $k$. WCSS always
  drops as $k$ increases (in the limit, $k = n$ gives WCSS = 0), but the drop has an "elbow" — the
  point beyond which increasing $k$ brings less and less benefit. It's subjective by nature; use
  it as a first estimate, not a final answer.
- **Silhouette coefficient.** For each point, it compares the average distance to points in its
  own cluster ($a$) with the average distance to points in the nearest neighboring cluster ($b$):

$$s = \frac{b - a}{\max(a, b)}, \qquad s \in [-1, 1]$$

  Silhouette values close to 1 indicate a point well-fitted to its cluster; close to 0, a point on
  the border between two clusters; negative, a point probably misclassified. The **average**
  silhouette of the whole solution, compared across different values of $k$, is a more objective
  criterion than the elbow for choosing $k$.

## 5 · Worked example: segmenting suppliers by quality profile

```py-r
--- python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, fcluster, dendrogram
from scipy.spatial.distance import pdist
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(56)

# 40 suppliers, 3 indicators: defect PPM, average Cpk, % on-time delivery
n = 40
grupo_real = rng.choice(["A", "B", "C"], n, p=[0.35, 0.40, 0.25])
ppm = np.where(grupo_real == "A", rng.normal(800, 250, n),
      np.where(grupo_real == "B", rng.normal(4500, 900, n), rng.normal(12000, 2200, n)))
cpk = np.where(grupo_real == "A", rng.normal(1.55, 0.18, n),
      np.where(grupo_real == "B", rng.normal(1.15, 0.15, n), rng.normal(0.75, 0.14, n)))
otd = np.where(grupo_real == "A", rng.normal(97.5, 1.8, n),
      np.where(grupo_real == "B", rng.normal(92.0, 3.0, n), rng.normal(81.0, 5.5, n)))
ppm = np.clip(ppm, 50, None)

d = pd.DataFrame({"fornecedor": [f"F{i+1:02d}" for i in range(n)],
                   "ppm": ppm, "cpk": cpk, "otd_pct": otd})

X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(d[["ppm", "cpk", "otd_pct"]])

# --- Hierarchical (Ward) ---
Z = linkage(X, method="ward")
d["cluster_hier"] = fcluster(Z, t=3, criterion="maxclust")

# --- K-means, choosing k via elbow + silhouette ---
wcss, sil = [], []
for k in range(2, 7):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=56).fit(X)
    wcss.append(km.inertia_)
    sil.append(silhouette_score(X, km.labels_))

print("k   WCSS      silhouette")
for k, w, s in zip(range(2, 7), wcss, sil):
    print(f"{k}   {w:8.1f}  {s:.3f}")

k_final = 3  # confirmed by the elbow + the maximum silhouette
km_final = KMeans(n_clusters=k_final, n_init=10, random_state=56).fit(X)
d["cluster_kmeans"] = km_final.labels_

print("\nAverage profile per cluster (k-means):")
print(d.groupby("cluster_kmeans")[["ppm", "cpk", "otd_pct"]].mean().round(1))

print(f"\nAverage silhouette of the final solution (k={k_final}): {silhouette_score(X, km_final.labels_):.3f}")
print(f"Agreement between hierarchical and k-means (cross table):")
print(pd.crosstab(d["cluster_hier"], d["cluster_kmeans"]))
--- r
library(cluster)   # silhouette()

set.seed(56)
n <- 40
grupo_real <- sample(c("A","B","C"), n, replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.35, 0.40, 0.25))
ppm <- ifelse(grupo_real == "A", rnorm(n, 800, 250),
       ifelse(grupo_real == "B", rnorm(n, 4500, 900), rnorm(n, 12000, 2200)))
cpk <- ifelse(grupo_real == "A", rnorm(n, 1.55, 0.18),
       ifelse(grupo_real == "B", rnorm(n, 1.15, 0.15), rnorm(n, 0.75, 0.14)))
otd <- ifelse(grupo_real == "A", rnorm(n, 97.5, 1.8),
       ifelse(grupo_real == "B", rnorm(n, 92.0, 3.0), rnorm(n, 81.0, 5.5)))
ppm <- pmax(ppm, 50)

d <- data.frame(fornecedor = sprintf("F%02d", 1:n), ppm, cpk, otd_pct = otd)
X <- scale(d[, c("ppm", "cpk", "otd_pct")])

# --- Hierarchical (Ward) ---
hc <- hclust(dist(X), method = "ward.D2")
d$cluster_hier <- cutree(hc, k = 3)

# --- K-means, k from 2 to 6: WCSS and silhouette ---
resultados <- data.frame(k = 2:6, wcss = NA, silhueta = NA)
for (k in 2:6) {
  km <- kmeans(X, centers = k, nstart = 10)
  resultados$wcss[resultados$k == k] <- km$tot.withinss
  resultados$silhueta[resultados$k == k] <- mean(silhouette(km$cluster, dist(X))[, 3])
}
print(resultados)

k_final <- 3
km_final <- kmeans(X, centers = k_final, nstart = 10)
d$cluster_kmeans <- km_final$cluster

cat("\nAverage profile per cluster (k-means):\n")
print(aggregate(cbind(ppm, cpk, otd_pct) ~ cluster_kmeans, data = d, FUN = mean))

cat(sprintf("\nAverage silhouette of the final solution (k=%d): %.3f\n",
            k_final, mean(silhouette(km_final$cluster, dist(X))[, 3])))
cat("Agreement between hierarchical and k-means:\n")
print(table(d$cluster_hier, d$cluster_kmeans))
```

**Engineering reading.** The elbow chart shows the WCSS drop leveling off around $k=3$, and the
average silhouette confirms it: it's the highest value between $k=2$ and $k=6$. The scatter plot
visually separates the three supplier profiles — the group with high PPM and low Cpk is
immediately identifiable with no manual cutoff rule. Notice the agreement table between the
hierarchical method and k-means: when two methods, with different clustering logics, converge on
the same partition, that's evidence the groups are a real feature of the data — not an artifact of
the chosen algorithm.

## 6 · The connection to PCA (Chapter 56) and to Six Sigma

Two practical connections are worth making explicit:

**PCA before clustering, when there are many correlated variables.** With dozens of strongly
correlated process variables (the same scenario as Chapter 56), computing distances directly over
all of them gives redundant weight to the dimensions that repeat. Common practice is to reduce the
dimensionality with PCA first (keep the components that explain, say, 90% of the variance) and
only then apply clustering to the component *scores* — this reduces noise and speeds up the
computation without losing the data's real structure.

**Clustering as a diagnostic before segmenting a Six Sigma project.** Before assuming that "the
process" has a single distribution (the premise behind all classic SPC in Chapters 65-70), it's
worth checking whether the data hides distinct sub-populations — for example, two machines with
different process signatures feeding the same control chart. A well-separated cluster in the
historical data of a "single process" is often the sign that the chart should be stratified by
machine, shift, or supplier — the same insufficient-stratification problem covered in Chapter 8,
now discovered in an unsupervised way instead of hypothesized a priori.

### Common mistakes

1. **Not standardizing the variables before computing distances.** The most common and most
   serious mistake — see section 2. A variable on a larger scale silently dominates the entire
   result.
2. **Choosing k from the elbow alone, without checking the silhouette.** The elbow is subjective
   and, in noisy data, often has no clear inflection point.
3. **Interpreting k-means clusters as "the truth" without checking stability.** Run k-means several
   times with different seeds (high `n_init`/`nstart`, as in the example) — if the partition
   changes a lot between runs, the structure is weak.
4. **Using k-means with highly non-spherical clusters or very uneven sizes.** K-means implicitly
   assumes roughly spherical clusters of similar variance; in those cases, hierarchical clustering
   with `single` linkage or density-based methods (outside this chapter's scope) work better.
5. **Treating every cluster found as actionable.** A statistically distinct cluster may have no
   engineering meaning at all. The final validation is always substantive, not statistical: does
   the group found correspond to something worth knowing about the process?
