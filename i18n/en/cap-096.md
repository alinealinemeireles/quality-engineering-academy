## Chapter 96: Technical Drawings and Specifications

### Unilateral and Bilateral Tolerances

There are two basic types of dimensional tolerance. Unilateral tolerance specifies the allowed variation in a dimension from a basic or nominal size in only one direction relative to that basic size. For example, the notation 2.000 +0.000/−0.005 inches describes variation allowed only at the lower limit: the specifications for a part with this tolerance will have 2.000 inches and 1.995 inches as the desired upper and lower limits, respectively. Bilateral tolerance, on the other hand, specifies allowed variations in both directions from the basic size; for example, 2.000 +0.005/−0.005 inches describes a dimension with variation allowed in both directions, resulting in limits of 2.005 and 1.995 inches.

### Interpreting the Specification in Relation to Product and Process Characteristics

Correctly interpreting a technical drawing means being able to translate each annotation — nominal dimension, tolerance, surface finish symbol, geometric symbol — into a product characteristic that can be controlled in the manufacturing process and verified during inspection. This is the link connecting this chapter to the previous one: once a characteristic has been classified as critical, major, or minor, that classification should be visible on the drawing itself (for example, with specific markings for critical characteristics), so both manufacturing and inspection know exactly what level of rigor to apply to each dimension.

### Geometric Dimensioning and Tolerancing (GD&T)

Geometric tolerancing defines tolerances for a part's geometric characteristics or features — not just a dimension's size, but its form, orientation, location, or runout relative to other features. The reference standard for these symbols in the United States is ASME Y14.5.

The standard organizes geometric symbols into five categories, totaling fourteen symbols:

- **Form controls** (don't require a datum, since they assess a single feature's form in isolation): straightness, flatness, circularity (or roundness), and cylindricity.
- **Profile controls**: profile of a line and profile of a surface — can be used with or without datum reference, depending on whether they control just form or also the feature's orientation/location.
- **Orientation controls** (relate a feature to a datum, describing the angular relationship without constraining the feature's location): parallelism, perpendicularity, and angularity.
- **Location controls**: position (also called true position), concentricity, and symmetry — all require reference to at least one datum.
- **Runout controls**: circular runout and total runout.

Beyond these fourteen geometric characteristic symbols, GD&T drawings use important modifiers, including maximum material condition (MMC, indicated by the letter "M" inside a circle) and least material condition (LMC, indicated by the letter "L" inside a circle). MMC represents the condition in which the part contains the greatest amount of material allowed by the tolerance — for example, the smallest hole allowed (which has the least material removed) or the largest shaft allowed (which has the most material). When a part measures its maximum material limit across its entire extent, it must have perfect form; this is the principle of perfect form at MMC.

### The Datum Concept

A datum is a reference element — typically a theoretically exact plane, axis, or point — used to establish the geometric relationship between a controlled feature and the rest of the part. Practically every orientation, location, and runout control requires at least one datum reference to establish how features relate to each other or to the part's coordinate system; only form controls dispense with a datum, since they assess a single isolated feature relative to itself. When a drawing uses more than one datum — common on parts with complex three-dimensional geometry — they're organized in a hierarchical order: the primary datum, the secondary datum, and the tertiary datum, each progressively constraining more degrees of freedom of the part relative to the measurement system. In a typical feature control frame, the sequence of referenced datums (for example, A, B, C) appears in the frame's last compartments, immediately after the controlled geometric characteristic, its tolerance zone, the applicable modifier, and the tolerance's numeric value.

### Positional Tolerancing

Positional tolerancing is a system for specifying a part feature's true position, size, or form, and the amount by which it may vary from the ideal. This system's advantage is that it lets whoever manufactures the part divide the tolerance between position and size in whatever way they judge most convenient, instead of fixing separate, independent limits for each. When positional tolerancing is combined with maximum material condition — the most common designation in practice — the smallest hole allowed determines any hole's inner limit, and the specified positional tolerance defines the diameter of a theoretical cylinder around the true position that the axis of any minimum-size hole must not fall outside of. Any hole that "clears" this theoretical cylinder is acceptable, as long as its diameter is within the specified size limits. This system is especially useful on parts with multiple holes or pins that must mate with a companion part, since it allows additional clearance (bonus tolerance) when the actual feature is closer to least material condition than to maximum material condition — clearance that raises the parts' acceptance rate without compromising the assembly's function.

Mastering how to read these symbols and this reasoning is what lets the quality engineer decide, when facing a part rejected on a dimensional measurement, whether that rejection is actually justified by the engineering specification or stems from a mistaken interpretation of the geometric tolerance shown on the drawing.
