## Chapter 124: Six Sigma in eCommerce

### Benefits of Six Sigma in the eCommerce Sector

**Shipping and Shipping-Related Logistics**

Shipping is the lifeblood of any eCommerce company. Without a system in place to process and fulfill orders, the company has no chance of success.

But it's not just about how the company handles shipping. It's about how much of the shipping burden gets placed on the customer. The customer needs the item within a reasonable time frame, and shouldn't have to pay an exorbitant amount of money to have the item shipped. Excessive shipping costs are the top reason consumers abandon items in their online shopping carts. The price can't be prohibitive for the customer, but at the same time, the company can't lose money on every sale because of shipping problems.

Six Sigma can help online retailers spot inefficiencies in their shipping processes — assuming those processes even exist to begin with. Variability in the process can make it hard for companies to guarantee a delivery date to consumers, which then disappoints prospective customers enough that they look elsewhere. In addition, poor deployment of internal resources can help drive up shipping costs and delays. This is especially true for companies with multiple warehouses or fulfillment centers. Six Sigma can help companies organize the logistics behind the process, clarifying each fulfillment center's responsibilities, so each unit can perform at optimal levels.

**Scope of Products Offered**

Many eCommerce retailers take advantage of their ability to offer a larger number of products to customers. A product list that would otherwise be limited by shelf space can now be displayed in full, giving customers a potentially endless list of products to choose from.

However, that isn't always the best approach to take. Too many items can make it hard for consumers to find what they want; they may also find it hard to choose among potential options. As a result, these prospective customers may end up buying nothing at all, falling victim to the "analysis paralysis" that plagues companies worldwide. Worse, those same consumers may end up seeking out other retailers, with simpler layouts and easier-to-find product pages.

The temptation for every eCommerce retailer is to offer more products, rather than fewer items, but they may not realize there can be diminishing returns to this approach. This is an area where Six Sigma can be highly beneficial. Beyond using purchase and site-activity data to identify the best-performing items on the site, a Six Sigma project can also dig into consumers' unspoken wants. Companies may not realize that an expanded range of products can make their audience feel alienated, believing the company has abandoned its niche and core customer base. A Six Sigma initiative will show a company where its focus should be, both in terms of product categories and the varieties of those products that should be offered.

**Improved Ability to Track Where Purchases Come From**

The digital marketing landscape gives organizations a variety of ways to promote their products. This is vital, because the internet is the best way for a marketer to drive traffic. It's easier for someone to click a link than to pick up the phone or the computer and then search for the company's website.

Because of the many marketing options available — social media, pay-per-click, and organic search engine optimization, to name a few — it can become quite challenging for companies to understand what's working and what isn't. While traffic is always a good thing, it can be fleeting if the company doesn't actually know what got people to its site in the first place. How can they repeat what worked, if they're not even sure what sparked people's curiosity?

Implementing Six Sigma can help any company that wants better control over its marketing. Such an initiative can help the company cut unnecessary spending, identify areas where the company is successfully reaching customers, and eliminate any marketing channels that simply aren't working. This lets the organization fully understand which paths it should follow from a marketing standpoint, and also lets the company calculate its return on marketing investment. Knowing that number will help the business anticipate demand in future marketing campaigns, so the appropriate infrastructure — order fulfillment, online servers, customer support — can be put in place ahead of time.

### Challenges of Using Six Sigma in the eCommerce Sector

**The Role of Third Parties in Shipping**

One of the best things about Six Sigma is that it encourages full control over the internal process. This is a big problem in eCommerce, because much of the order-fulfillment process depends on the outside world. If the postal service doesn't do its part, the customer doesn't get their product, which can potentially motivate that individual to post negative reviews and complain on social media. Even if it's not the company's fault, the consequences of a postal error can have serious effects on the business.

Products that arrive on time but were damaged during shipping can be just as problematic. In that case, the company may be forced to bear the cost of return shipping, as well as the costs of sending a non-defective item. In addition, the company now has one less item to sell. Once again, a problem the company didn't cause can significantly impact normal operations.

While these process defects aren't part of the normal process, they're still defects, and the organization is forced to deal with them. As these cases pile up, they can make it hard to track the normal flow of the process. They can also potentially discourage business owners from implementing Six Sigma initiatives. After all, if they follow the process to the letter and the product still becomes defective through no fault of their own, why bother with the project in the first place?

**Working with External Vendors**

Shipping is just one way outside factors can interfere with a company's normal workflow. The other external players in a process can be just as damaging.

Any force outside an individual's direct control can cause a disruption. For example, an independent retailer selling their products on eBay may have seen a drop in business because of the 2014 data breach. Similarly, a major retailer like Sears, which employs dropshipping, might see a drop in the number of users selling on its site because a competitor, like Amazon, starts offering sellers better margins.

External vendors struggling to fulfill orders not only contribute to inefficiency, they can also create PR nightmares for the company. Many print-based storefronts actually have their printing done by larger companies. This helps them save resources while offering end users a variety of printing options. However, if there's a problem at the print shop, customers will quickly blame the storefront, while the print shop escapes unscathed. These problems would also leave the company unable to accurately measure delivery times and print quality, hampering its ability to gather the data needed to kick off a Six Sigma project.

**Shifting Customer Preferences**

Perhaps the biggest obstacle to success in eCommerce has nothing to do with external vendors or product defects. The main threat, one could argue, is the fickle nature of online customers. Slow-loading websites, layout changes, and an overall shift in tastes can dramatically alter traffic and revenue numbers.

We've already seen this shift happen in eCommerce. More people access the internet via mobile devices than go online through laptops or home computers. As a result, online retailers had to adjust their site layouts to attract online customers. In fact, companies that didn't have a mobile-friendly site were heavily penalized by Google, in the form of unfavorable search engine rankings. In light of this rapid growth of mobile-based eCommerce, every retailer had to adjust. Those who couldn't do so lost significant revenue. Worse still, they probably didn't even realize why their numbers dropped so much.

This ever-changing nature of eCommerce is a major obstacle, one that might not even show up in a Six Sigma project, because it runs deeper than just numbers. Audience tastes can't always be quantified. And even if a company suspects its site might not be keeping up with modern times, the leadership team may not be ready to admit that change is needed. Staying ahead of the curve and keeping an eye on eCommerce trends isn't just a Six Sigma matter. It's a day-to-day matter.

### Six Sigma eCommerce Case Study: eBay

eBay is one of the most popular eCommerce sites in the world. Its mix of conventional online retail business (known on eBay as "Buy it Now") and auction-based bidding ensures the site has something for everyone.

At its core, eBay is essentially a middleman connecting sellers of goods with people who want to buy those products. The site lets users list products and facilitates bids from buyers; eBay also provides pre- and post-sale support, and integrates with PayPal to process payments between users, without disclosing either party's financial information.

The regulating body for eBay transactions is a feedback system, which lets both buyers and sellers see how many transactions each individual has completed, as well as any complaints raised by previous customers. Leaving negative feedback on a purchase is a major black mark, one that can have lasting ramifications, creating skepticism among the customer base and affecting future business. Before a buyer or seller leaves negative feedback, they're encouraged to exhaust every possibility for resolving the issue, before taking that final step. To that end, eBay customer service sometimes needs to get involved.

A Six Sigma project was carried out by Pete Abilla, a former eBay employee, to eliminate inefficiencies and defects that existed in the customer service department. While eBay's customer service team had always used DMAIC principles, additional study was needed to identify some of the causes of customer dissatisfaction.

One of the most notable areas of concern was the online chat department, which handled customer concerns and provided real-time service. This eliminated the need for a long email exchange, which could take days, if not longer, to resolve. Online chat resolved issues nearly instantly, unless there was a bigger problem that required a manager's involvement. However, the online chat model had a few significant flaws, which got in the way of the customer's ability to receive support.

After opening the chat window, the user was asked for some information to verify their identity, before the chat could begin. The user had to provide their user ID and email address, so the customer service rep could locate the user's account and provide the needed support. However, as soon as the customer service rep joined the chat, they would start by asking the user for additional information — the user's name, address, and phone number, along with the same user ID and email address the user had just provided.

Not only was this a significant redundancy, its consequences affected everyone. First, it frustrated the customer, who had already provided that information and didn't see why it was needed a second time. It also increased chat time. This kept the user in the chat longer than necessary, and also reduced the total number of users the customer service chat team was able to help. Beyond that, some users may have developed concerns that they were being phished for their personal information. While the initial message from eBay's chat team clearly stated that they would never ask for information like credit card numbers and passwords, users had reason to be skeptical, given that they'd just been asked to provide information they'd already provided.

By using the Six Sigma methodology, eBay realized it was asking for basic information too often. They also realized the pre-chat survey wasn't doing an adequate job of gathering the information the chat team needed. As a result, the pre-chat survey was expanded, to request all the information needed to start the support chat. This also helped the support team have that information organized in a predictable way, increasing their ability to help eBay users.

In the end, eBay was able to cut verification time from 3:42 to 2:57. That 45-second difference reduced average verification time by 20 percent, letting eBay serve more customers, and giving customers a bit more time to do what they most like doing on eBay — buying and selling items.

### Tips for Using Six Sigma in the eCommerce Sector

Six Sigma can be a valuable tool for identifying the strengths of an eCommerce operation. Six Sigma can also help a company resist the urge to become something it isn't, or to stretch itself too thin chasing revenue.

Keeping a narrow focus on what brings customers to an eCommerce store helps keep that store well-positioned within its chosen niche. Understanding the customers who frequent the store is crucial when it comes to changing with the times and adapting to the needs of the customer base.

Knowing the company's limits is essential. Six Sigma only works if the project scope is manageable. Adding items to the product mix can result in sunk costs and excess inventory, not to mention a bloated dataset that's incompatible with Six Sigma.

Understanding that the internet is an ever-changing medium is a necessary component of any eCommerce business. Embracing Six Sigma while knowing the whole sector can shift at any minute is a bit tricky. However, sticking to the company's pre-established principles will keep the company on the right track, and will let the organization focus on getting the desired results from its Six Sigma project.
