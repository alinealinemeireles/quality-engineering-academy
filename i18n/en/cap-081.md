## Chapter 81: FMEA — What It Is and How to Build It

### Definition, Purpose, and Benefits of FMEA

FMEA — Failure Mode and Effects Analysis — is, in D. H. Stamatis's definition, an engineering "reliability tool" that helps define, identify, prioritize, and eliminate known and/or potential failures of a system, design, or manufacturing process before they reach the customer. It's essential to understand this phrase carefully: FMEA isn't just a list of past problems — it's, above all, a preventive tool, one that tries to anticipate failures that haven't happened yet. Its goal isn't to react to defects, but to prevent them.

FMEA serves a dozen interconnected functions within a quality-mature organization: it provides structure for a cross-functional critique of a design or process; it facilitates cross-departmental dialogue (it's much more than a simple design review); it constitutes a mental discipline good engineering teams adopt when questioning what could go wrong; it generates a living document, reflecting the most current design, product, and process actions; it identifies the effects and severity of potential failure modes; it identifies the causes and likelihood of these failures occurring; it identifies existing controls and their effectiveness; it quantifies and prioritizes the risks tied to each failure mode; and, finally, it documents action plans aimed at reducing that risk. When done correctly, every FMEA — whether design, process, use, or any other variant — should build confidence that reasonable risks were identified early, establish priorities and justification for improvement actions, reduce scrap, rework, and manufacturing costs, preserve the organization's product and process knowledge, reduce field failures and warranty costs, and document risks and actions for use on future projects and processes.

A point Stamatis strongly emphasizes, and one that's easy to overlook in practice: an FMEA done by a single person is just that person's opinion — it doesn't serve FMEA's real purpose or intent. FMEA **needs** to be run as a team, with subject-matter expertise (ideally five to seven members), multi-level consensus, representation from every relevant stakeholder, cross-functional and multidisciplinary in nature, and properly empowered to make decisions. For a DFMEA (design FMEA), the natural team leader is usually the design engineer; for a PFMEA (process FMEA), the natural leader is the manufacturing engineer, with the explicit inclusion of the operator, who is closest to the operation and therefore best qualified to discuss attributable causes. The team also has important complementary roles: the sponsor/champion (who provides resources, support, and removes obstacles), the team leader (the project's technical "expert"), the recorder (responsible for documentation, minutes, and agendas), and the facilitator (the process's "watchdog," who keeps the team focused, ensures everyone participates, and, to be effective, shouldn't have a direct stake in the project's outcome).

### The Eight-Step Process

Stamatis describes a systematic, recommended eight-step approach for running any FMEA — system, design, product, process, equipment, or service:

1. **Select the team and brainstorm.** Make sure the right participants are involved, cross-functionally and multidisciplinarily, willing to share their experience and knowledge.
2. **Build a functional block diagram and/or a process flowchart.** For system and design FMEAs, the functional block diagram applies; for process and service FMEAs, the process flowchart is the appropriate tool. The goal is making sure the whole team understands the system, design, process, or service being analyzed, along with its associated issues.
3. **Prioritize.** Once the team understands the context, the actual analysis begins: where to start, what matters most. Sometimes this step is almost automatic, because the customer has already flagged the priority, or because warranty cost has already pointed to the starting point.
4. **Collect data.** The team starts gathering failure data and properly categorizing it, filling out the actual FMEA form — the failures identified at this point become the FMEA's failure modes.
5. **Analyze.** The data is used to generate a resolution, following the data → information → knowledge → decision flow. The analysis can be qualitative or quantitative, and can draw on brainstorming, cause-and-effect diagrams, QFD, DOE, SPC, another FMEA, mathematical modeling, simulation, or reliability analysis.
6. **Results.** The theme here is being data driven: based on the analysis, the severity, occurrence, and detection values and the Risk Priority Number (RPN) are derived, filling in the form's corresponding columns.
7. **Confirm/assess/measure.** Once the results are logged, it's time to confirm whether the actions succeeded or failed, answering three basic questions: is the situation better, worse, or the same as before?
8. **Start it all over again.** Regardless of the previous step's answer, the team should pursue improvement continuously, because the philosophy underlying FMEA is continuous improvement — every FMEA is, by definition, a living document.

### Essential FMEA Vocabulary

To correctly fill out an FMEA form, you need to master its specific jargon. **Function** describes the design or process intent — what the item is supposed to do, typically written as a measurable verb-noun phrase. **Failure mode** describes how that function can fail; Stamatis suggests at least six minimum failure categories for any function: no function (it doesn't work), degraded (the function fails over time), intermittent (it works sometimes, not others), partial (it doesn't work through the full cycle), unintended (the function acts unexpectedly), and excessive function (the function does more than intended).

The **failure effect** is that failure's consequences, typically assessed relative to the subcomponent, the next assembly level, the system, the overall product, government regulations, and the customer. **Severity (S)** measures how serious that effect is — it's always the worst possible numeric effect for a given failure mode, and it's a relative rating within that specific FMEA's scope; note that a reduction in the severity rating can only be achieved through a design change, never through better detection. The **classification** flags when severity hits 9 or 10 — that is, when safety and/or government regulations are at stake — requiring mandatory proactive design actions.

**Possible cause** is an indication of a design (or process) weakness whose consequence is the failure mode — in other words, what makes the function fail. The boundary diagram and the P-diagram, described below, are valuable sources for identifying causes. **Occurrence (O)** measures the likelihood that a specific cause/mechanism happens during the function's lifetime; like severity, it's a relative rating, and reducing it is only achieved by avoiding or controlling the cause through a design or process change (for example, a design checklist, design review). **Prevention controls** are part of the planning aimed at keeping the cause from happening, or reducing its occurrence rate; **detection controls** identify the effectiveness of the planning controls — analytical or physical — before the item is released to production. **Detection (D)** is the rating tied to the best type of design control available, as listed in the previous column; to reach a lower (better) rating, the planned design control generally needs to be improved.

The **Risk Priority Number (RPN)** is the product S × O × D, and it drives the priority of recommended actions. It's crucial to understand, though — and Stamatis is emphatic on this point — that the RPN isn't always the best indicator of importance, since severity or occurrence on their own can dominate the factor. A better approach for setting priorities on an already-completed FMEA is to first look at high severity numbers (9 or 10, and at some organizations 5 or higher, by customer-supplier agreement), then at the highest criticality indices (S × O), and only then at the highest RPN values. Each failure cause should have its own individually calculated RPN. **Recommendations** are the actions that should be taken to minimize or eliminate the failure's cause — to be effective, they need to be appropriate, applicable, achievable within a reasonable timeframe, and economically viable, always assigned to a specific owner with a set date. **Actions taken** document what was actually implemented from the recommendations, with a new reassessment of severity, occurrence, and detection — the revised values (new severity, new occurrence, new detection, new RPN) only change if, respectively, the design, standards, procedures, policies, or the process itself are actually changed.

### Robustness: Boundary Diagram, Interface Matrix, and P-Diagram

Every FMEA — especially the design FMEA — has an implicit focus on robustness, that is, on the design's ability to keep working correctly even in the face of variation sources it doesn't directly control. Three input tools feed this robustness focus even before the team starts filling out the actual FMEA form.

The **boundary diagram** identifies and represents the other components at the higher assembly level, typically including every system fastener and mechanism, as well as interfaces with other systems, with manufacturing/assembly tooling, and with maintenance/customer adjustments. It's generally built with dashed lines around the FMEA's item of interest, defining what's inside and what's outside the analysis's scope. The boundary diagram serves two essential functions: helping identify possible failure effects and, once the scope is defined, focusing the supporting team on the FMEA process.

The **interface matrix** is used to identify and prioritize the interactions between the items of interest, according to four parameters: physical touch, information exchange, energy transfer, and material exchange. It serves as input to a design FMEA, identifying and quantifying the strength of these interactions — whether the relationship is required or adverse, and what type of relationship it is — typically on a scale running from +2 (required interaction) to −2 (interaction that should be avoided for functionality to be achieved).

The **P-diagram** (parameter diagram) is a method for identifying an item's ideal function, as well as the parameters that can keep that ideal function from happening. It's particularly recommended for the design FMEA because it's a structured tool for identifying a function's intended inputs and outputs, describes noise factors, control factors, the ideal function, and error states, and helps identify potential failure causes, the failure modes themselves, potential failure effects, current controls, and recommended actions. The P-diagram is based on the input → process → output relationship, which represents the ideal function (no waste), and is organized around five elements: **input** (the items used in the process — manpower, machine, method, material, measurement, and environment), the **process** (the value-added activity, the process's reason for existing), the **output** (the process's expected result), **errors** (the items that make the output fall short of 100%, that is, the failures), and **control** (the items that ensure the output is at the optimal point — the actions that mitigate failures so the output is achieved), plus the **noise factors**, which are the factors that contribute to customer-use variation, part-to-part variation, external environment, interactions, and changes over time, without necessarily having a detectable adverse reaction in the process.

Together, these three tools — boundary diagram, interface matrix, and P-diagram — feed into the FMEA proper, which in turn generates, as a typical DFMEA output, a robustness checklist (summarizing error states, noise factors, and the associated design controls) and serves as input to the design verification plan (DVP), a step that connects with the verification and validation topic covered in another unit of this manual.

### The FMEA Form and Rating Scales

The generic FMEA form is surprisingly simple and straightforward, and — worth pointing out — the only real structural difference between a design FMEA form and a process FMEA form is the header, which identifies whether it's a DFMEA or a PFMEA. A typical form includes, among other columns: system/subsystem/component/process; function; failure mode; effect; severity; classification; cause; occurrence; prevention controls; detection controls; detection; RPN; recommendations; actions taken; and, after the actions, the revised severity, occurrence, detection, and RPN values.

An important point — and one often misunderstood — is that **there's no single, universal, mandatory standard** for the severity, occurrence, and detection rating scales used across every FMEA and every industry. Ratings should be based on logic, knowledge, and experience about the task at hand. That said, certain industries — aerospace, nuclear, automotive, among others — do recommend standardized criteria lists for severity, occurrence, and detection; if the organization chooses to deviate from these guidelines, it's acceptable to do so, as long as an addendum documents and explains these deviations, so any outside reader of the FMEA understands the departures from the suggested guide.

A widely used example from the automotive sector, derived from the SAE J1739 technical standard, nicely illustrates the logic of these 1-to-10 scales. For severity, the low end (rating 1) corresponds to "no discernible effect to the customer," rising progressively to rating 10, reserved for effects that endanger safe operation and involve noncompliance with regulation, occurring without warning (rating 9 is reserved for the same type of effect, but with warning). For occurrence, the scale runs from "remote" (rating 1, less than 1 failure in 1,500,000, typically associated with a Cpk capability index above 1.67) to "very high" (ratings 9 and 10, failure is almost inevitable, 1 in 3 or worse). For detection, the logic runs opposite to intuition: rating 1 means the control will almost certainly detect the cause before it produces the failure, while rating 10 means no known control is capable of detecting that cause. It's worth noting these same ratings apply, with small wording variations (for example, swapping "customer" for "production line" and including Cpk references), to both DFMEA and PFMEA — the next chapter details these specific nuances.

### RPN, Prioritization, and Action Priority (AP)

Once calculated, the RPN — the product S × O × D, ranging from 1 to 1,000 — guides, but doesn't alone determine, corrective-action prioritization. A common mistake is ranking actions strictly by descending RPN and working top to bottom. The problem is that, by multiplying the three components together, each one's relative importance gets lost: two failure modes can have exactly the same RPN of 100 (for example, S=2, O=10, D=5, or S=10, O=2, D=5) representing completely different risk profiles — one with very low severity but near-certain occurrence, the other with very high severity but rare occurrence. The generally accepted strategy for breaking these ties, per Palady (1997), is following a priority order: first eliminate the occurrence, then reduce the severity, then reduce any remaining occurrence, and only last improve detection — reflecting the philosophy that acting on the root cause (eliminating or reducing why the failure happens) is always preferable to just improving the ability to catch it after it's already happened.

A rule of thumb Stamatis mentions is the "95% rule": addressing every failure mode at a 95% confidence level mathematically corresponds to a cutoff RPN around 50 — but that number is only relative to the FMEA as a whole, and can shift as risk rises across every category and cause. Other prioritization approaches, considered more sensitive and proactive than looking only at RPN, include simply looking at any severity of 5 or higher (regardless of occurrence or detection), or using an "area chart" that crosses severity and occurrence on a Cartesian plane, splitting the region into low-, medium-, and high-priority zones — a more proactive approach because it uses only severity and occurrence, ignoring detection (which is, by nature, a reactive measure).

It's important for readers preparing for CQE certification to know that automotive FMEA methodology follows the harmonized AIAG & VDA handbook (2019), the result of a joint effort between AIAG (the U.S. Automotive Industry Action Group) and VDA (the German automotive industry association). One of its central points is using, instead of pure RPN (with an arbitrary numeric threshold, for example 100), an **Action Priority (AP) table**, which directly maps the 1,000 possible combinations of severity, occurrence, and detection (each on a 1-to-10 scale) to one of three qualitative levels: high, medium, or low priority. The AP table's logic ranks the three factors hierarchically instead of treating them as equivalent multiplicative weights — severity dominates, then occurrence, then detection — so that a very high-severity failure mode (for example, 9 or 10) will automatically get a high action priority even if its occurrence or detection are relatively favorable, avoiding the problematic situation, possible under the traditional RPN, of a very high-severity risk "slipping through" prioritization just because its numeric product falls below the established threshold. Certification candidates should be aware that both approaches — classic RPN and the AP table — remain in active use across different sectors and organizations, and that the conceptual logic of severity, occurrence, and detection as the three pillars of FMEA risk assessment stays the same in both methods; what changes is only how these three values get combined into a final priority.
