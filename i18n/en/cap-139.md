## Chapter 139: Reliability and Advanced Survival Analysis
### The engineering question

> *"I tested 30 components for 1,000 hours. Some failed; the rest are still working at the end of
> the test. How do I estimate the component's life without throwing away the information from the
> ones that didn't fail?"*

This is **the** reliability problem, and the reason classic quality statistics falls short: most
units **did not fail** by the end of the study. Ignoring them biases everything downward; treating
them as if they failed at the end biases everything too — in the wrong direction. The answer is
**censoring**, and that's what sets survival analysis apart.

---

## 139.1 Censoring: the three types, and why it matters

| Type | Situation | Available information |
|---|---|---|
| **Right (Type I)** | The test ended at a fixed t; the unit was still working | T > t |
| **Right (Type II)** | The test ended after r failures | T > t_(r) for the survivors |
| **Left** | The failure had already happened before the first observation | T < t |
| **Interval** | Periodic inspection: working at t₁, failed by t₂ | t₁ < T < t₂ |

Right censoring is the overwhelming majority of industrial cases. **Interval** censoring is more
common than people admit: when the failure is only detected at the next inspection, the exact time
is unknown, and treating it as the inspection instant introduces systematic bias.

> **The critical assumption: non-informative censoring.** The reason a unit was censored has to be
> **independent** of its failure time. If units were pulled from the test *because they looked about
> to fail*, censoring is informative and every classical estimator ends up biased. No method
> corrects this after the fact.

---

## 139.2 Kaplan-Meier: the nonparametric estimate

$$ \hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left( 1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i} \right) $$

where d_i is the number of failures at t_i and n_i is the number at risk immediately before t_i.

The estimator's elegance lies in how it handles censoring: a censored unit **contributes to the
denominator** (it was at risk) until the moment it exits, and then stops counting. The information
"survived at least until here" is fully used.

**Advantages:** it assumes no distributional form at all; it's an honest portrait of the data.
**Limitations:** it doesn't extrapolate beyond the last observed time — and that's often exactly
where the engineering question points ("what's the reliability at 10 years?", with a 1-year test).

---

## 139.3 Weibull: the parametric reliability model

$$ R(t) = \exp\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{\beta}\right], \qquad h(t) = \frac{\beta}{\eta}\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{\beta-1} $$

- **β (shape)** — the most important engineering information the fit gives you;
- **η (scale)** — the characteristic life; R(η) = e⁻¹ ≈ 36.8% **always**, whatever β is.

| β | Failure rate | Bathtub-curve phase | Engineering action |
|---|---|---|---|
| β < 1 | **decreasing** | infant mortality | Burn-in, supplier process control. **Preventive replacement is counterproductive** |
| β = 1 | **constant** (exponential) | useful life | Preventive replacement brings no benefit. Condition-based maintenance |
| β > 1 | **increasing** | wear-out | Preventive replacement **is effective**; compute the optimal interval |

> **The rule that sums up the chapter:** *preventive replacement only makes sense if β > 1.*
> Swapping a part in the infant-mortality regime for a new one raises the instantaneous failure rate.

**Estimation with censoring.** The likelihood combines the density for failures and the survival
function for the censored:

$$ L = \prod_{i \in \text{failures}} f(t_i) \prod_{j \in \text{censored}} S(t_j) $$

Maximizing this expression is the correct method. The Weibull probability-plot (median ranks) is
useful as a visual diagnostic, but ML estimation is what produces valid confidence intervals.

---

## 139.4 Accelerated testing and the life-stress model

You can't wait 10 years to characterize a 10-year component. The way out is to test under elevated
stress (temperature, electrical stress, cycles) and extrapolate using a physical model:

- **Arrhenius** (temperature): ln(life) = A + E_a/(k·T)
- **Inverse power law** (mechanical/electrical stress): life ∝ V⁻ⁿ
- **Coffin-Manson** (thermal fatigue)

**The risk, which must be stated:** the extrapolation is only valid if the **failure mechanism is
the same** under accelerated and use conditions. If the elevated temperature activates a mechanism
that doesn't exist under normal use, the extrapolation is fiction dressed up as rigor. The mandatory
check is **physical failure analysis** on the accelerated samples, compared against field failures.

---

## 139.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Discarding the units that didn't fail | Severely underestimated life | Likelihood with censoring |
| Treating censoring as a failure at the end of the test | Life **also** underestimated (less, but the same direction) | Same |
| Assuming exponential (β=1) for convenience | Wrong maintenance policy | Estimate β and test whether it differs from 1 |
| Confusing MTBF with guaranteed life | Wrong stock and maintenance sizing | R(MTBF) = 36.8% |
| Preventive replacement with β < 1 | Raises the failure rate | Check β before setting the policy |
| Extrapolating an accelerated test without checking the mechanism | A prediction with no physical grounding | Comparative failure analysis |
| Ignoring informative censoring | Uncorrectable bias | Document why each unit left the study |

# ============================================================================
# Capitulo 139 - Exemplo resolvido
# (1) O custo de ignorar a censura
# (2) Kaplan-Meier
# (3) Weibull por maxima verosimilhanca COM censura + IC bootstrap
# (4) Politica de substituicao preventiva: so faz sentido se beta > 1
# ============================================================================
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, optimize

rng = np.random.default_rng(139)

# ---- Ensaio: 30 unidades, terminado em 1000 h (censura Tipo I a direita) ----
BETA_REAL, ETA_REAL, T_FIM, N_UNID = 2.1, 1600.0, 1000.0, 30
t_real = ETA_REAL * rng.weibull(BETA_REAL, N_UNID)
tempo = np.minimum(t_real, T_FIM)
falhou = (t_real <= T_FIM).astype(int)

print("=" * 74)
print(f"ENSAIO: {N_UNID} unidades | fim a {T_FIM:.0f} h | "
      f"{falhou.sum()} falhas, {N_UNID-falhou.sum()} censuradas")
print("=" * 74)
print(f"  (verdade oculta ao analista: beta = {BETA_REAL}, eta = {ETA_REAL:.0f} h)")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (1)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(1) O CUSTO DE IGNORAR A CENSURA")
print("=" * 74)

def weibull_mv(t, d, chute=(1.5, 1000.0)):
    """MV de Weibull com censura a direita. d=1 falha, d=0 censurada."""
    def neg_ll(par):
        b, e = par
        if b <= 0.01 or e <= 1e-6:
            return 1e12
        z = (t / e) ** b
        # log f  para falhas ; log S  para censuradas
        ll = np.sum(d * (np.log(b) - np.log(e) + (b - 1) * np.log(t / e))) - np.sum(z)
        return -ll
    r = optimize.minimize(neg_ll, chute, method="Nelder-Mead",
                          options={"xatol": 1e-8, "fatol": 1e-10, "maxiter": 5000})
    return r.x

# (a) so as falhas (descarta as censuradas) - ERRADO
so_falhas = tempo[falhou == 1]
b_a, e_a = weibull_mv(so_falhas, np.ones(len(so_falhas)))
# (b) trata censuradas como falhas em T_FIM - ERRADO
b_b, e_b = weibull_mv(tempo, np.ones(len(tempo)))
# (c) verosimilhanca com censura - CORRETO
b_c, e_c = weibull_mv(tempo, falhou)

print(f"  {'Metodo':<42}{'beta':>8}{'eta (h)':>11}{'B10 (h)':>11}")
print("  " + "-" * 72)
def b10(b, e):
    return e * (-np.log(0.90)) ** (1 / b)
for nome, (b, e) in [
    ("(a) so as falhas  [ERRADO]", (b_a, e_a)),
    ("(b) censuradas contadas como falhas [ERRADO]", (b_b, e_b)),
    ("(c) verosimilhanca com censura  [CORRETO]", (b_c, e_c)),
    ("    VERDADE", (BETA_REAL, ETA_REAL)),
]:
    print(f"  {nome:<42}{b:>8.3f}{e:>11.1f}{b10(b,e):>11.1f}")
print("  " + "-" * 72)
print("\n  (a) subestima gravemente a vida: so ve as unidades que falharam cedo.")
print("  (b) tambem enviesa: assume que as sobreviventes falharam exatamente no fim.")
print("  (c) usa a informacao 'sobreviveu pelo menos ate 1000 h', que e real.")
print("\n  B10 = tempo ate 10% de falhas. E a metrica contratual mais usada")
print("  em componentes - e a diferenca entre (a) e (c) e economicamente enorme.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (2)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(2) KAPLAN-MEIER  -  estimativa nao parametrica de S(t)")
print("=" * 74)

def kaplan_meier(t, d):
    ordem = np.argsort(t); t, d = t[ordem], d[ordem]
    n = len(t); S = 1.0; var_ac = 0.0
    out = []
    for i in range(n):
        if d[i] == 1:
            em_risco = n - i
            S *= (1 - 1 / em_risco)
            var_ac += 1 / (em_risco * (em_risco - 1)) if em_risco > 1 else 0
            ep = S * np.sqrt(var_ac)
            out.append((t[i], em_risco, S, max(0.0, S - 1.96*ep), min(1.0, S + 1.96*ep)))
    return out

km = kaplan_meier(tempo, falhou)
print(f"  {'t (h)':>10}{'em risco':>10}{'S(t)':>9}{'IC 95% de S(t)':>22}   curva")
print("  " + "-" * 72)
for t_i, nr, S, lo, hi in km:
    barra = "#" * int(round(S * 30))
    print(f"  {t_i:>10.1f}{nr:>10d}{S:>9.4f}     [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}]   {barra}")
print("  " + "-" * 72)
print(f"\n  S({T_FIM:.0f} h) estimado por KM = {km[-1][2]:.4f}")
print(f"  S({T_FIM:.0f} h) pelo modelo Weibull ajustado = "
      f"{np.exp(-(T_FIM/e_c)**b_c):.4f}")
print("\n  KM nao assume forma nenhuma - e o retrato honesto dos dados. Mas nao")
print("  extrapola alem de 1000 h, e e para la que a pergunta de garantia aponta.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (3)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(3) INCERTEZA DE beta e eta  -  bootstrap com censura")
print("=" * 74)
B = 2000
bb, ee = [], []
for _ in range(B):
    idx = rng.integers(0, N_UNID, N_UNID)
    if falhou[idx].sum() < 3:
        continue
    try:
        b_, e_ = weibull_mv(tempo[idx], falhou[idx], chute=(b_c, e_c))
        if 0.05 < b_ < 20 and 1 < e_ < 1e6:
            bb.append(b_); ee.append(e_)
    except Exception:
        pass
bb, ee = np.array(bb), np.array(ee)
ic_b = np.percentile(bb, [2.5, 97.5]); ic_e = np.percentile(ee, [2.5, 97.5])
b10_boot = np.array([b10(b, e) for b, e in zip(bb, ee)])
ic_b10 = np.percentile(b10_boot, [2.5, 97.5])

print(f"  beta   = {b_c:6.3f}   IC 95% = [{ic_b[0]:.3f}, {ic_b[1]:.3f}]")
print(f"  eta    = {e_c:6.1f} h IC 95% = [{ic_e[0]:.1f}, {ic_e[1]:.1f}] h")
print(f"  B10    = {b10(b_c,e_c):6.1f} h IC 95% = [{ic_b10[0]:.1f}, {ic_b10[1]:.1f}] h")
print(f"\n  Fracao de reamostras bootstrap com beta > 1: {(bb > 1).mean():.3f}"
      f"   -> {'evidencia de DESGASTE' if (bb>1).mean() > 0.95 else 'inconclusivo'}")
print("  (NOTA: isto e uma fracao bootstrap, NAO uma probabilidade posterior.")
print("   Para uma probabilidade posterior seria preciso um prior - cap. 136.)")
print(f"\n  Com apenas {int(falhou.sum())} falhas, o IC de beta e largo. Isto NAO e um defeito")
print("  da analise: e a informacao honesta de que poucas falhas nao caracterizam")
print("  bem a FORMA da distribuicao. Comprometer-se com uma politica de")
print("  manutencao a partir daqui exige declarar esta incerteza.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (4)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(4) SUBSTITUICAO PREVENTIVA  -  so compensa se beta > 1")
print("=" * 74)
C_PREV, C_CORR = 250.0, 3000.0   # custo de substituicao preventiva vs corretiva

def custo_por_hora(T, beta, eta, c_prev=C_PREV, c_corr=C_CORR, n_sim=40_000, rng=rng):
    """Politica de idade: substitui na falha OU em T horas, o que ocorrer primeiro."""
    vida = eta * rng.weibull(beta, n_sim)
    falhou_antes = vida < T
    custo = np.where(falhou_antes, c_corr, c_prev)
    duracao = np.where(falhou_antes, vida, T)
    return custo.mean() / duracao.mean()

print(f"  Custo preventivo EUR {C_PREV:.0f} | custo corretivo EUR {C_CORR:.0f}\n")
for beta_cen, rotulo in [(0.7, "beta = 0,7  (mortalidade infantil)"),
                          (1.0, "beta = 1,0  (taxa constante)"),
                          (2.5, "beta = 2,5  (desgaste)")]:
    eta_cen = 1600.0
    base = custo_por_hora(1e9, beta_cen, eta_cen)   # so corretiva
    print(f"  {rotulo}")
    print(f"    {'intervalo T (h)':>18}{'custo/hora':>14}{'vs. so corretiva':>20}")
    melhor_T, melhor_c = None, base
    for T in (300, 600, 900, 1200, 1600, 2400):
        c = custo_por_hora(T, beta_cen, eta_cen)
        var = 100 * (c - base) / base
        if c < melhor_c:
            melhor_T, melhor_c = T, c
        print(f"    {T:>18d}{c:>14.4f}{var:>19.1f}%")
    print(f"    {'so corretiva':>18}{base:>14.4f}{0.0:>19.1f}%")
    if melhor_T:
        print(f"    -> OTIMO: substituir a cada {melhor_T} h "
              f"(poupanca {100*(base-melhor_c)/base:.1f}%)\n")
    else:
        print(f"    -> NENHUM intervalo compensa. Substituicao preventiva PIORA"
              f" o custo.\n")
print("  Confirmacao numerica da regra: com beta <= 1 a substituicao preventiva")
print("  nao traz beneficio (beta=1) ou e ativamente prejudicial (beta<1), porque")
print("  a peca nova tem taxa de falha MAIOR que a peca em servico.")

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Censoring and bias.**
*A report computes "average life" as the mean of the failure times of only the components that
failed, ignoring the censored ones. Quantify the problem.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The number is **severely underestimated**, and the size of the bias shows up in the code cell.

The reasoning: the ones that failed before the test ended are, by construction, the **weakest** in
the sample. The mean of the first ones to fail is roughly the mean of the lowest order statistics —
not the mean of the population.

The censored units carry the information "life > 1,000 h," which is highly informative and gets
entirely discarded. The cell shows the three estimators side by side against the true value
(η = 1,600 h): both wrong methods underestimate, and the censoring-aware method recovers the
correct order of magnitude.

**The correct fix** is the likelihood with censoring (option (c) in the code). Note that the
seemingly "conservative" alternative — counting the censored units as if they'd failed at 1,000 h —
**also underestimates**, though less: it assumes the surviving units failed at the exact moment the
test stopped, which is false and statistically arbitrary. Both wrong methods bias things **in the
same direction**; only the censoring-aware method recovers the correct order of magnitude.

**Practical consequence:** if this number is used to size spare-parts stock or to set the warranty
period, the error is expensive either way — over-stocking and excessive warranty provisions, or
(with the bias flipped) under-sizing and shortages.

</details>

**E2 — Interpreting β.**
*You fit a Weibull to bearing failures and got β = 0.75 with a CI of [0.52, 1.08]. What maintenance
policy do you recommend?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**First, the honest statistical reading:** the CI includes 1.0. The data is compatible with a
constant failure rate and with a decreasing rate. **There's no evidence of wear-out.**

**Second, the policy implication:** scheduled preventive replacement **isn't justified**. With β ≤
1, a new part has an instantaneous failure rate equal to or higher than that of a part already in
service — replacing it means paying for a part while potentially raising the risk.

**Third, and most important: β < 1 in a bearing is an anomalous result.** Bearings typically fail
by fatigue, with β between 1.5 and 3. A β of 0.75 is a diagnostic signal, not a component
characteristic. The hypotheses to investigate, in order:

1. **Installation defect** — misalignment, incorrect preload, wrong tool during assembly;
2. **Contamination or lubrication** — early failure from particles or inadequate lubricant;
3. A **bad lot** from the supplier;
4. **Mixed populations** — if there are two failure modes (early installation failures + late
   fatigue), fitting a single Weibull produces an artificially low β. The fix is a **mixed
   Weibull** or separating the modes via physical failure analysis.

**Recommendation:** suspend any preventive-replacement plan, do a **physical failure analysis** on
the failed units to identify the mode, and attack the root cause of the infant mortality. If β
climbs above 1 after the fix, preventive replacement becomes debatable — and then you compute the
optimal interval as in the code cell.

**Note on the wide CI:** with few failures, β is poorly estimated. Before expensive decisions, it's
worth gathering more failures or using field data from similar components as a prior (Chapter 136).

</details>

**E3 — Kaplan-Meier vs Weibull.**
*When do you use each one?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

| Criterion | Kaplan-Meier | Weibull |
|---|---|---|
| Assumptions | None about the shape | Assumes the Weibull family |
| Extrapolation | **Impossible** beyond the last time | Possible (with the risk stated) |
| Physical interpretation | Descriptive | β tells you the **mechanism** (infant/random/wear-out) |
| Few failures | Very imprecise, wide steps | More efficient (uses the shape) |
| Multiple modes | Shows the real shape, including a mixture | A single fit can mask the mixture |

**In practice, use both, in this order:**

1. **KM first, always.** It's the assumption-free portrait. It lets you see whether there are
   steps, plateaus, or slope changes suggesting more than one mechanism.
2. **Weibull afterward**, and **check the fit by overlaying it on the KM curve**. If the Weibull
   curve doesn't track the KM curve, the model is wrong and extrapolating with it would be worse
   than useless.
3. **Extrapolate with the Weibull** only when (a) the fit tracks the KM curve, (b) β is physically
   plausible for the known failure mechanism, and (c) the extrapolation is stated as such, with its
   corresponding confidence interval.

**Warning sign:** if the KM curve shows a clear "elbow" — a sharp drop followed by a plateau —
there are probably **two populations** (one bad lot + the rest). A single Weibull will pass through
the middle of both and describe neither.

</details>

**E4 — Accelerated testing.**
*You tested at 125 °C and got η = 400 h. The estimated activation energy is 0.7 eV. What life do you
expect at 55 °C use temperature? What caveats do you state?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**The calculation (Arrhenius):**

Acceleration factor AF = exp[(E_a/k)·(1/T_use − 1/T_test)], with temperatures in kelvin and
k = 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV/K.

- T_test = 398.15 K; T_use = 328.15 K
- E_a/k = 0.7/8.617×10⁻⁵ ≈ 8,123 K
- 1/328.15 − 1/398.15 = 3.0474×10⁻³ − 2.5116×10⁻³ = 5.358×10⁻⁴
- AF = exp(8,123 × 5.358×10⁻⁴) = exp(4.353) ≈ **77.7**

**η at 55 °C ≈ 400 × 77.7 ≈ 31,100 h** (≈ 3.5 years of continuous operation).

**The caveats that have to be stated — and this is the part of the exercise that matters:**

1. **Same failure mechanism.** The extrapolation is only valid if the units at 125 °C fail through
   the **same physical mechanism** they would fail through at 55 °C. Mandatory check: failure
   analysis on the accelerated samples, compared against field failures. If the high temperature
   activated diffusion or polymer degradation that doesn't occur at 55 °C, the 31,100 h figure is
   fiction.

2. **Sensitivity to E_a.** The exponent is linear in E_a, so AF is **exponentially** sensitive:
   - E_a = 0.6 eV → AF ≈ 41.7 → η ≈ 16,700 h
   - E_a = 0.7 eV → AF ≈ 77.7 → η ≈ 31,100 h
   - E_a = 0.8 eV → AF ≈ 144.6 → η ≈ 57,800 h

   A ±0.1 eV variation — within the typical uncertainty of an estimate — makes the predicted life
   swing by a factor of **≈3.5**. Reporting "31,100 h" without this analysis is misleading.

3. **E_a estimated from how many temperatures?** With just one temperature level, E_a comes from
   the literature, not from the data — and the uncertainty is large. A defensible accelerated test
   uses **at least three** stress levels, which lets you estimate E_a from the data itself and
   **test** the Arrhenius linearity.

4. **Statistical uncertainty in η.** The 400 h figure has its own confidence interval, which
   propagates multiplicatively.

**A defensible statement:** *"Under the assumption of a single Arrhenius mechanism with E_a = 0.7 ±
0.1 eV, the characteristic life at 55 °C is estimated between ~17,000 and ~58,000 h. The test used a
single temperature level, so E_a was not experimentally verified. Testing at three temperatures and
comparative failure analysis are recommended before this supports a warranty claim."*

</details>
