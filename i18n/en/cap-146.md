## Chapter 146: Dependence, Bayesian Networks, Markov, and Dynamic Risk ✦

### The engineering question

> *"We have two independent barriers, each with a 5% failure rate. The probability both fail is
> 0.25%. Right?"*

Probably wrong — and the error can span two orders of magnitude. This chapter covers the three
formalisms that represent dependence: **Bayesian networks** (structural dependence), **Markov
chains** (dependence over time), and **copulas** (tail dependence).

---

## 146.1 Why independence almost never holds

You've already seen the beta-factor model (Chapter 144) and the effect of correlation on tolerance
stack-up (Chapter 140). The generalization: in real systems, dependence arises through four
channels:

1. **Common physical cause** — shared infrastructure, power, environment, raw material.
2. **Common human cause** — same training, same procedure, same person.
3. **Cascade** — the failure of A increases the load on B (the most insidious one).
4. **Correlated stress** — in a crisis, all controls degrade at the same time.

The critical point is the fourth: **independence tends to fail precisely when it matters**. Under
normal operation, risks look independent; under stress, they correlate. A model calibrated on normal
operation systematically underestimates crisis risk.

---

## 146.2 Bayesian networks: structural dependence

A Bayesian network is a DAG (the same object from Chapter 137) with conditional probability tables.
The joint distribution factorizes as:

$$ P(X_1,\dots,X_n) = \prod_i P(X_i \mid \text{parents}(X_i)) $$

**What makes it valuable for quality risk, and that no other tool offers:**

1. **Represents common cause explicitly** — a parent node shared by several barriers.
2. **Allows inference in any direction.** Given that a defect reached the customer, what's the
   probability the cause was the supplier? It's **diagnostic**, not just predictive.
3. **Combines data and expert judgment** in the conditional tables.
4. **Updates with partial evidence.** Observe one node, all the others update.

**Compared to FTA:** FTA is a special case of a Bayesian network with deterministic nodes (AND/OR
gates) and no reverse inference. The Bayesian network generalizes: probabilistic gates (the barrier
works 92% of the time, not 100%), explicit common cause, and diagnosis.

---

## 146.3 Markov chains: risk that evolves

When risk depends on the system's **state** and the state changes over time:

$$ \pi_{t+1} = \pi_t P $$

with P the transition matrix. Typical states in an industrial process: *under control → drifting →
out of control → stopped*.

**What Markov analysis gives you:**

- **Stationary distribution** — the fraction of time in each state in the long run. It's the true
  availability metric.
- **Mean time to absorption** — how long until an undesired state is reached (system MTTF).
- **Effect of an intervention** — change one transition rate and recompute. This is how you quantify
  the value of preventive maintenance or faster detection.

**The Markov assumption** (the future depends only on the present, not the history) is strong. It
holds reasonably well for physical degradation; it fails when there's memory — accumulated fatigue,
for example. In that case, use states that encode the history (higher-order Markov) or semi-Markov
models.

---

## 146.4 Copulas: dependence only in the tail

A copula separates the **marginal distributions** from the **dependence structure**. It's the right
tool when risks are weakly dependent under normal operation and strongly dependent in a crisis:

| Copula | Tail dependence | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | **None** in the tails | The default, and systematically optimistic in a crisis |
| Student's t | Symmetric in both tails | Two-sided shocks |
| **Gumbel** | **Upper** | Risks that worsen together — the typical industrial case |
| Clayton | Lower | Joint failures in a benign scenario |

The Gaussian copula with moderate correlation gives **exactly zero** tail dependence — extreme
events are asymptotically independent. That property is what made it notoriously inadequate in the
2008 financial crisis, and the same limitation applies to industrial risk.

---

## 146.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Multiplying probabilities without justifying independence | Underestimation by orders of magnitude | Beta factor, Bayesian network, or copula |
| Redundancy without diversity | Two barriers that are actually one | Diversify method, person, and infrastructure |
| Correlation estimated under normal operation | Crisis risk underestimated | Copula with tail dependence; scenarios |
| Gaussian copula for extreme risk | Zero tail dependence by construction | Gumbel or t |
| Markov where there's memory | Wrong degradation forecast | States encoding history; semi-Markov |
| Bayesian network with made-up tables | Apparent precision with no basis | Document the source of every probability |

# ============================================================================
# Capitulo 146 - Exemplo resolvido
# (1) Rede bayesiana: causa comum + inferencia diagnostica
# (2) Cadeia de Markov: estados de um processo e valor de intervir
# (3) Copulas: dependencia de cauda e o seu efeito na perda agregada
# ============================================================================
import numpy as np
from itertools import product
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(146)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (1)
print("=" * 78)
print("(1) REDE BAYESIANA  -  causa comum e inferencia diagnostica")
print("=" * 78)
print("""  Estrutura:
        FORMACAO_DEFICIENTE (causa comum)
              /            \\
    INSPECAO_1_FALHA    INSPECAO_2_FALHA
              \\            /
                  E
              DEFEITO_PASSA
""")
P_FORM = 0.12                       # P(formacao deficiente na equipa)
P_FALHA_COM_FORM   = 0.35           # P(inspecao falhar | formacao deficiente)
P_FALHA_SEM_FORM   = 0.04           # P(inspecao falhar | formacao adequada)

# --- calculo exato por enumeracao
p_ambas = 0.0
for f in (True, False):
    pf = P_FORM if f else 1 - P_FORM
    pfalha = P_FALHA_COM_FORM if f else P_FALHA_SEM_FORM
    p_ambas += pf * pfalha * pfalha       # condicionalmente independentes DADO f

# --- o que a analise ingenua faria: usar a marginal e multiplicar
p_marg = P_FORM * P_FALHA_COM_FORM + (1 - P_FORM) * P_FALHA_SEM_FORM
p_ingenuo = p_marg ** 2

print(f"  P(formacao deficiente)                = {P_FORM:.4f}")
print(f"  P(inspecao falha | formacao deficiente) = {P_FALHA_COM_FORM:.4f}")
print(f"  P(inspecao falha | formacao adequada)   = {P_FALHA_SEM_FORM:.4f}")
print(f"\n  P(uma inspecao falhar), marginal      = {p_marg:.5f}")
print(f"  P(AMBAS falharem), calculo INGENUO    = {p_ingenuo:.6f}  "
      f"(1 em {1/p_ingenuo:,.0f})")
print(f"  P(AMBAS falharem), com causa comum    = {p_ambas:.6f}  "
      f"(1 em {1/p_ambas:,.0f})")
print(f"\n  Fator de subestimacao do calculo ingenuo: {p_ambas/p_ingenuo:.1f}x")
print("\n  As duas inspecoes SAO condicionalmente independentes dado o estado da")
print("  formacao. Mas NAO sao marginalmente independentes - e e a probabilidade")
print("  marginal que se usa quando se multiplica sem pensar.")

# --- inferencia diagnostica (Bayes ao contrario)
p_ambas_dado_form = P_FALHA_COM_FORM ** 2
p_form_dado_ambas = (P_FORM * p_ambas_dado_form) / p_ambas
print(f"\n  INFERENCIA DIAGNOSTICA - passou um defeito pelas duas inspecoes.")
print(f"    P(formacao deficiente) ANTES da evidencia  = {P_FORM:.4f}")
print(f"    P(formacao deficiente) APOS a evidencia    = {p_form_dado_ambas:.4f}")
print(f"    razao de verosimilhanca                    = "
      f"{p_form_dado_ambas/P_FORM:.1f}x")
print("\n  Isto e o que uma FTA nao consegue fazer: dado o efeito, atualizar a")
print("  probabilidade da causa. E exatamente a pergunta de uma investigacao de")
print("  causa-raiz - e a rede bayesiana responde-lhe quantitativamente.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (2)
print("\n" + "=" * 78)
print("(2) CADEIA DE MARKOV  -  estados de um processo produtivo")
print("=" * 78)
estados = ["Sob controlo", "Em deriva", "Fora de controlo", "Parado"]
# P[i][j] = P(estado j no turno seguinte | estado i agora)
P = np.array([
    [0.92, 0.06, 0.015, 0.005],   # sob controlo
    [0.30, 0.55, 0.130, 0.020],   # em deriva
    [0.10, 0.25, 0.550, 0.100],   # fora de controlo
    [0.60, 0.30, 0.050, 0.050],   # parado (reparacao)
])
custo_estado = np.array([0.0, 1_200.0, 9_500.0, 42_000.0])   # EUR por turno

def estacionaria(P):
    w, v = np.linalg.eig(P.T)
    i = np.argmin(np.abs(w - 1.0))
    pi = np.real(v[:, i]); return pi / pi.sum()

pi = estacionaria(P)
print(f"  {'Estado':<20}{'% do tempo':>13}{'custo/turno':>14}{'custo esperado':>17}")
print("  " + "-" * 64)
for k, e in enumerate(estados):
    print(f"  {e:<20}{100*pi[k]:>12.2f}%{custo_estado[k]:>14,.0f}"
          f"{pi[k]*custo_estado[k]:>17,.2f}")
print("  " + "-" * 64)
custo_base = float(pi @ custo_estado)
print(f"  {'TOTAL':<20}{100*pi.sum():>12.2f}%{'':>14}{custo_base:>17,.2f}")
print(f"\n  Custo esperado por turno: EUR {custo_base:,.0f}  "
      f"-> EUR {custo_base*3*250:,.0f}/ano (3 turnos, 250 dias)")

print("\n  VALOR DE INTERVIR - efeito de melhorar UMA taxa de transicao:")
print(f"  {'Intervencao':<50}{'custo/turno':>14}{'poupanca/ano':>17}")
print("  " + "-" * 78)
intervencoes = [
    ("SPC deteta a deriva mais cedo (0,30->0,55)", 1, 0, 0.55),
    ("Manutencao preventiva (0,06->0,03)",           0, 1, 0.03),
    ("Reacao mais rapida a saida de controlo (0,10->0,30)", 2, 0, 0.30),
]
for nome, i, j, novo in intervencoes:
    Q = P.copy()
    delta = novo - Q[i, j]
    Q[i, j] = novo
    # reequilibra a linha mantendo as proporcoes relativas das restantes
    outros = [k for k in range(4) if k != j]
    resto = Q[i, outros].sum()
    if resto > 0:
        Q[i, outros] = Q[i, outros] * (1 - novo) / resto
    piQ = estacionaria(Q)
    c = float(piQ @ custo_estado)
    print(f"  {nome[:48]:<50}{c:>14,.0f}{(custo_base-c)*3*250:>17,.0f}")
print("  " + "-" * 78)
print("\n  A cadeia de Markov transforma 'devemos investir em deteccao ou em")
print("  prevencao?' numa comparacao em euros. Repare que a intervencao mais")
print("  valiosa nao e necessariamente a que atua no estado pior.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (3)
print("\n" + "=" * 78)
print("(3) COPULAS  -  dependencia de cauda e o seu efeito no risco agregado")
print("=" * 78)
N_SIM = 200_000
print("  Dois riscos com AS MESMAS marginais lognormais (media EUR 500k),")
print("  e a MESMA correlacao de Spearman ~0,5, mas estruturas de cauda diferentes.\n")

def marginais(u):
    """Transforma uniformes em perdas lognormais com media 500k."""
    sg = np.sqrt(np.log(1 + 1.2 ** 2)); mu = np.log(500_000) - sg ** 2 / 2
    return np.exp(mu + sg * stats.norm.ppf(np.clip(u, 1e-12, 1 - 1e-12)))

# --- independente
u1 = rng.random(N_SIM); u2 = rng.random(N_SIM)
L_ind = marginais(u1) + marginais(u2)

# --- copula gaussiana (rho tal que Spearman ~0,5)
rho = 2 * np.sin(np.pi * 0.5 / 6)
Z = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], [[1, rho], [rho, 1]], N_SIM)
L_gauss = marginais(stats.norm.cdf(Z[:, 0])) + marginais(stats.norm.cdf(Z[:, 1]))

# --- copula de Gumbel (dependencia na cauda SUPERIOR)
theta = 1.54
def gumbel_amostra(n, theta, rng):
    """Amostragem de copula de Gumbel via variavel estavel positiva."""
    beta_ = 1.0 / theta
    w = rng.uniform(0, np.pi, n)
    e = rng.exponential(1.0, n)
    S = (np.sin((1 - beta_) * w) / e) ** ((1 - beta_) / beta_) \
        * np.sin(beta_ * w) / (np.sin(w) ** (1 / beta_))
    e1, e2 = rng.exponential(1.0, n), rng.exponential(1.0, n)
    u = np.exp(-(e1 / S) ** beta_)
    v = np.exp(-(e2 / S) ** beta_)
    return u, v
g1, g2 = gumbel_amostra(N_SIM, theta, rng)
L_gum = marginais(g1) + marginais(g2)

def es(L, q=0.99):
    v = np.percentile(L, 100 * q)
    return (L[L > v].sum() / len(L) + v * ((L <= v).mean() - q)) / (1 - q)

print(f"  {'estrutura':<22}{'Spearman':>10}{'media':>13}{'P99':>14}"
      f"{'P99,9':>14}{'CVaR 99%':>14}")
print("  " + "-" * 87)
for nome, L, a, b in [("independente", L_ind, u1, u2),
                       ("copula gaussiana", L_gauss, stats.norm.cdf(Z[:,0]), stats.norm.cdf(Z[:,1])),
                       ("copula de Gumbel", L_gum, g1, g2)]:
    sp = stats.spearmanr(a, b).statistic
    print(f"  {nome:<22}{sp:>10.3f}{L.mean():>13,.0f}{np.percentile(L,99):>14,.0f}"
          f"{np.percentile(L,99.9):>14,.0f}{es(L):>14,.0f}")
print("  " + "-" * 87)

# dependencia de cauda empirica
def dep_cauda(a, b, q=0.99):
    lim = np.quantile(a, q)
    return (b[a > lim] > np.quantile(b, q)).mean()
print(f"\n  Dependencia de cauda empirica P(B extremo | A extremo), q = 99%:")
print(f"    independente     : {dep_cauda(u1,u2):.4f}   (esperado ~0,01)")
print(f"    copula gaussiana : {dep_cauda(stats.norm.cdf(Z[:,0]),stats.norm.cdf(Z[:,1])):.4f}")
print(f"    copula de Gumbel : {dep_cauda(g1,g2):.4f}")
print("\n  A copula gaussiana tem dependencia de cauda ASSINTOTICAMENTE NULA:")
print("  por muito alta que seja a correlacao, os eventos extremos tendem a ser")
print("  independentes. A de Gumbel mantem a dependencia na cauda - e e essa a")
print("  estrutura realista quando dois riscos se agravam pela mesma causa.")
print("\n  Consequencia pratica: usar a copula gaussiana (o padrao da maioria das")
print("  ferramentas) SUBESTIMA sistematicamente o risco agregado de cenarios")
print("  extremos. Foi essa a licao de 2008 - e vale igualmente na industria.")

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Common cause.**
*Two "independent" barriers with 5% failure each. Quantify the effect of a common cause.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Naive calculation:** 0.05 × 0.05 = 0.0025 → 1 in 400.

**With the beta-factor model** (a fraction β of failures is common-cause):

P(both) = β × 0.05 + [(1−β) × 0.05]²

| β | P(both) | vs. independent |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.00250 | 1.0× |
| 0.05 | 0.00476 | 1.9× |
| 0.10 | 0.00703 | 2.8× |
| 0.20 | 0.01160 | 4.6× |

With β = 0.10 — perfectly realistic for two inspections following the same procedure — the real
probability is nearly **3 times** the calculated one.

**The code cell shows the structural version of the same phenomenon**, with a Bayesian network: the
two inspections are **conditionally independent given the training state**, but **marginally
dependent**. And it's the marginal probability that gets used when you multiply without thinking.

**How to estimate β in practice, in order of evidence quality:**

1. **Joint-failure data** — if there are enough records, estimate it directly. There's rarely enough
   data, because joint failures are rare (that's the whole point).
2. **Reference values from the literature** — the nuclear and process industries publish typical β
   values between 0.01 and 0.20 depending on the degree of diversity.
3. **Structured diversity analysis** — low β requires diversity in: physical principle,
   manufacturer, person, procedure, timing, and power source. Each shared dimension raises β.

**The engineering conclusion:** redundancy is only redundancy if it's **diverse**. Two visual
inspections following the same procedure aren't two barriers — it's one barrier applied twice.

</details>

**E2 — Diagnostic Bayesian network.**
*A defect passed through both inspections. What's the probability the cause was deficient training?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The cell computes it. With P(deficient training) = 0.12 as the prior, observing that **both**
inspections failed substantially raises that probability — via Bayes:

P(F | both failed) = P(F) × P(both | F) / P(both)

**Why this matters in a root-cause investigation:** the evidence of **simultaneous failure** is much
more informative than two isolated failures at different times. Two isolated failures are consistent
with chance; simultaneous failure points to a common cause.

**How this changes the investigation:** instead of investigating each inspection separately (the
natural, and wrong, approach), the analysis says to look for what the **two share** — training,
procedure, acceptance criterion, lighting, shift. It's a different, and more productive, direction of
investigation.

**The advantage over FTA:** FTA computes P(top) from the causes. A Bayesian network also does the
reverse: given the effect, it updates the probability of each cause. That's precisely an
investigation's question — and the reason Bayesian networks are increasingly used in incident
analysis.

</details>

**E3 — Markov and investment decisions.**
*Compare investing in faster detection versus prevention, using the Markov model.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The cell compares three interventions, each changing a transition rate, and converts the effect into
euros/year.

**The method, in five steps:**

1. Define the **states** so they're observable and mutually exclusive.
2. Estimate the **transition matrix** from history (counting transitions between consecutive
   shifts), or from structured judgment when there's no data.
3. Compute the **stationary distribution** → fraction of time in each state.
4. Multiply by the **cost of each state** → expected cost per shift.
5. Change the rate the intervention affects and **recompute**. The difference is the intervention's
   value.

**The result, in this example:** the most valuable intervention is **detecting drift earlier**
(€325k/year), followed by **faster reaction to an out-of-control state** (€239k/year), and only then
**preventive maintenance** (€204k/year).

This contradicts two intuitions at once:

- the most valuable intervention does **not** act on the worst state (the "stopped" one), but on the
  intermediate state, through which all the flow passes;
- **prevention** — reducing the probability of entering drift — is here the **least** profitable,
  despite being the one intuition would pick.

**The reason is structural:** the value of an intervention depends on **how much flow passes through
the transition it changes**. The "drifting" state occupies 15% of the time and is the gateway into
the expensive states; improving the exit from that state acts on all of that flow. Preventive
maintenance acts on a transition that already has low probability (0.06), so the absolute gain is
smaller.

**Methodological conclusion:** don't choose between prevention and detection on principle. Calculate
— the answer depends on the structure of the transition matrix, and it changes from process to
process.

**The two limitations that must be stated:**

- **The Markov assumption.** The future depends only on the current state. It fails when there's
  accumulated degradation — a worn tool doesn't "forget" the wear. In that case, either add states
  that encode the history, or use a semi-Markov model.
- **Stationarity.** The matrix is estimated from one period; if the process changes, it stops
  holding. Re-estimating periodically and comparing the matrices is, in itself, a form of drift
  detection.

</details>

**E4 — Copulas and tail risk.**
*Why does the Gaussian copula underestimate extreme risk?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Because the Gaussian copula has **zero tail-dependence coefficient** for any correlation ρ < 1.
Formally:

λ_U = lim_{q→1} P(V > q | U > q) = 0

It means that, in the limit of extreme events, the risks behave as **independent**. The code cell
measures this empirically: the tail dependence at q = 99% of the Gaussian copula is far below that
of the Gumbel, even with the same Spearman correlation.

**Why this is wrong in industrial practice:** reality is the opposite. Risks that look independent
under normal operation **worsen together** in a crisis:

- A flood simultaneously affects production, logistics, and suppliers in the same region.
- A power outage stops production **and** the traceability system.
- A demand spike simultaneously raises the risk of setup error, rushed inspection, and deferred
  maintenance.
- A supplier's financial crisis simultaneously degrades quality, lead time, and responsiveness.

**The quantitative consequence** is in the cell's table: with identical marginals and the same
Spearman correlation, the P99.9 and CVaR 99% of the aggregate loss are substantially higher under
Gumbel than under Gaussian. Using the Gaussian — the default in most tools — produces a
systematically insufficient provision.

**Practical recommendation:**

1. For decisions that depend on the **tail** (provisions, insurance, continuity), use a copula with
   upper tail dependence (Gumbel) or run explicit **scenario analysis**.
2. Report the result under **two dependence structures** and show the range. It's more honest than
   picking one and presenting a single number.
3. When the data doesn't allow estimating the dependence structure — the normal case, because joint
   extreme events are rare — scenario analysis is preferable to a copula: it forces you to name the
   mechanism by which the risks connect, instead of hiding it inside a parameter.

</details>
