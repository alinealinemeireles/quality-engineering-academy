## Chapter 908: Barriers to Quality Improvement

> **Editorial note.** Chapter written from scratch for the web edition, covering topic I.I of the ASQ CQE Body of Knowledge ("Barriers to Quality Improvement"), which didn't have a dedicated chapter in this edition.

### The engineering question

*The team solved the technical problem, the project closed successfully, and the KPI improved. Three months later, the process quietly drifted back to the old way of working. What was missing?*

Almost always, what was missing was treating the improvement as an organizational change, not just a technical one. A technically correct solution that ignores the human and structural barriers around it has a short life — and a quality engineer who only knows how to diagnose the process, without diagnosing the organization around it, will repeat the same "fixed, then reverted" cycle indefinitely.

### Four categories of barrier

Quality management literature — from Deming to recent systematic reviews on continuous improvement in complex environments — converges on four broad categories, which rarely appear in isolation:

- **Cultural barriers.** Resistance or reluctance toward a quality-focused culture, and fear of blame or punishment when a problem is reported — which leads people to hide deviations instead of flagging them. A culture that punishes whoever raises a hand against a problem guarantees that, next time, no one raises their hand.
- **Technical barriers.** Lack of statistical competence and improvement tools among those who need to apply them day to day, and the absence of quality information systems (Chapter 87) capable of sustaining data-based decisions.
- **Structural barriers.** Related to organizational structure, processes, and systems: functional silos that prevent collaboration between areas, reward systems that pay for volume or cost in isolation rather than quality, and the absence of clear roles and responsibilities to sustain improvement after the project ends.
- **Strategic barriers.** Inadequate planning and objectives poorly aligned with business strategy — quality initiatives treated as a parallel program, disconnected from the strategic planning covered in Chapter 87, instead of being part of it.

### Four specific, recurring barriers

Beyond the categories above, four specific barriers show up often enough in consulting and audit practice to deserve individual mention:

1. **Absence of a quality definition for that specific product or service.** "Quality" without an operational definition (fewer defects? on-time delivery? conformance to the customer's specification?) can't be measured, and what isn't measured isn't managed.
2. **Lack of long-term leadership commitment.** Deming's first of fourteen points (Chapter 1) already addressed this directly: without constancy of purpose from leadership, any improvement initiative competes with whatever priority shows up next on the executive agenda — and loses.
3. **Decisions made by opinion instead of data.** When the organization's decision-making culture isn't fact- and data-driven (Chapter 90, quality management principles), improvement becomes a matter of who argues loudest in the room, not who has the strongest evidence.
4. **Shortage of qualified professionals to lead improvement.** Without people trained in improvement tools (the tools covered in Part V) and without certification validating that competence, every project reinvents the method, with inconsistent results from team to team.

### How to identify and measure the impact

Identifying barriers calls for the same diagnostic tools already covered elsewhere in this manual, applied to the organizational system instead of the technical process: stakeholder analysis (Chapter 87) reveals who has the power to block or accelerate the change; force-field analysis (Chapter 11) separates the forces pushing the change from those holding it back, and is especially useful because it forces the team to explicitly name the barriers instead of leaving them implicit; and the cause-and-effect diagram (Chapter 39), normally applied to product defects, works just as well applied to the question "why didn't the change stick."

Measuring a barrier's impact, not just describing it, is what distinguishes a generic complaint ("leadership doesn't support this") from an actionable finding ("of the last five improvement projects in this area, three reverted to the prior state within six months, and all three share the absence of a formally designated process owner after project closeout").

### Methods for overcoming barriers

There's no single solution — the method depends on which category of barrier dominates the specific case — but a few principles are cross-cutting: visible, sustained leadership commitment (not just an opening speech, but periodic progress reviews); aligning reward and measurement systems with quality objectives, not just cost or volume; investment in training and, where applicable, in formal certification (the CQE Body of Knowledge itself is, in a sense, a catalog of what a quality professional needs to know to fill the technical barrier); and structured change-management techniques — stakeholder analysis, organizational readiness assessment, communication plans — instead of assuming a technically correct solution sustains itself.

### Proposed exercise: a barrier-prioritization matrix in Excel

Build, in a spreadsheet, a matrix with one row per identified barrier (use the force-field analysis from Chapter 11 and the cause-and-effect diagram from Chapter 39 as sources) and four columns: **Category** (cultural, technical, structural, strategic), **Impact** (1-5, how much it delays or reverses improvement), **Frequency** (1-5, in how many recent projects it showed up), and **Ease of treatment** (1-5, inverted: 5 = easy to resolve). Calculate a priority score with `=Impact*Frequency*(6-EaseOfTreatment)` — the same multiplicative logic as the risk priority number (RPN) covered in Chapter 81 — and sort descending. In **Power BI**, the same table feeds a simple dashboard tracking, over time, how many barriers in each category were closed per quarter — useful for showing leadership that removing barriers is itself a maturity indicator, not just a theoretical diagnostic exercise.

### Common mistakes

1. **Treating the barrier as an implementation detail, not part of the project's scope.** If the barrier isn't addressed in the project plan, it won't disappear on its own once the project ends.
2. **Confusing the absence of complaints with the absence of barriers.** A blame culture produces silence, not the absence of problems.
3. **Applying the same solution to barriers from different categories.** Technical training doesn't fix a misaligned reward system, and a process restructuring doesn't fix a lack of leadership commitment.
4. **Closing the project without transferring sustainment responsibility to a clear process owner.** It's the most common structural barrier behind improvements that revert.
