## Chapter 136: Bayesian Inference Applied to Quality Engineering
### The engineering question

> *"I inspected 40 parts from a new supplier and found no nonconforming ones. Can I say the defect
> rate is zero? What if I already know suppliers like this typically run around 2%?"*

The frequentist answer to "0 defects in 40" is the Wilson or Clopper-Pearson interval: something
like [0%, 8.8%]. Correct, but it ignores everything the organization already knows. The Bayesian
answer **formally incorporates that prior knowledge** and returns something frequentism, by
construction, cannot give: the **probability that the parameter lies in an interval**.

This chapter doesn't treat Bayesianism as a philosophical alternative. It treats it as a **decision
tool** for three situations where the classical method is weak: little data, relevant prior
knowledge, and the need for continuous updating.

---

## 136.1 The theorem, and what changes in practice

$$ p(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto p(\text{data} \mid \theta) \times p(\theta) $$

- **Prior** p(θ) — what was known beforehand: supplier history, a similar process, the physics of
  the problem, or deliberately little (weak prior).
- **Likelihood** p(data|θ) — the model of the data-generating mechanism. It's the same object
  frequentism uses.
- **Posterior** p(θ|data) — the **complete distribution** of the parameter given the data.

The decisive operational difference lies in the interpretation:

| | Frequentist | Bayesian |
|---|---|---|
| Object | 95% confidence interval | 95% credible interval |
| Reading | "95% of intervals built this way would contain θ" | "**There's a 95% probability θ is here**" |
| Possible question | — | "What's the probability that p > 3%?" |
| Use of history | Informal, outside the model | Explicit, in the prior |

The second reading is the one managers and auditors **think** they're reading off a confidence
interval. Bayesianism gives them the object they think they already have.

---

## 136.2 Beta-Binomial: quality's workhorse case

For a proportion (nonconforming rate, acceptance rate, a model's hit rate), the conjugate
Beta-Binomial pair solves everything analytically:

- **Prior:** θ ~ Beta(a, b)
- **Data:** x nonconforming out of n inspected
- **Posterior:** θ | data ~ **Beta(a + x, b + n − x)**

The update is arithmetic. No simulation needed.

**How to choose the prior — and how to defend it in an audit:**

| Situation | Prior | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| No information at all | Beta(1, 1) = Uniform | "any rate between 0 and 1 is equally plausible" |
| Reference prior | Beta(0.5, 0.5) = Jeffreys | minimal impact, invariant to reparameterization |
| History: ~2%, moderate confidence | Beta(2, 98) | "equivalent to having seen 2 NC out of 100 parts" |
| Strong history: ~2%, high confidence | Beta(20, 980) | "equivalent to having seen 20 NC out of 1000 parts" |

**The golden rule of defensibility:** a + b is the prior's **equivalent sample size**. A
Beta(20, 980) claims knowledge equivalent to 1000 already-inspected parts. If that history doesn't
exist, the prior is indefensible. Always state the prior **and its equivalent size**, and present a
**sensitivity analysis** with alternative priors.

---

## 136.3 Where Bayesian clearly wins in quality

**1. The zero-defects problem.** With 0 out of 40, the maximum-likelihood estimate is 0% — an
absurd claim. The Beta(1, 41) posterior gives a mean of 1/42 ≈ 2.4% and a 95% upper bound of
~7%. It doesn't claim the impossible.

**2. Sequential updating.** Each inspected lot updates the posterior, which becomes the prior for
the next lot. This is the exact mathematical formalization of what an experienced engineer does
informally when gaining confidence in a supplier — and it links directly to skip-lot schemes
(Chapter 141).

**3. Decisions with direct probability.** "P(rate > AQL) = 12%" is a sentence a risk manager can
use. "We failed to reject H0 at 5%" is not.

**4. Reliability with few failures.** Informative priors derived from component families let you
produce useful estimates where the data alone is insufficient.

**5. Combining heterogeneous sources.** Lab test + field history + expert judgment, in a single
coherent model.

---

## 136.4 Expected loss: the bridge to decision

The posterior isn't the final answer. The final answer is the **action**. For each action a, define
a loss function L(a, θ) and choose the action that minimizes the expected loss under the posterior:

$$ a^* = \arg\min_a \; \mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid \text{data}}\big[ L(a, \theta) \big] $$

This is where the cost asymmetry — letting a bad lot through vs. rejecting a good one — enters the
calculation formally, instead of staying an implicit judgment call. The code example shows the
"accept / inspect 100% / reject" decision solved this way.

## 136.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Strong prior with no documented basis | The conclusion is dominated by opinion, not data | State a+b and justify it; sensitivity analysis |
| Choosing the prior after seeing the data | Circularity; the result stops being evidence | Fix and record the prior before data collection |
| Reading the credible interval as a CI | Conceptual confusion (even though numerically close with a weak prior) | Use the correct language |
| Assuming "Bayesian" means you don't need data | Little data + weak prior = wide posterior, as it should be | The posterior is honest about the uncertainty |
| Stopping at the posterior | No decision actually gets made | Define the loss function and minimize expected loss |

```py-r
--- python
# ============================================================================
# Capitulo 136 - Exemplo resolvido
# (1) O problema dos "zero defeitos": bayesiano vs frequentista
# (2) Sensibilidade ao prior - a analise que torna o resultado defensavel
# (3) Atualizacao sequencial lote a lote
# (4) Decisao por perda esperada com custos assimetricos
# ============================================================================
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(136)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (1)
print("=" * 74)
print("(1) ZERO DEFEITOS EM 40 PECAS  -  o que se pode realmente afirmar")
print("=" * 74)
n, x = 40, 0

# Frequentista: Clopper-Pearson (exato)
lo_cp = 0.0 if x == 0 else stats.beta.ppf(0.025, x, n - x + 1)
hi_cp = stats.beta.ppf(0.975, x + 1, n - x)
# Regra dos tres: limite superior aproximado 3/n quando x = 0
regra3 = 3 / n

# Bayesiano com prior uniforme Beta(1,1)
a_post, b_post = 1 + x, 1 + n - x
media_b = a_post / (a_post + b_post)
ic_b = stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], a_post, b_post)

print(f"  Observado: {x} nao conformes em {n} pecas\n")
print(f"  Estimativa pontual frequentista (MLE) : {x/n:.4f}  <- afirma taxa ZERO")
print(f"  IC 95% Clopper-Pearson                : [{lo_cp:.4f}, {hi_cp:.4f}]")
print(f"  'Regra dos tres' (limite sup. aprox.) : {regra3:.4f}")
print(f"\n  Posterior bayesiano Beta({a_post},{b_post}) com prior uniforme:")
print(f"    media                               : {media_b:.4f}")
print(f"    intervalo de credibilidade 95%      : [{ic_b[0]:.4f}, {ic_b[1]:.4f}]")
print(f"    P(taxa < 1%)                        : {stats.beta.cdf(0.01, a_post, b_post):.3f}")
print(f"    P(taxa < 5%)                        : {stats.beta.cdf(0.05, a_post, b_post):.3f}")
print("\n  O MLE afirma o impossivel (taxa exatamente zero). O posterior nao.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (2)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(2) SENSIBILIDADE AO PRIOR  -  o que torna a analise defensavel")
print("=" * 74)
priors = [
    ("Uniforme     Beta(1,1)",       1.0,  1.0,  "sem informacao previa"),
    ("Jeffreys     Beta(.5,.5)",     0.5,  0.5,  "prior de referencia"),
    ("Hist. fraco  Beta(2,98)",      2.0, 98.0,  "~2%, equiv. a 100 pecas"),
    ("Hist. forte  Beta(20,980)",   20.0, 980.0, "~2%, equiv. a 1000 pecas"),
    ("Otimista     Beta(1,199)",     1.0, 199.0, "~0,5%, equiv. a 200 pecas"),
]
print(f"  Dados: {x} NC em {n} pecas\n")
print(f"  {'Prior':<26}{'n equiv.':>9}{'media post.':>13}{'IC 95% posterior':>26}")
print("  " + "-" * 74)
for nome, a0, b0, _ in priors:
    ap, bp = a0 + x, b0 + n - x
    m = ap / (ap + bp)
    lo, hi = stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], ap, bp)
    print(f"  {nome:<26}{a0+b0:>9.0f}{m:>13.4f}     [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}]")
print("  " + "-" * 74)
print("\n  Com 40 observacoes, um prior equivalente a 1000 pecas DOMINA os dados.")
print("  Regra: declare sempre a+b (tamanho de amostra equivalente do prior).")
print("  Se esse historico nao existe documentado, o prior e indefensavel.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (3)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(3) ATUALIZACAO SEQUENCIAL  -  10 lotes, prior fraco Beta(1,1)")
print("=" * 74)
p_real = 0.03
a, b = 1.0, 1.0
print(f"  Taxa real do processo (desconhecida do analista): {p_real:.1%}")
print(f"  Cada lote: 50 pecas inspecionadas\n")
print(f"  {'Lote':>5}{'NC':>4}{'acum.n':>8}{'media post.':>13}{'IC 95%':>22}{'P(p>5%)':>10}")
print("  " + "-" * 64)
n_ac = x_ac = 0
for lote in range(1, 11):
    nc = rng.binomial(50, p_real)
    a, b = a + nc, b + 50 - nc
    n_ac += 50; x_ac += nc
    m = a / (a + b)
    lo, hi = stats.beta.ppf([0.025, 0.975], a, b)
    p_alto = 1 - stats.beta.cdf(0.05, a, b)
    print(f"  {lote:>5}{nc:>4}{n_ac:>8}{m:>13.4f}   [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}]{p_alto:>10.3f}")
print("  " + "-" * 64)
print("\n  O intervalo estreita a cada lote e P(p>5%) cai. Esta e a base")
print("  estatistica de um esquema de skip-lot (cap. 141): a confianca no")
print("  fornecedor deixa de ser impressao e passa a ser numero atualizavel.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (4)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(4) DECISAO POR PERDA ESPERADA  -  custos assimetricos")
print("=" * 74)
# Lote de 5000 pecas. Posterior atual sobre a taxa de NC:
N_LOTE = 5000
a_p, b_p = 4.0, 196.0     # posterior: media 2%, equivalente a 200 pecas
CUSTO_NC_NO_CLIENTE = 400.0   # euros por peca NC que chega ao cliente
CUSTO_INSPECAO      = 1.20    # euros por peca inspecionada a 100%
CUSTO_REJEITAR_LOTE = 9000.0  # euros: devolucao, atraso, reposicao
EFICACIA_INSPECAO   = 0.90    # a inspecao 100% deteta 90% dos NC

# amostra do posterior
theta = stats.beta.rvs(a_p, b_p, size=200_000, random_state=136)

perda_aceitar     = theta * N_LOTE * CUSTO_NC_NO_CLIENTE
perda_inspecionar = (N_LOTE * CUSTO_INSPECAO
                     + theta * N_LOTE * (1 - EFICACIA_INSPECAO) * CUSTO_NC_NO_CLIENTE)
perda_rejeitar    = np.full_like(theta, CUSTO_REJEITAR_LOTE)

acoes = {
    "Aceitar sem inspecao": perda_aceitar,
    "Inspecionar a 100%":   perda_inspecionar,
    "Rejeitar o lote":      perda_rejeitar,
}
print(f"  Lote de {N_LOTE} pecas | posterior Beta({a_p:.0f},{b_p:.0f}) "
      f"(media {a_p/(a_p+b_p):.2%})")
print(f"  NC no cliente: EUR {CUSTO_NC_NO_CLIENTE:.0f}/peca | inspecao: "
      f"EUR {CUSTO_INSPECAO:.2f}/peca | rejeicao: EUR {CUSTO_REJEITAR_LOTE:.0f}\n")
print(f"  {'Acao':<24}{'Perda esperada':>17}{'P95 da perda':>15}")
print("  " + "-" * 56)
for nome, perda in acoes.items():
    print(f"  {nome:<24}{perda.mean():>17,.0f}{np.percentile(perda,95):>15,.0f}")
print("  " + "-" * 56)
melhor = min(acoes, key=lambda k: acoes[k].mean())
print(f"\n  DECISAO OTIMA (minima perda esperada): {melhor}")
print("\n  Repare que a decisao depende dos CUSTOS, nao apenas da taxa estimada.")
print("  A tabela abaixo mostra a acao otima a mudar DUAS vezes conforme o custo")
print("  do NC no cliente - informacao que uma matriz de risco 3x3 nunca revela.")

# sensibilidade ao custo do NC no cliente
print("\n  Sensibilidade - acao otima em funcao do custo do NC no cliente:")
for custo in (50, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600):
    pa = (theta * N_LOTE * custo).mean()
    pi = (N_LOTE*CUSTO_INSPECAO + theta*N_LOTE*(1-EFICACIA_INSPECAO)*custo).mean()
    pr = CUSTO_REJEITAR_LOTE
    d = {"Aceitar": pa, "Inspecionar": pi, "Rejeitar": pr}
    print(f"    EUR {custo:>5}/peca -> {min(d, key=d.get):<12} "
          f"(perdas: aceitar {pa:>8,.0f} | inspecionar {pi:>8,.0f} | rejeitar {pr:>8,.0f})")
```

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Zero defects.**
*You inspected 40 parts from a new supplier, all conforming. The buyer wants to write in the report
"defect rate: 0%." What do you argue?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

That "0%" is a claim the data doesn't support, and one that's falsifiable by the very next bad part.

The posterior with a uniform prior is **Beta(1, 41)**: mean ≈ 2.4%, 95% credible interval of
[0.06%, 8.6%]. The defensible statement is:

> *"With 0 nonconforming out of 40 parts, and with no prior information, the rate is estimated at
> ~2.4%, with 95% credibility of being below 8.6%. The data is compatible with a rate as high as
> ~8.6%."*

**The frequentist check** gives the same signal: the "rule of three" says that, with 0 defects out
of n, the 95% upper bound is approximately **3/n** = 3/40 = 7.5%. The two methods agree — which is
expected with a weak prior, and it's the strongest argument for anyone skeptical of Bayesian methods.

**If there's documented history** of suppliers in this category running ~2% (say, equivalent to
100 parts, Beta(2, 98)), the posterior becomes Beta(2, 138): mean 1.43%, CI [0.17%, 3.94%]. More
precise — but only because real, stated information was added.

**Practical recommendation:** 40 parts don't qualify a new supplier for a critical characteristic.
The number needed follows from the upper bound you want to be able to claim: to claim < 1% with 95%
confidence and zero defects, you need ~300 parts (3/0.01).

</details>

**E2 — Defensible prior.**
*Management wants to use Beta(1, 999) because "we know our process is good." Assess this.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The Beta(1, 999) prior has an equivalent size of **a + b = 1000 parts** and a mean of 0.1%. It's a
very strong claim: equivalent to saying 1000 parts have already been inspected and 1 nonconforming
one was found.

**The questions to ask, in order:**

1. **Does that history exist and is it documented?** If yes — same process, same product, same
   period, same nonconformance definition — the prior is legitimate and should be used, with a
   reference to the record.
2. **If it doesn't exist**, the prior encodes optimism, not knowledge. In a customer audit or a
   dispute, a prior with no documented basis destroys the credibility of the entire analysis.

**The decisive test:** with 40 new observations and a prior of 1000, the new data carries 4% of the
weight in the conclusion. If the answer is practically independent of the data collected, it wasn't
an analysis — it was an opinion with mathematical notation.

**The correct practice**, one that resolves the discussion without confrontation:

- Report the result with a **reference prior** (Jeffreys or uniform) as the main result.
- Present the informative prior as a **sensitivity analysis**, stating its equivalent size.
- If the conclusions agree, the decision is robust and the debate over the prior becomes irrelevant.
- If they diverge, the conclusion depends entirely on the prior — and that has to be stated
  explicitly, because it's the most important piece of information the analysis produced.

</details>

**E3 — Sequential updating and skip-lot.**
*After 8 lots of 50 parts with 12 total nonconforming, what's the probability the rate exceeds the
2.5% AQL? Does that justify reducing inspection?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Uniform prior Beta(1,1), data: x = 12 out of n = 400. Posterior = **Beta(13, 389)**.

- Mean = 13/402 ≈ **3.23%**
- 95% CI ≈ [1.74%, 5.17%]
- **P(θ > 0.025) ≈ 0.79**

**Conclusion: no.** There's roughly a 79% probability the true rate exceeds the 2.5% AQL. Reducing
inspection would be exactly the wrong call — the evidence points the opposite way, toward tighter
inspection.

**The contrast that teaches the method:** if the same 8 lots had produced **2** nonconforming out of
400, the posterior would be Beta(3, 399): mean 0.75%, P(θ > 2.5%) ≈ 0.25%. There, reducing
inspection would have solid quantitative footing.

**The connection to the standard.** ISO 2859-1 implements this same logic with deterministic rules
(the switching rules and the skip-lot scheme in ISO 2859-1:2026), calibrated so the user doesn't
have to calculate anything. The Bayesian approach carries out the same reasoning explicitly and
continuously, and it lets you answer questions the table can't — like "what's the probability we're
above the AQL right now?"

**Important compliance note:** when the contract or standard requires ISO 2859-1, it's the standard
that governs the acceptance decision. The Bayesian model is a **supplier and risk management**
tool, not a substitute for the contracted plan.

</details>

**E4 — Decisions with asymmetric costs.**
*Explain why the optimal decision in the code cell changes with the cost of a nonconforming unit
reaching the customer.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Because the three actions have different cost structures with respect to θ:

- **Accept:** loss = θ × N × C_customer. **Grows linearly with C_customer.**
- **Inspect 100%:** loss = N × C_inspection + θ × N × (1−effectiveness) × C_customer. Has a high
  fixed cost but only 10% exposure to the customer cost (inspection catches 90%).
- **Reject:** constant loss, independent of θ and C_customer.

With a low C_customer, the fixed cost of inspection (5000 × €1.20 = €6000) isn't justified:
accepting is cheaper. As C_customer rises, the loss from accepting grows until it crosses the loss
from inspecting, and then the loss from rejecting.

**The risk-engineering lesson:** the correct decision **doesn't depend on the estimated rate
alone**. It depends on the rate, the cost structure, and the effectiveness of the control. Two
plants with exactly the same posterior on θ should make different decisions if the costs of the
consequences differ — which is obvious once written this way, and systematically ignored by
qualitative 5×5 risk matrices, which collapse this information into a single color.

**Extension to tail risk (Chapter 145):** the output table also shows the **P95 of the loss**. A
risk-averse organization might prefer the action with the higher expected loss and the smaller tail
— exactly the CVaR reasoning. Minimizing the mean is a choice, not an obligation, and it should be
a stated choice tied to a defined risk appetite.

</details>
