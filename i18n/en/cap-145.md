## Chapter 145: Risk Quantification — Probability, Consequence, Monte Carlo, and Expected Value ✦

### The engineering question

> *"Both risks have the same expected loss: €200,000/year. Should I treat them the same way?"*

**No.** And the reason why is this chapter's content.

Quantitative risk is not `probability × impact`. That formula is a first-order approximation that
collapses an entire distribution into a single number — and loses exactly the information that
decides: the **shape of the tail**.

---

## 145.1 Why expected loss isn't enough

Consider two risks, both with an expected loss of €200,000/year:

| | Risk A | Risk B |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 20 events/year | 0.02 events/year |
| Severity per event | €10,000 | €10,000,000 |
| **Expected loss** | **€200,000** | **€200,000** |
| P95 of annual loss | ~€290,000 | **€0** |
| P99.5 of annual loss | ~€350,000 | **€10,000,000** |
| Threat to viability | None | **Possible** |

Risk A is an **operating cost** — predictable, budgetable, manageable through continuous
improvement. Risk B is a low-frequency **existential threat**. Treating them the same because they
share a mean is the most consequential quantification mistake there is.

**The methodological conclusion:** the quantity to model isn't the mean — it's the **distribution of
aggregate annual loss**. The metrics that matter are extracted from it.

---

## 145.2 The frequency-severity model

The standard structure for aggregating risk over a period:

$$ L = \sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i, \qquad N \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \quad X_i \sim F_X $$

- **N** — number of events in the period (Poisson; or negative binomial if there's overdispersion);
- **Xᵢ** — severity of each event (lognormal, Pareto, gamma, or empirical);
- **L** — aggregate loss, whose distribution rarely has a closed form → **Monte Carlo**.

**Choosing the severity distribution matters a great deal**, because it governs the tail:

| Distribution | Tail | When it's appropriate |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | Thin | Rarely suitable (allows negative losses) |
| Lognormal | Moderate | Multiplicative costs; the working default |
| Gamma | Moderate | A flexible alternative |
| Pareto / heavy-tailed | **Heavy** | Recalls, litigation, catastrophic events |

> **The trap:** fitting a lognormal to historical data where the catastrophic event never occurred.
> The fit will be excellent in the body of the distribution and catastrophically optimistic in the
> tail — exactly the region that matters. For tail risk, internal historical data is almost always
> insufficient, and it needs to be supplemented with structured expert judgment, industry data, or
> scenario analysis.

---

## 145.3 Tail metrics: VaR and CVaR

**VaR_q (Value at Risk)** — the q-quantile of the loss distribution. "With 95% confidence, the
annual loss won't exceed X."

**CVaR_q (Conditional VaR / Expected Shortfall)** — the **average of the losses that exceed the
VaR**. "If we're in the worst 5% of years, the average loss will be Y."

**Why CVaR is superior to VaR, and the reason is technical:**

1. **VaR says nothing about what lies beyond it.** Two distributions with the same VaR₉₅ can have
   radically different tails. CVaR looks inside the tail.
2. **VaR is not sub-additive.** VaR(A+B) can exceed VaR(A) + VaR(B) — that is, VaR can say that
   diversifying increases risk, which is absurd. CVaR is a **coherent** risk measure and doesn't
   suffer from this problem.

**Application in quality engineering:** sizing warranty provisions, deciding insurance coverage,
setting the risk tolerance limit in euros, and comparing portfolios of improvement projects.

---

## 145.4 Sensitivity: where the uncertainty comes from

Quantifying without sensitivity analysis is spending effort on the less useful half of the problem.
Three levels:

- **Tornado** (varying one parameter at a time, between plausible percentiles) — simple and
  sufficient in most cases.
- **Input-output correlation** over the simulation — cheap, gives the ranking immediately.
- **Sobol indices** (variance decomposition, with interactions) — when relevant interactions exist.

The question sensitivity analysis answers is the only one the decision-maker cares about: *"what
should I invest in to reduce uncertainty — and is it worth it?"*

---

## 145.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Using only `P × I` | Tail invisible; existential risks mistreated | Model the aggregate distribution |
| Summing risks assuming independence | Underestimates the aggregate tail | Model dependence (Ch. 146) |
| Fitting severity only to internal data | Optimistic tail, precisely where it matters | Supplement with scenarios and external data |
| Reporting VaR without CVaR | Unknown what lies beyond the quantile | Report both |
| Insufficient replicates for extreme percentiles | Unstable P99+ estimate | ≥10⁵ replicates; verify with several seeds |
| Quantifying without sensitivity analysis | No idea where to invest | Tornado / Sobol |
| False precision | "€2,347,812/year" on an estimate with a factor-3 uncertainty | Report intervals, not points |

# ============================================================================
# Capitulo 145 - Exemplo resolvido
# (1) Mesma perda esperada, caudas opostas
# (2) Modelo frequencia-severidade e a distribuicao da perda agregada
# (3) VaR, CVaR e a nao sub-aditividade do VaR
# (4) Sensibilidade (tornado): onde reduzir a incerteza compensa
# ============================================================================
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(145)
N_SIM = 200_000

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (1)
print("=" * 78)
print("(1) MESMA PERDA ESPERADA, CAUDAS OPOSTAS")
print("=" * 78)
def agregar(lam, media_sev, cv_sev, n_sim=N_SIM, rng=rng):
    """Perda anual agregada: N ~ Poisson(lam), severidade lognormal."""
    sigma = np.sqrt(np.log(1 + cv_sev ** 2))
    mu = np.log(media_sev) - sigma ** 2 / 2
    n = rng.poisson(lam, n_sim)
    total = np.zeros(n_sim)
    max_n = n.max()
    if max_n > 0:
        sev = rng.lognormal(mu, sigma, (n_sim, max_n))
        mask = np.arange(max_n)[None, :] < n[:, None]
        total = (sev * mask).sum(axis=1)
    return total

A = agregar(lam=20.0,  media_sev=10_000,     cv_sev=0.5)
B = agregar(lam=0.02,  media_sev=10_000_000, cv_sev=0.5)

print(f"  {'':<26}{'Risco A':>16}{'Risco B':>16}")
print("  " + "-" * 58)
print(f"  {'frequencia (ev/ano)':<26}{20.0:>16.2f}{0.02:>16.2f}")
print(f"  {'severidade media (EUR)':<26}{10_000:>16,.0f}{10_000_000:>16,.0f}")
print(f"  {'PERDA ESPERADA (EUR/ano)':<26}{A.mean():>16,.0f}{B.mean():>16,.0f}")
print("  " + "-" * 58)
for q in (50, 75, 90, 95, 99, 99.5, 99.9):
    print(f"  {'P'+str(q)+' da perda anual':<26}{np.percentile(A,q):>16,.0f}"
          f"{np.percentile(B,q):>16,.0f}")
print("  " + "-" * 58)
print(f"  {'P(perda = 0)':<26}{(A==0).mean():>16.4f}{(B==0).mean():>16.4f}")
print(f"  {'P(perda > 1 M EUR)':<26}{(A>1e6).mean():>16.4f}{(B>1e6).mean():>16.4f}")
print(f"  {'coef. de variacao':<26}{A.std()/A.mean():>16.3f}{B.std()/B.mean():>16.3f}")
print("\n  A MESMA perda esperada, tratamentos completamente diferentes:")
print("   - Risco A: custo operacional previsivel. Orcamenta-se e reduz-se por")
print("     melhoria continua. Nunca ameaca a viabilidade.")
print("   - Risco B: em 98% dos anos custa ZERO. No ano em que ocorre, pode")
print("     ultrapassar a capacidade de absorcao. Trata-se por transferencia")
print("     (seguro), barreiras e planos de contingencia - nao por orcamento.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (2)
print("\n" + "=" * 78)
print("(2) O EFEITO DA CAUDA DA SEVERIDADE")
print("=" * 78)
print("  A MESMA frequencia e a MESMA severidade media, tres distribuicoes:\n")
lam, media_sev = 3.0, 200_000.0

def agregar_dist(tipo, n_sim=N_SIM, rng=rng):
    n = rng.poisson(lam, n_sim); max_n = n.max()
    if tipo == "lognormal cv=0,5":
        sg = np.sqrt(np.log(1.25)); mu = np.log(media_sev) - sg**2/2
        sev = rng.lognormal(mu, sg, (n_sim, max_n))
    elif tipo == "lognormal cv=2,0":
        sg = np.sqrt(np.log(5.0)); mu = np.log(media_sev) - sg**2/2
        sev = rng.lognormal(mu, sg, (n_sim, max_n))
    else:  # Pareto com alfa = 2,5 (cauda pesada), mesma media
        alfa = 2.5; xm = media_sev * (alfa - 1) / alfa
        sev = xm * (1 + rng.pareto(alfa, (n_sim, max_n)))
    mask = np.arange(max_n)[None, :] < n[:, None]
    return (sev * mask).sum(axis=1)

print(f"  {'distribuicao':<20}{'media':>13}{'P95':>13}{'P99':>14}{'P99,9':>15}")
print("  " + "-" * 75)
for tipo in ("lognormal cv=0,5", "lognormal cv=2,0", "Pareto alfa=2,5"):
    L = agregar_dist(tipo)
    print(f"  {tipo:<20}{L.mean():>13,.0f}{np.percentile(L,95):>13,.0f}"
          f"{np.percentile(L,99):>14,.0f}{np.percentile(L,99.9):>15,.0f}")
print("  " + "-" * 75)
print("\n  A MEDIA e praticamente a mesma nas tres. O P99,9 difere por um fator")
print("  grande. Se a decisao for sobre provisao ou cobertura de seguro, a")
print("  escolha da distribuicao de severidade e MAIS importante que a")
print("  estimativa da frequencia - e e a que menos atencao costuma receber.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (3)
print("\n" + "=" * 78)
print("(3) VaR, CVaR e a NAO SUB-ADITIVIDADE do VaR")
print("=" * 78)
def var_cvar(L, q=0.95):
    """VaR e Expected Shortfall (CVaR) com tratamento correto de atomos.

    A formula ingenua  mean(L[L >= VaR])  falha quando a distribuicao tem um
    atomo no VaR (o caso de riscos binarios, em que VaR = 0). A definicao
    correta e:
        ES_q = (1/(1-q)) * [ E(L . 1{L > VaR}) + VaR * (P(L <= VaR) - q) ]
    """
    v = np.percentile(L, 100 * q)
    acima = L[L > v]
    excesso = acima.sum() / len(L)                 # E(L . 1{L > VaR})
    ajuste = v * ((L <= v).mean() - q)             # parte do atomo que entra na cauda
    c = (excesso + ajuste) / (1 - q)
    return v, c

print(f"  {'':<14}{'perda esp.':>14}{'VaR 95%':>14}{'CVaR 95%':>14}{'CVaR/VaR':>11}")
print("  " + "-" * 67)
for nome, L in [("Risco A", A), ("Risco B", B)]:
    v, c = var_cvar(L)
    razao = f"{c/v:.2f}" if v > 1 else "n/d (VaR=0)"
    print(f"  {nome:<14}{L.mean():>14,.0f}{v:>14,.0f}{c:>14,.0f}{razao:>11}")
print("  " + "-" * 67)
print("\n  Para o Risco B, o VaR 95% e ZERO (em 95% dos anos nao ha evento) -")
print("  o que sugeriria 'risco nulo'. O CVaR 95% revela a realidade da cauda.")
print("  Reportar VaR sem CVaR num risco de baixa frequencia e enganador.")

print("\n  NAO SUB-ADITIVIDADE do VaR - dois riscos binarios independentes:")
p_ev, perda_ev = 0.04, 1_000_000.0
X = (rng.random(N_SIM) < p_ev) * perda_ev
Y = (rng.random(N_SIM) < p_ev) * perda_ev
S = X + Y
vX, cX = var_cvar(X); vY, cY = var_cvar(Y); vS, cS = var_cvar(S)
print(f"    Cada risco: P(evento) = {p_ev:.0%}, perda = EUR {perda_ev:,.0f}\n")
print(f"    {'':<22}{'VaR 95%':>14}{'CVaR 95%':>14}")
print("    " + "-" * 50)
print(f"    {'Risco X':<22}{vX:>14,.0f}{cX:>14,.0f}")
print(f"    {'Risco Y':<22}{vY:>14,.0f}{cY:>14,.0f}")
print(f"    {'VaR(X) + VaR(Y)':<22}{vX+vY:>14,.0f}{cX+cY:>14,.0f}")
print(f"    {'X + Y (conjunto)':<22}{vS:>14,.0f}{cS:>14,.0f}")
print("    " + "-" * 50)
print(f"\n    VaR(X+Y) = {vS:,.0f} > VaR(X)+VaR(Y) = {vX+vY:,.0f} ?  "
      f"{'SIM - VIOLACAO' if vS > vX+vY else 'nao'}")
print(f"    CVaR(X+Y) = {cS:,.0f} <= CVaR(X)+CVaR(Y) = {cX+cY:,.0f} ?  "
      f"{'SIM - coerente' if cS <= cX+cY + 1 else 'nao'}")
print("\n  O VaR diz que juntar dois riscos independentes AUMENTA o risco face")
print("  a soma dos individuais - o que e absurdo e viola a intuicao de")
print("  diversificacao. O CVaR e uma medida COERENTE e nao tem este defeito.")
print("  E por isso que a regulacao financeira migrou de VaR para Expected")
print("  Shortfall, e a mesma razao vale na engenharia de risco industrial.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (4)
print("\n" + "=" * 78)
print("(4) ANALISE DE SENSIBILIDADE (TORNADO)")
print("=" * 78)
# modelo de custo de garantia
base = dict(unidades=250_000, taxa_falha=0.012, custo_rep=180.0,
            taxa_reclamacao=0.65, custo_logistica=45.0)
faixas = dict(taxa_falha=(0.006, 0.030), custo_rep=(120.0, 300.0),
              taxa_reclamacao=(0.40, 0.90), custo_logistica=(25.0, 90.0),
              unidades=(200_000, 320_000))

def custo_garantia(p):
    return p["unidades"] * p["taxa_falha"] * p["taxa_reclamacao"] * \
           (p["custo_rep"] + p["custo_logistica"])

c_base = custo_garantia(base)
print(f"  Custo de garantia no cenario base: EUR {c_base:,.0f}\n")
print(f"  {'Parametro':<20}{'baixo':>16}{'alto':>16}{'amplitude':>16}")
print("  " + "-" * 68)
res = []
for k, (lo, hi) in faixas.items():
    pl = dict(base); pl[k] = lo
    ph = dict(base); ph[k] = hi
    cl, ch = custo_garantia(pl), custo_garantia(ph)
    res.append((k, cl, ch, abs(ch - cl)))
for k, cl, ch, amp in sorted(res, key=lambda x: -x[3]):
    barra = "#" * int(round(40 * amp / max(r[3] for r in res)))

print("\n  A taxa de falha domina a incerteza do custo de garantia. Consequencia")
print("  operacional: gastar dinheiro a estimar melhor o custo de logistica e")

print("  fiabilidade, cap. 139) reduz substancialmente a incerteza da provisao.")
print("\n  Esta e a pergunta que a analise de sensibilidade responde e que nenhuma")
print("  quantificacao isolada responde: EM QUE VALE A PENA INVESTIR PARA SABER")
print("  MELHOR - e quanto vale essa informacao.")

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Same mean, different tails.**
*Compare two risks with the same expected loss but very different tails. What treatments would you
propose?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The code cell quantifies the example. The treatment strategies are structurally different:

**Risk A — high frequency, low severity (20 × €10k):**

- It's predictable: the coefficient of variation of the annual loss is low, and P95 lands ~45% above
  the mean.
- **Treatment:** absorb and reduce. Budget it as an operating cost, and attack the **frequency**
  through continuous improvement (DMAIC, poka-yoke, SPC). Each event is a learning opportunity, and
  there are enough events to learn from.
- **Insurance doesn't make sense here:** the premium would include the insurer's margin on a cost
  the organization already absorbs easily.
- **Tracking metric:** event rate, with a control chart.

**Risk B — low frequency, high severity (0.02 × €10M):**

- It costs zero in 98% of years. In the year it occurs, it can exceed absorption capacity.
- **Treatment:** layered —
  1. **Reduce the probability** with preventive barriers (this is where the Ch. 144 FTA is used);
  2. **Reduce the severity** with mitigating barriers (early detection, traceability that limits the
     scope of a recall, fast-response capability);
  3. **Transfer** what's left (insurance, contractual clauses);
  4. **Plan the contingency** for when it occurs (Ch. 148).
- **Don't budget the mean:** provisioning €200k/year doesn't protect against €10M in a single year.
- **Tracking metric:** leading KRIs (Ch. 143) and barrier effectiveness verification (Ch. 147) —
  because there aren't enough events to measure the outcome directly.

**The general rule:** high-frequency risks are managed with **improvement**; tail risks are managed
with **barriers and transfer**. Using the mean to choose between the two is the mistake.

</details>

**E2 — Percentiles and CVaR.**
*Model 100,000 warranty-cost scenarios and estimate P95 and CVaR.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The procedure is in the cell, using the frequency-severity model. What to report:

1. **Expected loss** — for budgeting and accounting provision.
2. **P95 and P99** — for sizing the reserve.
3. **CVaR 95%** — for sizing insurance coverage. It's the average of the losses in the worst 5% of
   years, and it's the metric that answers "if it goes badly, how badly?"
4. **P(loss > tolerance)** — for the escalation decision defined in Chapter 143.

**The two mandatory checks before trusting the numbers:**

- **Stability of the extreme percentiles.** Repeat the simulation with 3-5 different seeds. If P99.9
  varies more than ~10% between runs, more replicates are needed. Extreme percentiles are estimated
  from a handful of tail observations and are intrinsically unstable.
- **Sensitivity to the severity distribution.** The second table in the cell shows that the mean is
  practically insensitive to the choice between lognormal and Pareto, but P99.9 varies by a large
  factor. **Reporting the result under two plausible distributions** is more honest than reporting a
  single number under whichever distribution best fit the historical data.

**What to never do:** report "€2,347,812" when the uncertainty about the failure rate is a factor of
2. Spurious precision destroys the analysis's credibility with anyone who knows the process. Report
intervals.

**E3 — Tornado.**
*Run a sensitivity analysis of the main risk drivers and interpret it.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The cell produces the tornado for warranty cost. Interpretation at three levels:

**Level 1 — ranking.** The failure rate dominates the range. The other variables, even when varied
across their plausible extremes, move the result much less.

**Level 2 — decision on where to invest in information.** Reducing uncertainty about a parameter
that doesn't influence the result is waste. More reliability testing (Ch. 139) to characterize the
failure rate substantially reduces the provision's uncertainty; a detailed study of logistics costs
reduces almost nothing.

**Level 3 — expected value of perfect information (EVPI).** The rigorous question is: *what's it
worth to know the failure rate exactly?* Computed by comparing the optimal decision under
uncertainty with the optimal decision under perfect information. If the EVPI is €80,000 and the test
costs €25,000, run the test. It's the formalization of "is it worth investigating further?"

**A tornado limitation you need to know:** it varies **one parameter at a time**. If there are
strong interactions (the effect of the failure rate depends on the repair cost), the tornado
underestimates their joint importance. In that case use **Sobol indices**, which decompose the
variance into main and interaction effects. For most quality risk models — multiplicative and
without complex interactions — the tornado is sufficient.

</details>

**E4 — Independence.**
*Show why two "independent" risks shouldn't be multiplied without checking the assumption.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**The arithmetic demonstration.** If P(A) = P(B) = 0.01:

- Under independence: P(A ∩ B) = 0.0001 → **1 in 10,000**
- With correlation ρ = 0.3 between the indicators: P(A ∩ B) ≈ 0.0001 + 0.3 × √(0.01×0.99×0.01×0.99)
  ≈ 0.0001 + 0.00297 = **0.00307** → 1 in 326

**A factor of 30.** And ρ = 0.3 is modest for events that share causes.

**Where independence fails in an industrial setting — real cases:**

1. **Common infrastructure cause.** Two redundant pumps on the same electrical panel. Two servers in
   the same datacenter. Two suppliers who buy from the same raw-material producer (a tier-2
   dependency nobody maps).
2. **Common human cause.** Two inspections following the same procedure, with the same training and
   the same ambiguous criterion. They aren't two barriers — they're one barrier applied twice.
3. **Common environmental cause.** A flood, a heat wave, a power outage simultaneously affects
   several risks the register treats as separate.
4. **Correlated stress.** During a production peak, the probability of setup error, rushed
   inspection, and deferred maintenance all rise at once. The risks are independent under normal
   operation and strongly correlated under stress — which is exactly when it matters.

**How to handle it, in order of effort:**

- **Beta-factor model** (used in Chapter 144): a fraction β of failures is common-cause. Simple and
  adequate for technical redundancy.
- **Copulas** — allow modeling dependence only in the tail (Gumbel copula), which is the real
  pattern: risks independent under normal operation and correlated in crisis.
- **Scenario analysis** — instead of modeling dependence, define explicit scenarios ("flood in the
  industrial region") in which multiple risks occur together, and evaluate the aggregate impact.

**The practical rule that sums it all up:** before multiplying probabilities, write out what would
have to be true for the events to be independent. If that statement looks implausible written out in
full, don't multiply.

</details>
