## Chapter 134: Data Scientist Statistical Reasoning in Quality ✦

### The engineering question

> *"I have the data. What question can this data actually answer — and what question did I want to answer?"*

A classically trained quality engineer learns **tests**: when to use t, when to use
chi-square, when to use ANOVA. A data scientist applied to quality first learns to
**formulate the quantity to be estimated** and to interrogate the **mechanism that generated the data**.
The difference isn't one of mathematical sophistication — it's one of question order. And it's that
order that separates an analysis defensible in an audit from an analysis that collapses at the first
hard question.

This chapter establishes the required vocabulary for PART XVII. The chapters that follow assume it.

---

## 134.1 The estimand: deciding what to estimate, before choosing the method

**Estimand** is the quantity you intend to estimate, defined precisely enough that two independent
people would arrive at the same number if they had the entire population.

This isn't an academic formality. Consider the seemingly simple question:

> *"What's line 3's defect rate?"*

There are at least five different estimands hidden inside it:

| Estimand | Precise definition | When it's the right one |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal proportion of parts | number of NC parts / total number of parts, over the period | Per-piece contract, per-piece cost |
| Average proportion by lot | average of each lot's proportions | Lot-by-lot decision, acceptance sampling |
| Expected proportion of a future lot | parameter of the generating process | Forecasting, capability, warranty |
| Defect rate (not defective rate) | defects / opportunity | Processes with multiple defects per unit |
| Conditional proportion | NC given a product type/shift/machine | Diagnosis and improvement |

These five numbers are **numerically different** for the same data. If the small lot has a
high rate, the marginal proportion and the average of per-lot proportions diverge — and only one of
them is correct for the decision at hand.

**The required sequence**, before any code:

1. **Engineering question** — what decision will be made with the result?
2. **Experimental unit** — what entity does the treatment/condition apply to, independently?
3. **Target population** — what set do you want to draw conclusions about?
4. **Data-generating mechanism (DGP)** — how did this data come to exist?
5. **Design** — observational or experimental? sampling how?
6. **Estimand** — the quantity, defined unambiguously.
7. **Estimation method** — only now do you choose the tool.
8. **Uncertainty** — an interval, not just the point.
9. **Decision and consequence** — what changes based on the result, and what's the cost of being wrong.

Notice that choosing the statistical test is **step 7 of 9**. The overwhelming majority of analysis
errors in quality happen in steps 2 through 5, and no amount of sophistication in step 7 fixes them.

## 134.2 Experimental unit vs. measurement unit: pseudoreplication

This is the distinction that most often invalidates industrial analyses.

- **Experimental unit** — the smallest entity to which the condition is applied **independently**.
- **Measurement (observational) unit** — the entity on which the reading is taken.

A concrete example: you want to compare two heat-treatment recipes. You process **one entire furnace**
with recipe A and **another entire furnace** with recipe B. From each furnace you pull **50 parts** and
measure hardness.

How many independent observations do you have? **Two.** Not a hundred.

The 50 parts from the same furnace share everything: the same load, the same temperature profile, the
same day-to-day variation. They're **subsamples**, not replicates. Treating them as n=50 per group is
**pseudoreplication**: the standard error gets divided by √50 instead of √1, the p-value becomes
tiny, and you conclude with enormous confidence something the data doesn't support.

*(With a single furnace per recipe, the correct test doesn't even exist — there are no degrees of
freedom to estimate the error. That's the most important diagnosis: the problem isn't the analysis,
it's the design.)*

The code cell below uses a **less extreme but still realistic** version of the same problem —
**3 furnaces per recipe, 20 parts per furnace** — where the correct test is possible, and it
quantifies the damage of getting it wrong.

**How to fix it:** either replicate the experimental unit (several furnaces per recipe, or several
cycles randomized in time), or aggregate to the correct level (each furnace's average is one
observation), or explicitly model the hierarchical structure with a **mixed model**
(Chapter 54), which attributes the variation to the right source.

## 134.3 The data-generating mechanism

Before analyzing, answer: **why does this row exist in the table?**

In industrial data, the answer is rarely "because it was randomly sampled." It's more often:

- because the operator decided to measure that part (suspect parts get measured more);
- because the system only logs when the test finishes (the ones that abort disappear);
- because the part survived until the step where the measurement happens (**post-treatment selection**);
- because the customer complained (you only see the failures someone bothered to report).

Each of these mechanisms introduces a distinct bias, and none is fixed by increasing n.

### Missing data: MCAR, MAR, MNAR

| Type | Definition | Industrial example | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MCAR** | Missingness is independent of everything, observed and unobserved | Sensor broke down on a random day | Complete-case analysis is unbiased (only loses efficiency) |
| **MAR** | Missingness depends only on **observed** variables | Less measurement happens on the night shift, and shift is recorded | Multiple imputation or weighting, conditioning on shift |
| **MNAR** | Missingness depends on the **unobserved value itself** | The test wasn't logged *because* the result was bad | No standard method fixes this. You need to change data collection or model the mechanism |

**MNAR is the common case in industry and the most dangerous one**, because it's indistinguishable
from MAR just by looking at the data. Only process knowledge reveals it. When you suspect MNAR, the
only honest response in an analysis is a **sensitivity analysis**: how bad would the missingness
mechanism have to be to flip the conclusion?

> **Capital error:** imputing with the mean. Besides biasing things under MAR and MNAR, it **always**
> underestimates variance — because it replaces unknown values with a value that has no dispersion at
> all. The resulting confidence interval is falsely narrow.

## 134.4 Leakage: the model that predicts the past

**Leakage** occurs when a variable used in a prediction only exists **after** the moment the
prediction would actually be needed.

The decisive test is temporal, and it has just one question:

> *At the exact moment I need this prediction, is this variable already available in the system?*

Real cases in quality:

- predicting scrap using the **disposition code**, which the inspector fills in after the decision;
- predicting a customer complaint using the **warranty cost**, which only exists after the complaint;
- predicting equipment failure using the **corrective maintenance work order**, which gets opened by the failure;
- normalizing/scaling **before** splitting into train and test, letting the test set's mean influence
  the transformation (subtle but real leakage).

The signature of leakage is performance that's **too good**: an AUC of 0.97 on a task process
experts consider hard. When the result seems too excellent, the working hypothesis should be
leakage, not genius.

---

## 134.5 Simpson's paradox and aggregation

An association can **flip sign** when groups are aggregated. This isn't a statistical curiosity —
it's the mechanism by which aggregated quality reports produce wrong conclusions every month.

The example in the code below: two suppliers, two part types. Supplier B has a **lower** scrap rate
**for each part type** — and, in aggregate, a **higher** rate. The cause is mix composition: B
produces proportionally more of the hard part type.

The operational lesson: **never compare aggregate rates without checking whether the mix is
comparable.** When it isn't, either stratify or standardize (apply the same reference mix to both).

## 134.6 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing the test before defining the estimand | Precisely answering the wrong question | Go through the 9 steps in order |
| Confusing measurement unit with experimental unit | Falsely small p-value, false significance | Aggregate to the correct level or use a mixed model |
| Imputing missing data with the mean | Bias + underestimated variance | Multiple imputation; sensitivity analysis if MNAR |
| Ignoring where the row in the table came from | Undetected selection bias | Document the DGP before analyzing |
| Accepting excellent performance without investigating | A model with leakage goes to production and fails | Temporal audit of every variable |
| Comparing aggregate rates with different mixes | Reversed conclusion (Simpson) | Stratify or standardize |

```py-r
--- python
# ============================================================================
# Capitulo 134 - Exemplo resolvido
# (1) Pseudorreplicacao: o custo de confundir unidade de medicao com experimental
# (2) Paradoxo de Simpson: quando a agregacao inverte a conclusao
# ============================================================================
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- (1)
# Cenario: 2 receitas de tratamento termico. Cada receita foi aplicada a
# 3 fornos. De cada forno retiram-se 20 pecas. O EFEITO REAL da receita e ZERO;
# toda a diferenca observavel vem da variacao ENTRE fornos.
n_fornos_por_receita = 3
n_pecas_por_forno    = 20
efeito_real_receita  = 0.0     # a verdade: as receitas sao equivalentes
sd_entre_fornos      = 3.0     # variacao de forno para forno (grande)
sd_dentro_forno      = 1.0     # variacao peca a peca dentro do mesmo forno

def simular_experimento(rng):
    dados = {}
    for receita, deslocamento in [("A", 0.0), ("B", efeito_real_receita)]:
        medias_forno = rng.normal(50 + deslocamento, sd_entre_fornos, n_fornos_por_receita)
        pecas = np.array([rng.normal(m, sd_dentro_forno, n_pecas_por_forno)
                          for m in medias_forno])
        dados[receita] = {"medias_forno": medias_forno, "pecas": pecas}
    return dados

d = simular_experimento(rng)

# ANALISE ERRADA: trata cada peca como observacao independente (n = 60 por grupo)
t_errado, p_errado = stats.ttest_ind(d["A"]["pecas"].ravel(),
                                     d["B"]["pecas"].ravel())
# ANALISE CORRETA: a unidade experimental e o FORNO (n = 3 por grupo)
t_certo, p_certo = stats.ttest_ind(d["A"]["medias_forno"],
                                   d["B"]["medias_forno"])

print("=" * 74)
print("(1) PSEUDORREPLICACAO  -  o efeito real da receita e ZERO")
print("=" * 74)
print(f"  Analise ERRADA  (n=60 pecas/grupo) : t = {t_errado:+.3f}   p = {p_errado:.4f}")
print(f"  Analise CORRETA (n= 3 fornos/grupo): t = {t_certo:+.3f}   p = {p_certo:.4f}")

# Taxa de falsos positivos ao longo de 2000 experimentos simulados
n_sim = 2000
fp_errado = fp_certo = 0
for _ in range(n_sim):
    s = simular_experimento(rng)
    if stats.ttest_ind(s["A"]["pecas"].ravel(), s["B"]["pecas"].ravel()).pvalue < 0.05:
        fp_errado += 1
    if stats.ttest_ind(s["A"]["medias_forno"], s["B"]["medias_forno"]).pvalue < 0.05:
        fp_certo += 1

print(f"\n  Taxa de FALSOS POSITIVOS em {n_sim} experimentos (alfa nominal = 5%):")
print(f"    analise ERRADA  : {100*fp_errado/n_sim:5.1f} %   <-- muito acima de 5%")
print(f"    analise CORRETA : {100*fp_certo/n_sim:5.1f} %   <-- proximo do nominal")
print("\n  Leitura: a pseudorreplicacao nao torna a analise 'um pouco otimista'.")
print("  Destroi o controlo do erro Tipo I. Conclui-se com confianca algo que")
print("  os dados nao suportam - e o relatorio parece impecavel.")

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- (2)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(2) PARADOXO DE SIMPSON  -  fornecedores A e B, pecas simples e complexas")
print("=" * 74)

# (produzidas, refugadas) por fornecedor e por tipo de peca
dados = {
    "Fornecedor A": {"Simples": (1000,  20), "Complexa": ( 200,  30)},
    "Fornecedor B": {"Simples": ( 200,   3), "Complexa": (1000, 130)},
}

print(f"\n  {'':<14}{'Simples':>18}{'Complexa':>18}{'AGREGADO':>18}")
print("  " + "-" * 68)
agregado = {}
for forn, tipos in dados.items():
    linha = f"  {forn:<14}"
    tot_n = tot_d = 0
    for tipo in ("Simples", "Complexa"):
        n, defeitos = tipos[tipo]
        tot_n += n
        tot_d += defeitos
        linha += f"{100*defeitos/n:>16.2f} %"
    agregado[forn] = 100 * tot_d / tot_n
    linha += f"{agregado[forn]:>16.2f} %"
    print(linha)

print("  " + "-" * 68)
print("\n  Em CADA tipo de peca, B tem taxa de refugo MENOR que A.")
print("  No AGREGADO, B tem taxa MAIOR. Ambas as leituras estao corretas.")
print("  A causa e o MIX: B produz 83% de pecas complexas, A produz apenas 17%.")

# Padronizacao directa: aplica a ambos o mesmo mix de referencia (50/50)
print("\n  Padronizacao com mix de referencia 50% simples / 50% complexa:")
for forn, tipos in dados.items():
    taxas = {t: tipos[t][1] / tipos[t][0] for t in tipos}
    padronizada = 100 * (0.5 * taxas["Simples"] + 0.5 * taxas["Complexa"])
    print(f"    {forn:<14} taxa padronizada = {padronizada:5.2f} %"
          f"   (bruta = {agregado[forn]:5.2f} %)")
print("\n  Com o mix igualado, a comparacao passa a ser justa e B fica a frente.")
print("  Regra operacional: nunca comparar taxas agregadas sem verificar o mix.")
```

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Identifying the estimand.**
*A line produces 30 lots of 500 parts. Question: "what's the proportion of nonconforming parts per lot?"
Identify the experimental unit and the observational unit.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The question, as worded, is **ambiguous** — and that's the point of the exercise.

- **Observational unit:** the part (that's where you observe conforming/nonconforming).
- **Experimental unit:** depends on what varies between lots. If each lot corresponds to a
  distinct condition (new raw-material load, new setup, new shift), then **the lot** is the
  experimental unit and n = 30.

The two possible estimands:

1. **Marginal proportion:** Σ(nonconforming) / Σ(parts) = a single proportion over 15,000 parts.
   Weight proportional to lot size.
2. **Average of per-lot proportions:** (1/30)·Σ pᵢ. Each lot weighs equally, regardless of size.

With equal-sized lots, these coincide. With different sizes, they don't — and if small lots are
systematically worse (startups, short runs), the difference is large.

**Which to choose?** The estimand follows the decision: for a per-piece contract, the marginal one;
to assess process stability lot by lot, the average by lot (and, above all, the **dispersion** of
those proportions, which is the genuinely interesting information).

</details>

**E2 — Leakage.**
*A model predicts customer complaints using a variable created after the complaint was opened.
Explain why a high AUC doesn't imply operational usefulness.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

AUC measures the ability to **rank** cases, evaluated on historical data where the contaminated
variable is present. In production, at the moment the prediction is needed, that variable
**doesn't exist** — it's created by the very event you're trying to predict.

The model didn't learn anything about the causes of the complaint. It learned to recognize the
**mark left by the complaint**. It's equivalent to "predicting" that it rained by observing that the
ground is wet: the association is nearly perfect and the predictive value is nil.

**How to systematically detect it:**

1. For each variable, record the **timestamp of availability** in the source system.
2. Compare it to the **timestamp of the decision** the model is meant to support.
3. Remove everything that only exists afterward.
4. Validate with **temporal separation** (train up to T, test after T), never with a random split
   when there's temporal structure — a random split masks leakage.

**Rule of thumb:** if performance is far above what process experts judge possible, the leading
hypothesis is leakage. Investigate before celebrating.

</details>

**E3 — Simpson.**
*Build an example with two suppliers and two part types where the paradox occurs.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

It's built and verified in the code cell above. The numbers:

| | Simple | Complex | Aggregate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supplier A | 20/1000 = 2.00% | 30/200 = 15.00% | 50/1200 = **4.17%** |
| Supplier B | 3/200 = 1.50% | 130/1000 = 13.00% | 133/1200 = **11.08%** |

B is better at both types (1.50% < 2.00% and 13.00% < 15.00%) and worse in aggregate (11.08% > 4.17%).

**The condition for the paradox:** part type is simultaneously (a) associated with the supplier —
different mixes — and (b) associated with the outcome — the complex part scraps more. In other
words, part type is a **confounder** of the comparison between suppliers (Chapter 137).

**The fix** is to stratify or standardize. With a 50/50 reference mix: A → 8.50%, B → 7.25%.
The correct ordering reappears.

**Practical application:** this is exactly the mechanism by which a supplier scorecard unfairly
penalizes whoever takes on the hard work. A scorecard that doesn't adjust for complexity creates
the wrong incentive: the supplier learns that the way to improve its score is to turn down
difficult parts.

</details>

**E4 — Missing data.**
*Create three scenarios, MCAR, MAR, and MNAR, and choose a defensible strategy for each.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**MCAR** — The CMM's acquisition board broke down on a Tuesday; every measurement from that day
was lost, regardless of part, shift, or outcome.
→ *Strategy:* complete-case analysis. There's no bias, only a loss of efficiency (smaller n,
wider intervals). Document the loss in the report.

**MAR** — Dimensional inspection is done 100% on the morning shift and by sampling on the night
shift. Shift is recorded on every row. The probability of missingness depends on shift (observed),
not on the measurement's value.
→ *Strategy:* multiple imputation conditioning on shift, or inverse-probability-of-observation
weighting. Unbiased analysis is possible **because the variable governing the missingness was
recorded**.

**MNAR** — The operator only logs the hardness test when the result is within specification; when
it's out, they repeat the test and log only the second one. The probability of missingness depends
on the value itself.
→ *Strategy:* no standard statistical method fixes this, because the information needed isn't in
the data. There are two defensible responses, in this order:
1. **Fix data collection** — make logging automatic and non-editable. This is the only real solution.
2. Until that happens, **sensitivity analysis**: assume the missing values follow a distribution
   shifted by δ, and show how the conclusion changes with δ. Report the critical δ that flips the
   decision. If that δ is implausibly large, the conclusion is robust; if it's small, the conclusion
   doesn't hold up, and you should say so.

**What to never do:** impute with the mean and present the resulting confidence interval as if it
were valid. It's the most common practice and the least defensible one.

</details>
