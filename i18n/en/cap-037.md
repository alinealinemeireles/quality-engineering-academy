## Chapter 37: Improve

### Solution Selection Matrix

A solution selection matrix is an analytical tool that lets teams propose and rank solutions for any of the root causes identified in the analyze phase. Although teams can work on multiple solutions simultaneously, if multiple prioritized root causes were found, the Black Belt and other team leaders should stay mindful of timelines, resources, scope, and the project's purpose. If a single solution provides enough positive impact to reach the project's goals, then other solutions can be saved for future improvement projects. If one solution would achieve results, but another solution would be especially easy to implement and would provide additional positive results, the team will likely decide to implement both.

A solution selection matrix can be created in Excel, and teams should work through the document together, in brainstorming mode. It's a good idea to include the whole team, along with relevant subject-matter experts and stakeholders, during solution brainstorming. This ensures the solutions the team proposes are more likely to be a realistic fit for the process and the business; once solutions are selected using the matrix, teams will also likely need to get sponsor, champion, or leadership-board buy-in before partial or full implementation is possible. This is especially true when solutions require spending or will impact processes and people outside the project's scope.

An example solution selection matrix is shown below.

The matrix is filled out as follows:

1. Enter the problem statement in the first column. This should be the final problem statement, arrived at during the measure phase, if the team decided the statement should change after data collection. Otherwise, it can be the define phase's problem statement.
2. Enter a prioritized, validated root cause from the analyze phase. If the team is going to try to solve more than one root cause during the improve phase, it should create a solution selection matrix for each root cause.
3. Brainstorm potential solutions in the third column. During the brainstorming process, teams shouldn't question or try to analyze the solutions, but should record any suggested solution that seems at all viable. The only solutions that can be disregarded are ones clearly out of scope or impossible, but the Black Belt leading the brainstorming exercise will need to use their own judgment about such suggestions.
4. Note, at a fairly high level, the practical methods by which a solution could be implemented. In an example used in the analyze chapter, a commercial bakery had a problem caused by a lack of oven instructions. In that case, the Six Sigma team might propose that the team receive appropriate instructions. The method by which that solution happens is written documentation and training.
5. Rank the solutions. Once a list of possible solutions and practical methods has been created, the team ranks each possibility for effectiveness, feasibility, and cost-benefit. Each category gets a rating between 1 and 10.
6. **Effectiveness** is the measure of how well a solution will eliminate a problem's root cause, with 1 being not effective at all and 10 being highly effective.
7. **Feasibility** is the measure of the effort needed to implement the improvement, with 1 being infeasible due to the effort or resources required, and 10 being highly feasible.
8. **Cost-benefit** is an estimated measure of how a project's costs compare to expected savings. This rating isn't a formal cost-benefit analysis, but a high-level estimate. If the expected savings outweigh the costs associated with a project, the team rates the solution high. Otherwise, the team rates the solution low.
9. The effectiveness, feasibility, and cost-benefit scores are multiplied to calculate an overall score. The overall score can be used to prioritize solutions and select the solution with the best overall rating for effectiveness, feasibility, and cost-benefit.

Consider a possible solution selection matrix created for the medical claim denial example used in the analyze chapter.

During the analyze chapter, a Pareto analysis indicated filing-deadline issues were a root cause for the high claim denials. In the solution selection matrix above, a team identified and prioritized four possible solutions, after discovering claims weren't being submitted on time because the claims-billing team didn't have all the proper information in time.

The first solution is requiring front-desk staff to collect as much information as possible. The team gave this solution a middling rating for effectiveness, because it doesn't address the fact that clinical information could still be missing from the claim. But the process would be effective at collecting demographic information and wouldn't require significant expense or effort to implement, so the team rated it high on the other categories.

Next, the team considered creating and requiring an email or portal system for patients, so the billing team could quickly communicate with patients when information was missing. Since not every patient has email and many likely wouldn't use such a system, the team rated this solution low on feasibility and effectiveness.

The third solution the team considered was requiring clinicians to include all the information needed for billing when charting during the patient visit. The team felt this solution would be reasonably effective, but that clinicians likely wouldn't have time to be that thorough while handling patient concerns. The solution could also delay medical appointments, resulting in a negative impact on revenue.

Finally, the team considered a solution that put certain team members in charge of claims with missing information. Since these team members could focus on missing claims and would work through a new workflow built by the team, they'd be more likely to submit the claims on time. Overall, the team rated this solution the highest, but decided to implement both solution one and solution four, because solution one required so little effort and would actually help drive solution four's success.

### Cost-Benefit Analysis

When teams include the right individuals in the process, a solution selection matrix is very effective at identifying the best possible solutions for implementation. Leadership boards and executive sponsors often want more information about a solution's expected costs and benefits, though, so teams should be prepared to create a cost-benefit analysis. Black Belts often have many of the elements needed for such an analysis after the measure and analyze phases, but teams may also need to work with accounting, finance, or business-planning departments, to get the precise financial details needed.

The goal of a cost-benefit analysis is to compare the costs of implementing a solution against the solution's expected monetary benefits. Costs include expenses like developing or purchasing software, purchasing equipment, developing or renovating facilities, additional labor or hiring, training expenses, additional supplies, and any losses associated with disruption while the solution is implemented. Benefits can include an increase in product margin, increased revenue, cost savings or avoided costs, and intangible benefits, like increased team morale or customer retention.

Six Sigma teams generally aren't in a position to handle a detailed cost-benefit analysis, like one a certified accountant might perform, but they can build a sense of the cost-benefit relationships through the payback analysis method (return on investment). This is the simplest way to approach such an analysis and gives leadership an estimated time before a project "pays for itself."

**Payback Analysis (Return on Investment)**

To run a payback analysis, Six Sigma teams need an estimated cost for the project, as well as the estimated financial benefit per year. Remember: financial benefits don't just include revenue increases. Cost savings, new customers, or mitigating customer loss can all be considered contributors to each year's benefits. The team also needs to understand the estimated operating costs of an improvement for each year.

The formula for this analysis is:

**(Cost of implementing the solution) / (Annual financial benefits − annual costs)**

If a project costs $50,000 to implement and $2,000 a year in extra labor, and the team expects the project to generate $15,000 in financial benefits each year, then the calculation is:

$50,000 / ($15,000 − $2,000)

$50,000/$13,000

Or, approximately 3.84 years until the project pays for itself.

Obviously, organizational leaders are looking for the shortest payback calculations possible, but if the solution will solve a major problem or lay the groundwork for extraordinary future success, a longer payback time doesn't necessarily take a solution out of consideration.

**Net Present Value (NPV)**

A more concise way to calculate cost-benefit is known as Net Present Value, or NPV. NPV adjusts benefits and costs as time passes, because cash flow in the future isn't as valuable as cash flow today, due to inflation and other economic factors. The discount rate for various ventures can be calculated using expected returns, interest rates, or inflation rates. Corporate finance departments can often provide Six Sigma teams with a discount rate used for NPV at the company. A pretty basic NPV template is shown below.

### Piloting a Solution

Once a solution is selected and work is done to bring it to production at a minimal level, the Six Sigma team is ready for a pilot. A pilot is a limited test of a solution in a real environment. No matter how much analysis has been completed or how well test cases have been run, teams can't know for sure how a solution will behave "in the real world." The actual production environment will always have variables teams can't predict, particularly when people are involved in the process.

The benefits of a limited pilot include:

- Resource use is limited, which reduces waste if the solution proves incorrect or ineffective at solving the problem
- Confirmation that the expected results occur
- Lets a new solution's problems be worked out at a smaller scale, minimizing disruption during the full transition
- Lets employees outside the Six Sigma team provide feedback on the solution and implementation, making the final rollout more successful

Teams don't need to pilot-test every change they make. Simple or small changes can be made without a pilot, as long as teams thoroughly document the changes and measure results for verification. When changes are broad in scope, could cause expensive or extensive consequences, or would be hard to reverse, teams should start with a pilot. The same goes for any solution that could be expensive to implement at full scale.

Pilots can happen on a limited scale or for a limited time. Limited-scale pilots involve a specific region, team, group of people, or piece of machinery. Limited-time pilots implement a temporary change; at the end of a scheduled period, the team makes a decision about whether the change should become permanent.

Pilots can happen with both processes and products. Process pilots might feature tests at specific locations, testing results with a few customers, working with a few employees to test new processes, or simulated runs of a process with no impact on the end user. Product pilots are run using test markets, product models, or alpha and beta testing of the product with certain end users.

To build a pilot, a Six Sigma team first needs to select the pilot's target audience. Internal process pilots can be run by a selected team or a few selected employees. External product pilots can be run using a subset of customers. For the best pilot results, avoid skewing the results by selecting the best possible performers or the customers most likely to work hard to ensure a product's success. When the solution is implemented at full scale, it'll be used by everyone, so you want to make sure it works for everyone.

When possible, pilot-test at a very small level and then expand the pilot to a larger audience. That's the premise behind alpha and beta testing. A very small set of loyal customers tests the product first, because you know they'll provide feedback. Then an expanded set of customers tests the product after teams have made changes based on the first group of users' feedback. Finally, if the limited tests are successful, teams choose to roll the product out to the entire audience.

#### Analyzing Pilot and Test Results

Six Sigma specialists can use all the tools associated with the analyze phase to test whether solutions have a positive impact during test or pilot programs. Hypothesis testing can be used to compare before-solution data with after-solution data, determining whether there's a statistically significant, positive change. Graphical analysis can be very useful for demonstrating to executive leaders how a solution positively impacted a problem, by reducing defects, improving output or efficiency, or reducing costs.

### Planning Implementation

Once solutions have been verified through testing and analysis, teams can start the work of implementing changes at full scale. Teams should create and work from an action plan during this vital, active phase of the DMAIC process, to make sure no plan or requirement goes unnoticed. During the active part of the improve phase, the project leader will likely delegate numerous activities to team members, and the team will also depend on input and assistance from people outside the Six Sigma team. Keeping an action-plan document helps everyone on the team see where they stand in the process, what they're responsible for, and by what date the work needs to be finished.

If the Six Sigma team is working with a project manager, they're likely responsible for documenting and tracking the action plan. Teams can also use a basic spreadsheet or Word document, which should be saved in a shared location, to track what needs to be done and who's going to do it.

Since every project is unique, improvement action plans are unique too. Most action plans will contain common tasks, like documentation, training, and transition.

**Documentation**

First, the Six Sigma team should have documented all of its work so far throughout the DMAIC process. If asked, the Black Belt or designated team member should be able to present the analysis in the form of data tables, statistical calculations and explanations, and graphical analysis. All brainstorming activities and diagrams should be saved in a central file location, where every team member can view them, and appropriate team members can edit them as needed. Access to these documents helps team members work efficiently on the additional documentation needed for implementation.

Practically any organization will require new processes or process changes to be logged in standard operating procedures. Depending on a company's organizational structure, Six Sigma teams may be responsible for drafting such documents, or they may need to work with knowledge-management resources to create SOPs according to corporate branding and templates. Teams can also create general announcements, informing other employees about upcoming changes and the reasons for those changes, as well as general reference documents, like quick-reference sheets and FAQs.

**Training**

Strong documentation is fundamental to the next part of implementing improvements, which is team training. Six Sigma teams generally aren't responsible for training the whole team on any improvement they make. Instead, teams start the training process by working with training subject-matter experts or delegates in the department impacted by the upcoming changes. The Six Sigma team trains those individuals, who then go on to train other individuals who'll be impacted by the process. At some point, training on the process should be folded into regular organizational training, run by trainers or knowledge-management departments.

**Transition**

During a DMAIC project's improve phase, teams should start considering the need to hand a process back to the business and to the traditional process owner. Transition is part of the control phase, but teams should move from improve to control with a solid understanding of how the process should be measured and monitored. Strong documentation and training during the improve phase help cement the control phase's success.

### Improve Tollgate Checklist

- Solutions were reviewed and prioritized.
- One or two main solutions were selected for action.
- The solutions were implemented on a limited basis.
- Data from the limited tests was analyzed, and the solutions appear to work as expected.
- A cost-benefit analysis was performed.
- The sponsor, champion, or executive committee approved full implementation of the solution.
- Every team member agrees the solution should be implemented.
- The solution is fully documented through SOPs and training materials.
- Critical staff have been trained on the solution and are prepared to pass that training on to others.
