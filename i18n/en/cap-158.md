## Chapter 904: Advanced OEE — Loss Decomposition, Digital Maturity and Real-Time Data

### The engineering question

*This week's report says OEE 73%. Leadership signs off, the board turns green, everyone moves on.
Except line 2 stopped four times on Tuesday, SKU C had a three-hour ramp-up after the changeover,
and nobody in the report knows that — because the average absorbed all of it.*

The aggregate number isn't wrong. It's just the wrong question. OEE exists to decide **where to
invest the next hour of improvement**, and a weekly average doesn't point anywhere. This chapter
separates the two questions: "what's the number" (Chapter 25 already answers that) and "where
exactly is it being lost" — which is the question that pays the bill.

---

## 1 · Fundamentals: what OEE is, and how each component is calculated

**OEE (Overall Equipment Effectiveness)** was formalized by Seiichi Nakajima in the 1960s, as part
of Total Productive Maintenance (TPM), at Toyota. It's a single number that answers a simple, hard
question: *of all the time the machine could have produced a good part at design speed, how much
did it actually produce?*

$$OEE = Availability \times Performance \times Quality$$

Each factor isolates a different type of loss — which is why all three need to be measured and
reported separately, never just the final product:

| Component | Formula | What it measures | Losses it captures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Availability** | $\dfrac{\text{Operating time}}{\text{Planned time}}$ | Was the machine running when it should have been? | Breakdowns, product changeovers (setup), adjustments |
| **Performance** | $\dfrac{\text{Ideal cycle} \times \text{Parts produced}}{\text{Operating time}}$ | Did it run at design speed? | Minor stops, speed reduction |
| **Quality** | $\dfrac{\text{Good parts}}{\text{Parts produced}}$ | Was what it produced any good? | Scrap, rework, startup losses |

This is TPM's classic theoretical base: the **six big losses**, two per component — exactly the
table Chapter 25 introduces, which this chapter takes as its starting point before going deeper.

**A full, step-by-step example.** An 8-hour shift (480 min) on a packaging line:

1. **Planned time** = 480 min (planned stops like meal breaks already deducted).
2. **Operating time** = 480 − 60 min of unplanned breakdown = 420 min →
   **Availability = 420 / 480 = 87.5%**.
3. Ideal cycle = 1 min/part. In 420 min of operation, the line produced 400 parts (it should have
   produced 420 at the ideal cycle) →
   **Performance = (1 × 400) / 420 = 95.2%**.
4. Of the 400 parts, 360 passed inspection →
   **Quality = 360 / 400 = 90.0%**.
5. **OEE = 0.875 × 0.952 × 0.900 = 74.9% ≈ 75%.**

| OEE range | Reading |
|---|---|
| **< 40%** | Needs urgent improvement — usually a sign of a structural problem, not just execution |
| **40–60%** | Typical of lines with no active loss management |
| **60–85%** | Industry's common range, with real room to gain |
| **≥ 85%** | "World-class" benchmark for most discrete processes |

> **Why this matters for capability (Chapter 71).** A process that stops, speeds up, and slows
> down unpredictably isn't a statistically stable process — and capability only makes sense once
> there's stability. Low Availability and Performance usually show up later as a low Cpk: the
> variation OEE had already flagged on the shop floor.

The rest of this chapter takes these three formulas as known and goes straight to what Chapter 25
doesn't have room to dig into: **exactly where the minutes and the parts get lost**, and how to
read that detail without being fooled by the average.

## 2 · The waterfall: where did the 120 minutes go?

A 480-minute shift. At the end, only 360 minutes produced a good part. Hover over each bar:

```plotly
oee-waterfall
```

*Availability 87.5% (loses 60 min to unplanned stops) × Performance 95.2% (loses 20 min to minor
stops and speed loss) × Quality 90.0% (loses 40 equivalent minutes to scrap and rework) = **OEE
75.0%**.*

The reading the formula alone doesn't give: the three factors don't cost the same to recover.
Availability is the cheapest to attack first (point causes, often already logged in downtime
records). Performance is the most hidden — nobody opens a maintenance order for "the machine is
running 5% slower than normal." Quality is the most expensive, because every lost minute already
consumed material, energy, and machine time before being scrapped.

| Layer | What it consumes | The right question |
|---|---|---|
| **Availability** | Breakdowns, material shortages, product changeover | "Why wasn't the machine running?" |
| **Performance** | Minor stops, cycle slower than standard | "Why did it run slower than it should have?" |
| **Quality** | Scrap, rework, out-of-spec startup | "Why did it produce a bad part while running?" |

## 4 · Why the weekly average lies

A hundred and twenty OEE% points hidden behind a single number. Four SKUs, same week, same line.
Hover over each bar:

```plotly
oee-sku
```

The 73% aggregate is arithmetically correct and operationally useless. **SKU B** (83%) has a
concentrated quality problem — worth investigating the batch's color and dimensions before
touching anything else. **SKU C** (60%) has a slow ramp-up — the gain is in the changeover, not in
the machine running. **SKU D** (74%) has downtime fragmented across many small minor stops — the
kind of loss no aggregate report ever captures, because each one alone "is nothing."

> **Practical rule.** A weekly OEE review always needs two lenses: the number (to know if it's
> improving) and what's behind it (to know what to do on Monday). One without the other turns into
> management theater.

## 5 · The downtime cause tree

Before decomposing performance and quality, clearly separate **planned** stops from **unplanned**
ones — they have different budgets and owners.

| Branch | Category | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Planned** | Maintenance | Lubrication, preventive inspection, scheduled part replacement |
| **Planned** | No production scheduled | Legal restriction, over-capacity, production trial |
| **Planned** | External | Power, water, or warehouse-capacity outage |
| **Unplanned** | Breakdown | Failure of a specific component (nozzle, head, roller, arm) |
| **Unplanned** | Changeover | Reel change, label reload, format change |
| **Unplanned** | Operational | Start-up, end of shift, breaks and meetings |
| **Unplanned** | Speed loss | Wear, manual adjustment, repaired tool |

The point that usually goes unnoticed: **"breakdown" isn't a category, it's a symptom.** An
"encapsulator breakdown" needs to become "encapsulator nozzle-unit failure" before it can generate
any maintenance action — otherwise the same component fails again the following month and the
report shows "breakdown" again, as if it were a new event.

## 6 · The full calculation, in Python, R, Excel, and Power BI

```py-r
--- python
import numpy as np

# 480-min shift. Downtime, part count, and scrap for the day.
tempo_planejado = 480          # min, planned time
downtime = 60                  # unplanned stops
ciclo_ideal = 1.0              # min/part, ideal cycle
total_pecas = 400
pecas_boas = 360

tempo_operando = tempo_planejado - downtime
disponibilidade = tempo_operando / tempo_planejado
performance = (ciclo_ideal * total_pecas) / tempo_operando
qualidade = pecas_boas / total_pecas
oee = disponibilidade * performance * qualidade

print(f"Availability = {disponibilidade:6.1%}")
print(f"Performance  = {performance:6.1%}")
print(f"Quality      = {qualidade:6.1%}")
print(f"OEE          = {oee:6.1%}")

# Decomposition in minutes ("waterfall")
min_disponibilidade = downtime
min_performance = tempo_operando - ciclo_ideal * total_pecas
min_qualidade = ciclo_ideal * (total_pecas - pecas_boas)
tempo_produtivo = tempo_planejado - min_disponibilidade - min_performance - min_qualidade
print(f"\nMinutes lost: availability {min_disponibilidade:.0f}, "
      f"performance {min_performance:.0f}, quality {min_qualidade:.0f}")
print(f"Fully productive time = {tempo_produtivo:.0f} min "
      f"(check: {tempo_produtivo/tempo_planejado:.1%})")

# MTBF / MTTR for the period (5 failures, 480 min cumulative operation, 60 min total repair)
n_falhas = 5
tempo_operacao_total = 12348.0   # hours, accumulated over the month
tempo_reparo_total = 5.0         # hours, accumulated over the month
mtbf = tempo_operacao_total / n_falhas
mttr = tempo_reparo_total / n_falhas
print(f"\nMTBF = {mtbf:8.1f} h    MTTR = {mttr:5.2f} h")
--- r
# 480-min shift. Downtime, part count, and scrap for the day.
tempo_planejado <- 480
downtime <- 60
ciclo_ideal <- 1.0
total_pecas <- 400
pecas_boas <- 360

tempo_operando <- tempo_planejado - downtime
disponibilidade <- tempo_operando / tempo_planejado
performance <- (ciclo_ideal * total_pecas) / tempo_operando
qualidade <- pecas_boas / total_pecas
oee <- disponibilidade * performance * qualidade

cat(sprintf("Availability = %.1f%%\n", disponibilidade * 100))
cat(sprintf("Performance  = %.1f%%\n", performance * 100))
cat(sprintf("Quality      = %.1f%%\n", qualidade * 100))
cat(sprintf("OEE          = %.1f%%\n", oee * 100))

min_disponibilidade <- downtime
min_performance <- tempo_operando - ciclo_ideal * total_pecas
min_qualidade <- ciclo_ideal * (total_pecas - pecas_boas)
tempo_produtivo <- tempo_planejado - min_disponibilidade - min_performance - min_qualidade
cat(sprintf("\nFully productive time = %.0f min (check: %.1f%%)\n",
            tempo_produtivo, tempo_produtivo / tempo_planejado * 100))

n_falhas <- 5
tempo_operacao_total <- 12348.0
tempo_reparo_total <- 5.0
mtbf <- tempo_operacao_total / n_falhas
mttr <- tempo_reparo_total / n_falhas
cat(sprintf("\nMTBF = %.1f h    MTTR = %.2f h\n", mtbf, mttr))
--- excel
' Data: A2=planned time, A3=downtime, A4=ideal cycle, A5=total parts, A6=good parts

Operating Time      =A2-A3
Availability        =(A2-A3)/A2
Performance         =(A4*A5)/(A2-A3)
Quality              =A6/A5
OEE                 =Availability*Performance*Quality

' Waterfall in minutes
Availability Loss   =A3
Performance Loss    =(A2-A3)-(A4*A5)
Quality Loss         =A4*(A5-A6)
Productive Time       =A2-[Availability Loss]-[Performance Loss]-[Quality Loss]

' MTBF / MTTR (B2=n failures, B3=operating hours, B4=repair hours)
MTBF =B3/B2
MTTR =B4/B2
--- dax
// OEE measures in Power BI. 'Production' table with [PlannedTime],
// [Downtime], [IdealCycle], [TotalParts], [GoodParts] per shift.

OperatingTime = SUM(Production[PlannedTime]) - SUM(Production[Downtime])

Availability = DIVIDE([OperatingTime], SUM(Production[PlannedTime]))

Performance =
DIVIDE(
    SUMX(Production, Production[IdealCycle] * Production[TotalParts]),
    [OperatingTime]
)

Quality = DIVIDE(SUM(Production[GoodParts]), SUM(Production[TotalParts]))

OEE = [Availability] * [Performance] * [Quality]

// Traffic light for the KPI card
StatusOEE =
SWITCH (
    TRUE (),
    [OEE] >= 0.85, "World class",
    [OEE] >= 0.60, "Industry average",
    "Needs improvement"
)
```

## 7 · Digital maturity isn't the same as operational maturity

A plant can have real-time dashboards and still react poorly — because the problem was never a
lack of data.

| | Low operational maturity | High operational maturity |
|---|---|---|
| **High digital maturity** | *Report-driven chaos.* Real-time dashboards, but inconsistent reactions — whole meetings spent interpreting what the system already showed. | *Action-driven system.* Real-time signal → predefined reaction → clear shift owner. Data becomes action in minutes. |
| **Low digital maturity** | *Reactive plant.* Manual tracking, firefighting culture, inconsistent routine shift to shift. | *Disciplined but manual.* Strong routines, clear accountability, limited visibility — the discipline exists, it just doesn't scale. |

The difference isn't the tool, it's what the system was designed to trigger. A dashboard built to
**report** creates observers. A system built to **act** creates performance — even with fewer
screens.

### Common mistakes

1. **Reporting only the aggregate OEE.** It hides exactly what needs action (section 4).
2. **Confusing "breakdown" with root cause.** "The machine broke" isn't a cause — it's the symptom
   that still needs the 5 Whys (see the extra Problem-Solving chapter).
3. **Measuring performance with the wrong ideal cycle.** If the ideal cycle is wrong, the whole
   waterfall is wrong — validate it against the equipment specification, not against the
   historical average.
4. **Treating minor stops as "normal."** Each one alone is small; added up, they're usually the
   largest slice of the performance loss.
5. **Buying a new machine before working out stage 6.** If maturity is still at 2 or 3, the
   problem isn't capacity — it's execution discipline.
