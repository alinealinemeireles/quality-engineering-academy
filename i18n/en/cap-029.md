## Chapter 29: Hoshin Kanri: Policy Deployment in Lean Management

### What Hoshin Kanri Is

The term *hoshin kanri* literally means "direction setting," which is why the system is also known as hoshin planning or policy deployment. It's a two-front approach: on one side, long-term strategic alignment and planning, typically with a two-to-five-year horizon; on the other, day-to-day management of operational fundamentals. The underlying principle is that the best results are only achieved when everyone in the organization fully understands the objectives and is involved in the actual planning process to reach them — not just in executing goals defined by others. To be truly effective, hoshin planning also needs to be cross-functional, promoting cooperation across the entire value stream and among different business functions, not just within departmental silos.

### The Seven-Step Process

Hoshin planning systematizes strategy through a process structured into seven steps: identify the main business issues the organization faces; establish measurable business objectives that address those issues; define the overall vision and goals; develop supporting strategies to pursue those goals — in the Lean organization, these strategies explicitly incorporate the already-familiar Lean methods and techniques; determine the specific tactics and objectives that make each strategy achievable; implement performance measures for each business process involved; and finally, measure business fundamentals on an ongoing basis. Standardizing this format — through the specific tables described below — lets the whole organization understand not just the plan's content, but the planning process itself, which makes cascading deployment throughout the structure easier.

### The Hoshin Tables

The hoshin system relies on a standardized set of reports, called tables, used in the review process. Each table contains a header identifying the author and scope of the plan, the current situation that gives meaning to the planned items, the objective to be achieved, the milestones that will indicate when the objective has been reached, the strategies for achieving it, and the measures that will verify whether the strategies are actually working.

The **hoshin review table** presents, during periodic reviews, a single objective and its supporting strategies; each group or individual responsible for multiple objectives generates several of these tables, one per objective. The **strategy implementation table** identifies the tactics or action plans needed to make each strategy achievable, specifying who's involved in each tactic and their exact responsibility, the timeline — generally presented as a Gantt chart — the performance measures, and when the implementation plans will be reviewed. The **business fundamentals table** monitors the basic elements that define a key process's success — safety, people, quality, responsiveness, or cost, for example — and these fundamental indicators need to be under control before the organization turns its attention to longer-term strategies. And the **annual planning table** records the organization's objectives and strategies and passes them down to the next level of the organizational structure, in a cascading process where each level develops its own plan supporting the plan above it — and, in doing so, also gains a real sense of ownership over the plan, not just the obligation to execute it.

### The Annual Cycle: Implementation and Review

Since hoshin is a cyclical process, reviewing the previous year's performance is always the basis for the following year's plan — closing, in practice, the PDCA cycle at the strategic level. For objectives that were successfully achieved, the organization analyzes what worked well, whether the strategies and performance measures originally established were in fact the most appropriate, and captures that learning to pass it on to the rest of the organization. For objectives not achieved, the analysis is more detailed: the responsible teams identify what they would have done differently, looking both backward and forward — this dual look is what actually improves organizational learning capacity over time. It's important to stress that this reflection shouldn't turn into a blame session; the focus falls on the strategies and actions, not on the people.

These reviews happen at every level of the organization, starting at the lowest level that owns a plan, with information flowing progressively up through the management structure — each level uses the review tables from the levels below to build its own review, and discussions between the different levels are essential for reaching consensus on the results. Although the planning cycle is annual, tracking can't wait until year-end: business fundamentals indicators should be reviewed monthly to make sure performance stays on the right track, and the annual plan as a whole should be reviewed quarterly to confirm it's still the right plan given actual business conditions. When indicators aren't evolving in the expected direction, the organization triggers countermeasures in up to three phases: an emergency countermeasure to relieve the immediate problem, a short-term correction to prevent recurrence, and, finally, determining and removing the root cause — the only permanent solution capable of keeping the problem from happening again.
