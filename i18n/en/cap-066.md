## Chapter 66: Rational Subgrouping: the Decision That Determines Everything That Follows ✦

### The engineering question

*Which sources of variation do we want **within** the subgroup, and which do we want **between** subgroups?*

This is, by far, the most consequential and least discussed decision in SPC. It isn't a question of "how many parts per sample." It's a question of **experimental design applied to routine data collection** — and getting it wrong here invalidates the chart, the control limits, the capability study, and the project's conclusions.

### Shewhart's principle

Shewhart put it this way: subgroups should be chosen so that, **if special causes exist, they show up as differences *between* subgroups, not *within* them**.

The mechanics are simple to state and easy to get wrong:

- Variation **within** the subgroup ($\hat{\sigma}_{within}$, estimated from $\bar{R}/d_2$ or $\bar{s}/c_4$) is what the chart assumes to be **unavoidable background noise**. It's what sets the width of the control limits.
- Variation **between** subgroups is what the chart is trying to **detect** as a signal.

$$LC_{\bar{X}} = \bar{\bar{X}} \pm A_2 \bar{R} \qquad \text{where} \qquad A_2\bar{R} \;\text{comes exclusively from within-subgroup variation}$$

From this follows a critical consequence:

> **Anything placed inside the subgroup becomes invisible to the chart.**

If you group parts from a mold's 4 cavities into the same subgroup, the difference between cavities gets absorbed into $\bar{R}$, the limits widen, and the chart loses the ability to detect... the difference between cavities. And anything else of similar magnitude.

### The two pathologies

| Pathology | How it happens | Symptom on the chart | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Subgroup too wide** (stratified) | Units from different sources get grouped together: cavities, spindles, shifts, raw-material lots | Limits too wide; points "glued" to the center line; **almost nothing ever goes out of control** | Blind chart. False sense of a stable process. Underestimated capability. |
| **Subgroup too narrow** (mixed) | Consecutive-in-time subgroups when there's fast drift, or correlated data | Limits too narrow; **lots of false alarms** | The team stops trusting the chart and starts ignoring it. |

The symptom "my chart never flags anything" is almost always a stratified subgroup, and it's — wrongly — read as good performance.

**The rule of thumb.** Draw a sample that represents **as homogeneous a set of conditions as possible**: same machine, same cavity, same operator, same lot, over a short time interval. Then let the differences between conditions show up *between* subgroups — or, better yet, use separate charts per source.

### Worked example: the same machine, two subgrouping strategies

```py-r
--- python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

# --- Processo real: injetora com 4 cavidades ---
# Cada cavidade tem um viés próprio (fonte de variação sistemática),
# e existe ruído comum dentro de cada cavidade.
n_ciclos = 60
vies_cavidade = np.array([0.00, 0.06, -0.03, 0.11])   # mm
sigma_dentro_cavidade = 0.020                          # mm
alvo = 25.00

# deriva térmica lenta a partir do ciclo 35 (a causa especial que queremos detetar)
deriva = np.where(np.arange(n_ciclos) >= 35,
                  (np.arange(n_ciclos) - 35) * 0.004, 0.0)

dados = []
for c in range(n_ciclos):
    for cav in range(4):
        val = alvo + vies_cavidade[cav] + deriva[c] + rng.normal(0, sigma_dentro_cavidade)
        dados.append({"Ciclo": c, "Cavidade": cav + 1, "Diametro": val})
df = pd.DataFrame(dados)

# constantes de cartas de controlo
A2 = {2:1.880, 3:1.023, 4:0.729, 5:0.577}
D3 = {2:0.000, 3:0.000, 4:0.000, 5:0.000}
D4 = {2:3.267, 3:2.574, 4:2.282, 5:2.114}
d2 = {2:1.128, 3:1.693, 4:2.059, 5:2.326}

def carta_xbar_r(g, n, rotulo):
    xbar = g.mean(axis=1); R = g.max(axis=1) - g.min(axis=1)
    xbb, Rb = xbar.mean(), R.mean()
    lsc, lic = xbb + A2[n]*Rb, xbb - A2[n]*Rb
    fora = ((xbar > lsc) | (xbar < lic)).sum()
    sigma_w = Rb / d2[n]
    return dict(Estrategia=rotulo, n=n, LinhaCentral=round(xbb,4),
                LIC=round(lic,4), LSC=round(lsc,4),
                Largura=round(lsc-lic,4),
                sigma_dentro=round(sigma_w,4),
                Pontos_fora=int(fora), Total_pontos=len(xbar)), xbar, lic, lsc

# ESTRATÉGIA A (ERRADA): subgrupo = as 4 cavidades do mesmo ciclo
gA = df.pivot(index="Ciclo", columns="Cavidade", values="Diametro").values
resA, xbarA, licA, lscA = carta_xbar_r(gA, 4, "A) Subgrupo = 4 cavidades (estratificado)")

# ESTRATÉGIA B (CORRETA): subgrupo = 4 ciclos consecutivos DA MESMA cavidade
cav1 = df[df["Cavidade"] == 1]["Diametro"].values
gB = cav1[:(len(cav1)//4)*4].reshape(-1, 4)
resB, xbarB, licB, lscB = carta_xbar_r(gB, 4, "B) Subgrupo = 4 ciclos da cavidade 1")

comp = pd.DataFrame([resA, resB])
print(comp.to_string(index=False))

print(f"\nsigma real dentro de uma cavidade = {sigma_dentro_cavidade:.4f} mm")
print(f"  Estratégia A estima {resA['sigma_dentro']:.4f} mm "
      f"({resA['sigma_dentro']/sigma_dentro_cavidade:.1f}x o valor real — inclui o viés entre cavidades)")
print(f"  Estratégia B estima {resB['sigma_dentro']:.4f} mm "
      f"({resB['sigma_dentro']/sigma_dentro_cavidade:.1f}x o valor real)")

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 4), sharey=False)
for ax, (xb, lic, lsc, r) in zip(axes, [(xbarA, licA, lscA, resA), (xbarB, licB, lscB, resB)]):
    ax.plot(xb, marker="o", ms=3, lw=1, color="#00688f")
    ax.axhline(lsc, color="red", ls="--"); ax.axhline(lic, color="red", ls="--")
    ax.axhline(np.mean(xb), color="green")
    fora = np.where((xb > lsc) | (xb < lic))[0]
    ax.plot(fora, np.array(xb)[fora], "ro", ms=7)
    ax.set_title(f"{r['Estrategia']}\n{r['Pontos_fora']} pontos fora de controlo", fontsize=9)
    ax.set_xlabel("subgrupo")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

```py-r
--- python
%%R
set.seed(2026)
n_ciclos <- 60
vies <- c(0.00, 0.06, -0.03, 0.11)
sigma_w <- 0.020; alvo <- 25.00
deriva <- ifelse(0:(n_ciclos-1) >= 35, (0:(n_ciclos-1) - 35) * 0.004, 0)

m <- outer(1:n_ciclos, 1:4, Vectorize(function(c, cav)
  alvo + vies[cav] + deriva[c] + rnorm(1, 0, sigma_w)))

A2_4 <- 0.729; d2_4 <- 2.059

carta <- function(g, rotulo) {
  xbar <- rowMeans(g); R <- apply(g, 1, function(x) max(x) - min(x))
  xbb <- mean(xbar); Rb <- mean(R)
  lsc <- xbb + A2_4 * Rb; lic <- xbb - A2_4 * Rb
  cat(sprintf("%-42s largura=%.4f  sigma_dentro=%.4f  fora=%d\n",
              rotulo, lsc - lic, Rb / d2_4, sum(xbar > lsc | xbar < lic)))
}
carta(m, "A) subgrupo = 4 cavidades (ERRADO)")
cav1 <- m[, 1]; gB <- matrix(cav1[1:(4*floor(length(cav1)/4))], ncol = 4, byrow = TRUE)
carta(gB, "B) subgrupo = 4 ciclos da cavidade 1")
cat(sprintf("\nsigma real dentro da cavidade = %.4f\n", sigma_w))
```

**Engineering reading.** Strategy A produces a chart that looks excellent: almost nothing out of control, process apparently under control. But the $\sigma_{within}$ estimate is inflated several times over, because it absorbed the between-cavity bias. The limits ended up so wide that the thermal drift introduced at cycle 35 — a real special cause, with a production effect — slips by unnoticed.

Strategy B, restricting the subgroup to a single cavity, recovers the correct background-noise estimate, tightens the limits to less than half the width, and **detects the drift**. Notice that B produces fewer subgroups from the same number of cycles — that's the real cost of not mixing sources, and it's a cost worth paying.

**The engineering decision that follows from this:** with 4 systematically different cavities, the right approach isn't one chart — it's **four charts**, one per cavity, or an averages-by-cavity chart plus a separate analysis of the difference between cavities (which is an ANOVA problem, not an SPC one). The difference between cavities is a structural feature of the mold to be fixed in the tooling, not a signal to be chased daily.

### Consequence for capability

This chapter comes before the capability chapter for a direct reason:

$$C_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{6\hat{\sigma}_{within}} \qquad\qquad P_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{6\hat{\sigma}_{overall}}$$

If the subgroup is stratified, $\hat{\sigma}_{within}$ comes out inflated and $C_p$ comes out **underestimated** — the organization concludes the process is worse than it is, and invests where it shouldn't. If the subgroup is too narrow and there's drift between subgroups, $\hat{\sigma}_{within}$ comes out underestimated and $C_p$ comes out **optimistic**, while the real $P_p$ (what the customer feels) is much worse.

**The gap between $C_p$ and $P_p$ is, by construction, a measure of between-subgroup variation.** A $C_p / P_p$ ratio well above 1 is a diagnosis, not a detail: it means systematic variation exists between subgroups that the chart should be catching.

### Common mistakes

1. **"Let's pull 5 parts in a row every hour" without checking what those 5 parts have in common.** If they come off 5 different spindles, the subgroup is stratified.
2. **Increasing the subgroup size to "have more data."** Increasing $n$ tightens the $\bar{X}$ chart's limits (by $\sqrt{n}$), but that only helps if the subgroup stays homogeneous. A large, heterogeneous $n$ is the worst of both worlds.
3. **Recalculating limits every time there's an out-of-control point.** Limits should be calculated from a demonstrated period of stability and then **frozen**. Continuously recalculating makes the chart chase the process instead of controlling it.
4. **Using control limits equal to specification limits.** These are quantities from different origins: the specification comes from the customer, the control limits come from the process. Drawing them on the same chart invites confusion — and it's one of the reasons the next chapter insists that **SPC ≠ capability**.
5. **Ignoring time order.** If the data isn't in the actual production sequence, the chart has no meaning at all.
