## Chapter 168: Bartlett's Test: Checking the Equal-Variance Assumption

<h3 id="the-engineering-question">The engineering question</h3>
<p><em>Three operators produce, on average, exactly the same conforming-parts rate. The ANOVA from <a href="#/aula/cap-050">Chapter 50</a> finds no significant difference between means. Case closed? Not necessarily — one of the operators might have much higher variability than the other two, turning out both the best and the worst lots of the shift. The mean hides exactly that kind of problem.</em></p>
<p>ANOVA tests whether the <strong>means</strong> are equal. It doesn't test, or guarantee, that the <strong>variances</strong> are equal — in fact, it assumes that from the start. Bartlett's test checks whether that assumption holds.</p>
<h3 id="anovas-hidden-assumption">ANOVA's hidden assumption</h3>
<p>The one-way ANOVA (<a href="#/aula/cap-050">Chapter 50</a>) rests on three assumptions: normality of the residuals, independence of observations, and <strong>homogeneity of variance</strong> (homoscedasticity) across groups — the idea that, while the means may differ, the spread around each mean is the same. When that assumption fails (heteroscedasticity), the ANOVA's F-test gets distorted: the pooled standard error stops representing any of the groups well individually, and the real false-positive (or false-negative) rate drifts away from the stated nominal α.</p>
<p>The methodologically correct sequence, followed by every applied-statistics reference (for example, the NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook), is: <strong>test equality of variances first, only trust the equality-of-means test afterward.</strong></p><h3 id="bartletts-test">Bartlett's test</h3>
<p>For k groups with sample variances <span class="math-inline" data-math="s_i^2"></span> and sizes <span class="math-inline" data-math="n_i"></span>, Bartlett's statistic is:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\chi^2 = \frac{(N-k)\ln(s_p^2) - \sum_{i=1}^{k}(n_i-1)\ln(s_i^2)}{1 + \frac{1}{3(k-1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n_i-1} - \frac{1}{N-k}\right)}"></div>
<p>where <span class="math-inline" data-math="N = \sum n_i"></span> and <span class="math-inline" data-math="s_p^2"></span> is the pooled variance, weighted by each group's degrees of freedom. Under the null hypothesis of equal variances, this statistic approximately follows a chi-squared distribution with k−1 degrees of freedom. A small p-value rejects the hypothesis of equal variances.</p>
<p>In practice, nobody computes this by hand — <code>scipy.stats.bartlett</code> does the work — but understanding the formula helps you see the test's most important limitation: <strong>Bartlett is very sensitive to departures from normality.</strong> If the data within each group isn't reasonably normal, the test can flag heteroscedasticity that isn't really there (or miss it), simply because the assumed distribution doesn't hold.</p><h3 id="levene-the-robust-alternative">Levene: the robust alternative</h3>
<p>The <strong>Levene test</strong> (and its more robust variant using the median instead of the mean, sometimes called the Brown-Forsythe test) tests the same hypothesis — equal variances — but transforms the data before applying a conventional ANOVA to the absolute deviations from each group's center. That transformation makes the test far less sensitive to departures from normality than Bartlett.</p>
<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> if there's good reason to believe the data is roughly normal within each group, Bartlett is slightly more powerful (it detects smaller variance differences with the same sample). When normality is in doubt — which is most real shop-floor cases —, prefer Levene. This isn't the same test as the goodness-of-fit test in <a href="#/aula/cap-047">Chapter 47</a>: that one tests whether a single dataset follows a specific distribution; Bartlett/Levene test whether <strong>several</strong> datasets share the <strong>same</strong> variance, regardless of what the shape of the distribution actually is.</p><h3 id="unequal-variance-and-capability-readings">Unequal variance and capability readings</h3>
<p>Heterogeneous variance across groups isn't just an ANOVA technicality — it has a direct practical consequence for how <a href="#/aula/cap-071">Chapter 71</a> calculates Cp/Cpk. If a capability study pools data across several operators, shifts, or machines without first checking whether variability is homogeneous, the pooled standard deviation used in the Cp/Cpk denominator can end up dominated by the most dispersed group — artificially inflating the "process" variation and understating the real capability of each group individually. Worse still: if the rational subgroup (<a href="#/aula/cap-065">Chapter 65</a>) mixes, deliberately or by accident, sources of variation with different spreads, the resulting Cpk stops corresponding to any real production scenario — it's not the good operator's Cpk, nor the problem operator's, it's an average with no direct physical meaning.</p>
<p>So before reporting an aggregate Cp/Cpk across multiple operators, shifts, or machines, it's worth running Bartlett or Levene on the groups making up the aggregate. If the equal-variance hypothesis is rejected, the right move isn't to "fix" the test — it's to report Cpk separately by group, and treat the difference in variability as a process finding in its own right, exactly as in the operator-C example above.</p><h3 id="worked-example-equal-means-different-variances">Worked example: equal means, different variances</h3><div class="codetabs"><div class="ct-bar" role="tablist"><button type="button" class="ct-tab on" role="tab" aria-selected="true" id="ctc1795d8c-t0">Python</button><button type="button" class="ct-tab" role="tab" aria-selected="false" id="ctc1795d8c-t1">R</button></div><div class="ct-pane" role="tabpanel" aria-labelledby="ctc1795d8c-t0"><div class="codeblock" data-lang="python"><div class="codebar"><span class="lang">Python</span><button class="btn-copy" type="button">Copy</button></div><pre><code class="language-python">import numpy as np

<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Running ANOVA without checking variance at all.</strong> This is the most common practice, and the riskiest one when group sample sizes are unequal — ANOVA is more sensitive to heteroscedasticity the more unequal the per-group n's are.</li>
<li><strong>Using Bartlett blindly on clearly non-normal data.</strong> In that case, the test can flag variance heterogeneity that's actually an artifact of the distribution's shape — prefer Levene.</li>
<li><strong>Confusing this test with the goodness-of-fit test.</strong> Bartlett/Levene compare variances <em>across</em> groups; they say nothing about whether any single group, on its own, is normal — see <a href="#/aula/cap-047">Chapter 47</a> for that.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring a significant Bartlett/Levene result just because the means ANOVA "passed."</strong> Uncontrolled variance is, by itself, a process problem — even when the mean is on target, as the operator-C example shows.</li>
</ol>
