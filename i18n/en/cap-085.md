## Chapter 85: Matrix Diagram and Prioritization Matrix

### What a Matrix Diagram Is

The matrix diagram's purpose is helping people discover, visualize, and communicate relationships within a single set of factors or between two or more sets of factors. In practice, it's generally used to display relationships between two sets of characteristics — for example, between customer needs and a product's or process's technical characteristics — though it can also be used to show interrelationships within a single set of factors, in which case the same items appear in both the rows and the columns.

The typical layout is two-dimensional: one set of factors runs down the vertical dimension and the other along the horizontal, forming a grid. In each grid cell, whether and how strongly the two factors intersecting at that point are related gets documented — generally qualitatively, with symbols. It's common to use a small set of symbols to represent different relationship strengths, like a filled circle (strong relationship), an open circle (moderate relationship), and a triangle (weak relationship), plus "+" and "–" signs to indicate positive and negative relationships among the factors within the same set, when those also appear in the matrix's triangular appendices.

The best-known application of the matrix diagram in quality work is quality function deployment (QFD) and its well-known "house of quality." In that format, customer demands (in the customer's language) go in the rows, technical characteristics (in the producer's language) go in the columns, and the matrix's triangular "roof" shows the interrelationships among the technical characteristics themselves — whether improving one hurts or helps another. Additional columns can carry each demand's importance to the customer and a competitive assessment against rivals, letting priority scores be calculated for each technical characteristic.

### When to Use It

The matrix diagram should be used whenever the team needs to systematically relate two (or more) sets of items — for example, customer needs versus technical specifications, potential causes versus processes, or requirements versus owners — and when that relationship isn't obvious or is often a source of disagreement among process experts. It's especially valuable because it forces the team to make explicit what each person "thinks" matters, revealing gaps in process knowledge and serving as a basis for control plans, setup procedures, and later QFD deployments.

### How to Build a Matrix Diagram, Step by Step

1. **List the first set of factors** (for example, customer demands) along the matrix's vertical axis.
2. **List the second set of factors** (for example, technical characteristics) along the horizontal axis.
3. **Identify items that function simultaneously as input and output.** This is normal and doesn't invalidate the matrix.
4. **Assess each intersecting cell**, rating the relationship between the corresponding row and column as critical (strong), moderate (weak), or nonexistent (none).
5. **Log the relationship with a standardized symbol or letter**, so anyone at the organization can read the matrix unambiguously.
6. **Quantify when applicable.** Multiply the first set's relative weight for each factor (for example, how much importance the customer places on each demand) by the numeric value tied to the relationship symbol, summing by column to get a priority score for each item in the second set.

### Practical Example — Prioritization Matrix — Matrix Diagram

A commercial laundry wants to translate its customers' demands ("clean clothes," "clothes that look good," "fast service," "friendly service," "return of items found in pockets") into measurable technical process characteristics, like wash-cycle time, thoroughness of pocket inspection, front-desk staff training, and machine location. When building the matrix, the team assigns relative weights to each customer demand (for example, "clean clothes" gets a weight of 7, "friendly service" gets a weight of 3), then rates the strength of the relationship between each demand and each technical characteristic using the strong, moderate, and weak relationship symbols. Multiplying weight by relationship value and summing by column, the team gets priority scores for each technical characteristic — revealing, for example, that "wash-cycle time" and "pocket inspection" account for most of the score and should be the first characteristics to get improvement attention.

### What a Prioritization Matrix Is

Once relationships and alternatives have been mapped out — whether through a matrix diagram, a tree diagram, or any other analysis technique — the team enters "decision mode." This is where the prioritization matrix comes in, whose purpose is helping people measure and evaluate relationships coming from a matrix or a tree analysis, against a weighting scheme and decision criteria, in order to establish implementation priorities. The prioritization matrix lets you make relative comparisons and present the information in an organized way, so decisions rest on consistent, objective, quantitative assessment — rather than being made by "shouting it out" in the meeting.

Every prioritization requires two ingredients: (1) decision criteria, which flow from the organization's sense of what matters (cost, timeline, technical performance, customer service, etc.) and need to be weighted for their relative importance; and (2) a means of structuring the relative comparisons between alternatives.

### How to Build a Prioritization Matrix, Step by Step

1. **Define the decision criteria** that will be used to assess the alternatives (for example, rollout cost, customer impact, timeline, risk).
2. **Weight each criterion's relative importance.** This can be done subjectively, through group consensus and voting techniques, like the Delphi method or the nominal group technique, or objectively, through the analytic hierarchy process (AHP), which lets several decision-makers combine their priorities through pairwise comparisons, generating a priority matrix for the decision criteria.
3. **List the candidate alternatives or solutions** to be compared.
4. **Assess each alternative against each criterion**, assigning a relative score.
5. **Calculate each alternative's combined score**, multiplying the score obtained on each criterion by that criterion's weight and summing the results — arriving at a weighted priority matrix of the alternatives.
6. **Set a cutoff criterion** (a minimum score) to decide which alternatives move forward to implementation and which get dropped or deferred.

### Practical Example

Picking back up the laundry's matrix diagram example: after identifying that "wash-cycle time" and "pocket inspection" are the most critical technical characteristics, management has three candidate initiatives for improving them — buying faster machines, redesigning the pocket-inspection procedure, and training the staff. Using a prioritization matrix with criteria like rollout cost, expected impact on customer satisfaction, and speed of implementation (each weighted according to business strategy), the team scores the three initiatives, multiplies by the criteria weights, and sums the scores. A total of 60 points (previously set as the cutoff criterion) is used to select which initiatives enter the immediate action plan, giving the decision a quantitative basis and reducing the influence of personal preferences.
