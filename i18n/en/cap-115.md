## Chapter 115: Six Sigma in Hospitality and Tourism

### Benefits of Six Sigma in a Hospitality and Tourism Setting

Hospitality and tourism organizations typically walk a very fine line between productivity and customer satisfaction, and where that line sits depends on the type and level of service provided. Hotels geared toward business travelers, for example, need to provide efficient, hassle-free service, with enough amenities to keep the business traveler comfortable. Too many amenities can push the service out of the affordable price range for most business travelers; too little comfort, and the business traveler goes elsewhere. Luxury resorts walk a different line, as do discount services that attract vacationing families with bundled pricing packages.

To walk the most profitable line appropriately, hospitality and tourism organizations need to stay attuned to what their target customer needs and wants. Six Sigma helps organizations do this through tools like Voice of the Customer and statistical analysis. One reason customer satisfaction is critical for hospitality and tourism organizations is that many companies in this sector depend on customer loyalty to drive revenue. From hotels to theme parks, hospitality and tourism organizations offer loyalty programs and rewards for a reason: they want repeat customers. Hospitality and tourism organizations also rely heavily on social media and online reviews, to make sure new customers are more likely to choose their service or location. Employing Six Sigma — and statistical process control — lets hospitality and tourism organizations create consistent quality service, so customer-satisfaction wins can be repeated.

Just like manufacturing organizations, hospitality and tourism companies need to keep an eye on inventory. Too much inventory increases costs and contributes to waste, and neither is good for profits. Too little inventory leaves the customer without what they need, which can result in low customer satisfaction, lost repeat business, and bad word-of-mouth marketing. Recall the Just in Time, or JIT, manufacturing principle from the Lean chapter in PART I: this principle matters a great deal for hospitality and tourism. A hotel guest doesn't want excess towels taking up all the bathroom space, but they do want clean towels when they need them. Using Six Sigma and Lean methods lets hospitality organizations meet customer needs in a timely, cost-effective way.

### Challenges of Implementing Six Sigma in a Hospitality and Tourism Setting

Possibly the biggest challenge Six Sigma practitioners face in the hospitality and tourism sector is the large role played by people outside the organization, and how much control those people seem to be able to exert. Hospitality and tourism is a people-facing service, and there's no success in the sector without a lot of people walking through your door or using your service. Six Sigma practitioners can't always expect customers to conform to guidelines, rules, or controls, which means there's always an inherent uncertainty in a hospitality and tourism process.

This is true, to some degree, in almost any sector. Certainly in a healthcare setting, the patient is at least partly responsible for outcomes in some processes. If the patient doesn't follow discharge instructions, the treatment outcome may differ. If the patient doesn't provide information to the facility on time, billing may be affected. In many other sectors, including healthcare, staff exert greater authority because of the weight of the situation, their credentials, or a response ingrained by culture. In general, people are more likely to listen and respond to instructions given by a doctor than by, say, someone checking tickets at a theme park. When designing or improving processes, Six Sigma practitioners must keep in mind the fact that people are so involved in the process.

Another challenge Six Sigma practitioners may face in the hospitality and tourism sector is that many processes are seasonal. While successful Six Sigma projects can indeed reduce the problems associated with inventory, staffing, and running a seasonal operation, having to think in seasonal scope can be difficult for Six Sigma practitioners used to applying the methodology to longer-running processes.

Turnover rates at hospitality and tourism organizations, and the level at which many of these organizations hire, can present another challenge for Six Sigma practitioners. First, with high turnover rates at every level of the sector, it can be hard to get enough support to sustain Six Sigma initiatives. Once support at leadership levels is secured, a change in the executive or middle-management guard can impact that support, leaving Six Sigma practitioners within the organization constantly working to educate leaders on the importance and effectiveness of the methodology. Second, constant turnover in the lower ranks of staff can mean an ongoing need for retraining. This is actually something Six Sigma and Lean methodology can address, by making it easier to control processes regardless of who's involved, but turnover is something Six Sigma practitioners and teams must always keep in mind.

### Six Sigma Hospitality Case Study: The Starwood Hotels & Resorts Example

The Starwood Hotels and Resorts chain was reportedly one of the first hospitality organizations to adopt Six Sigma's change-management and continuous-process-improvement methodology, and certainly one of the first to do so at the corporate and global level. Among other brands, Starwood owned the Westin and Sheraton hotel names, and the organization wanted to differentiate these chains from others in the market. Starting in the early part of this century, Starwood leveraged Six Sigma tools to drive efficiencies and improvements — all without impacting the signature creativity underpinning the brand's design and hospitality elements.

The need to protect the brand's creativity was an important factor in how the hotel chain chose to implement Six Sigma — and one reason many people in the industry were surprised to see Starwood choose Six Sigma over what were considered more traditional hospitality approaches. In the latter part of the 20th century, Six Sigma gained a reputation for being somewhat "rigid," though that reputation was unfair and perpetuated by people who didn't fully understand how the methodology works. Professionals in sectors outside manufacturing believed Six Sigma and Lean methods were about making everything the same to boost productivity, which they believed would naturally diminish creativity.

Hopefully, by studying the various components and tools of Six Sigma, you've realized this is far from true. Six Sigma, when implemented correctly, actually facilitates creativity, by involving everyone in improvements and fostering out-of-the-box thinking. The goal isn't to stop people from thinking creatively, but to combine creative approaches with data, to make sure final decisions are more likely to work as intended.

One reason Starwood integrated Six Sigma into its processes was to increase its ability to drive creativity across the organization and to repeat the results of successful programs. One example of the Six Sigma approach used by Starwood can be seen by reviewing a 2006 initiative called Unwind.

Unwind was an initiative Starwood launched to engage travelers within its upscale hotels. While the project itself was definitely creative in nature, the Starwood team chose it after reviewing industry trends, customer needs, and other data. Specifically, an industry study indicated that travelers often feel lonely because they're away from friends and family back home. Roughly a third of travelers expressed this sentiment, and Starwood saw a good opportunity to align its own wants with a real customer need.

As stated earlier, customer loyalty and engagement are critical in the hospitality and tourism sector, and Starwood believed it could increase customer loyalty to its brand by providing solutions for these feelings of loneliness. The organization used Six Sigma to design and implement solutions — and those solutions weren't always the same for every local hotel. Teams that used Six Sigma appropriately recognized that every hotel — all in different locations around the country — serves customers with very different wants and needs. What works for vacationers at a tropical resort won't work the same way for business travelers in Chicago, and Starwood's leaders know that. It wasn't about making everything the same — it was about making everything better.

As part of its Six Sigma approach, Starwood trained hundreds of black belts and green belts. These Six Sigma practitioners led teams around the world, constantly working out ideas for improvements and new programs. Teams had to pitch ideas to company leadership, and only those that met certain criteria received approval, because resources — even for a large upscale hotel chain — are never unlimited.

One result of Starwood's Unwind initiative was a complimentary massage offer for travelers. The suggestion came about after a black belt, a few green belts, and subject-matter experts at a Chicago hotel got together to brainstorm. The fitness director suggested massages, and everyone liked the idea. The Six Sigma team launched a project to pilot a complimentary massage offer. The pilot was so successful that teams had to figure out how to solve a new problem: how could hotels tactfully communicate that the complimentary massage time was limited? Customers wanted more time in the massage chairs!

Working through Six Sigma processes, the team resolved these issues and later rolled the program out to many hotel locations. The project was a big success — not only did customer satisfaction and loyalty increase, but massage-related revenue rose 30 percent at locations where hotels offered paid spa services. Apparently, customers agreed with Starwood: a massage was exactly what a traveler needed.

Massages weren't the only amenity generated by the Unwind initiative. Across hundreds of hotel locations, the Starwood team designed, piloted, and rolled out 120 amenities to engage customers and increase loyalty. Amenities ranged from additional perks, like massages, to entertainment, like Chinese water-painting classes and fire dancing. Amenities and entertainment were matched to the look, theme, atmosphere, and purpose of various hotels, to better meet the needs of the customers staying at those properties.

Starwood's approach of creative innovation through Six Sigma reportedly brought big returns. In a single year, the company claims to have seen an additional $100 million in profits, thanks to new programs designed and implemented using Six Sigma.

### Tips for Using Six Sigma in Hospitality and Tourism

To succeed with Six Sigma in the hospitality and tourism sector, Six Sigma teams and practitioners should:

- Be able to account for the uncertainty people bring to every process, and know how to mitigate that uncertainty with Six Sigma and Lean tools
- Understand the seasonal nature of the sector, and factor trends in when developing processes and projects
- Be able to skillfully convey Six Sigma principles in a way that's relevant to hospitality and tourism professionals
