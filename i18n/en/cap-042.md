## Chapter 42: After the Project: Quality Katas, Coaching, and the Digitalization of Continuous Improvement

### The problem with the "belt" system

The author draws an analogy with martial arts to question Lean Six Sigma's traditional system of green belts, black belts, and the like. In martial arts, each belt corresponds to specific katas — standardized sequences of movements that demonstrate, before a qualified instructor, that the practitioner genuinely masters certain skills. A black belt in karate represents dozens of katas mastered and individually assessed. In the more common Lean Six Sigma certification programs, by contrast, the candidate goes through one to three weeks of training covering theory and tools, "demonstrates" skills with one or two projects, and receives a belt that says little about what that person is actually capable of doing in practice — hence the situation, familiar to many managers, of asking a certified "Black Belt" about their practical experience and hearing something generic in reply, like "I'm more of a Lean person."

### Quality Katas: certifying concrete skills, not generic theory

The proposed alternative is creating "quality katas": not tools, but concrete, verifiable actions, specific to each of the four paths. A few example katas suggested by the author:

- **Reducing Variability:** create a process map; determine a process's natural variability; evaluate a continuous measurement system; evaluate an attribute measurement system; deploy a control chart; create a special-cause reaction tree; conduct a "fit-for-use" specification study.
- **Reducing Failures:** create a process map; improve MTBF (mean time between failures); reduce MTTR (mean time to repair); implement predictive tools; perform an asset utilization loss analysis; reduce a changeover/conversion's duration; identify and mitigate failure-mode risk.
- **Reducing Waste:** run a "sort" event (from 5S); implement a kanban; eliminate sources of deterioration; implement centerlining on a process; run an SMED event.
- **Reducing Defects:** perform a Pareto analysis; compare two samples; compare several samples; eliminate potential errors (poka-yoke); run a structured brainstorming session.

The logic is simple: instead of "covering" a huge vocabulary of tools superficially, people are trained and certified in specific katas, which any manager can concretely verify. If the organization wants to keep the belt language, it can redefine the criteria — mastering a few katas becomes green belt, mastering all of them becomes black belt, teaching the katas to others becomes master black belt — but the author suggests going further and dropping the belt idea altogether in favor of something more like badges displayed on each employee's profile, making visible exactly what each person knows how to do, not just which classes they attended.

### Putting the idea into practice: the six steps of "doing"

The author proposes six steps for implementing this way of working within the organization:

1. **Identify the paths that matter for the business**, considering the nature of the operation (high or low speed, continuous or batch, automated or manual, tight or wide specifications, high or low changeover frequency).
2. **Determine each path's katas**, adapting or creating specific mini-paths when the organization already has relevant in-house expertise.
3. **Assess the team's current skills** to deliver those katas, using a skills matrix that maps job titles/roles to competencies, with levels (for example, 1 = aware, 2 = proficient, 3 = expert) that make training gaps explicit.
4. **Develop or redesign training** in the critical paths and katas, moving from long, generic training toward a just-in-time (JIT), agile training model: teaching specific, practical skills (visualizing variability with a histogram, showing relationships with a scatter plot, determining whether two means differ, doing a short-term capability study, creating a Gantt chart) instead of covering a list of tools abstractly. Training should be visual, hands-on, and practical — the shift from "knowing the concept" to "knowing how to apply it" rarely happens just by watching slides; it requires repetition and real practice on the shop floor.
5. **Create a list of specific items to improve**, always tied to the unit's strategic needs — never chosen by the trainees themselves. The author stresses this point strongly: continuous improvement projects disconnected from the business's key performance indicators (KPIs) tend to lose energy and support along the way.
6. **Run the projects using the right path**, which drastically cuts the typical project size and duration — from nine-to-twelve-month cycles down to efforts of days or weeks, making it possible to celebrate wins far more often.

### Chartering

A common mistake, according to the author, is asking the belt candidate or team leader themselves to write their own project charter — choosing the problem, the SMART goal, the team, the metric, and the expected savings before even the first week of training is over. This intimidates people and produces "safe," low-relevance projects. The recommendation is that the charter be leadership's responsibility, tied to the unit's strategic plan and to the gaps identified in the periodic review of performance indicators against targets. A good chartering system should include: the path (strategy) the team will follow; the team's objectives; how those objectives connect to the unit's priorities; the timeline (which should be short); the team's leader and coach; the suggested members; an assessment of the members' skills/training; existing background information (lessons learned, current input/output matrix, etc.); and the team's kick-off meeting. It's important to balance strategic improvement projects (which pursue bigger, but rarer, performance leaps) with a larger volume of incremental, tactical improvements — it's often the sum of these small, continuous improvements that builds a genuine CI culture over time.

### Roles and coaching

Sustaining a team-based improvement program requires clearly defined roles: the **process owner**, responsible for making sure all the team systems are working, selecting goals aligned with the unit's priorities, and assigning coaches and team leaders; the **process leader**, responsible for maintaining the team systems and providing technical expertise in chartering, strategy selection, training, communication, team reviews, and recognition; the **coach**, a member of management directly accountable for a specific team's success, who should know at any given moment what stage the team is at, what barriers it faces, and act to remove them; the **team leader**, responsible for running meetings, keeping the focus on objectives, tracking assignments, and updating the team's storyboard; and the **team member**, responsible for applying the chosen strategy's methods and tools, participating in meetings, using data to make decisions, and following through on commitments made.

### Communication, recognition, and reviews

Communication and recognition systems exist to increase the visibility of teams' activities and results and to positively reinforce the improvement process. Suggested communication channels include storyboards, plant video systems, newsletters, and shift meetings; forms of recognition include mentions at key meetings, "walls of fame," letters sent to employees' homes, individual gifts (t-shirts, plaques), and plant-level celebrations. It's also important to recognize employees who aren't formally part of the CI teams but who make them possible by covering shifts during meetings and helping with data collection.

Finally, there should be a formal system for reviewing teams' progress and results, with different levels and frequencies of informal and formal reviews. As a minimum benchmark, each team's performance should be formally reviewed by management at least once a quarter (newer teams may need more frequent reviews), checking whether the teams are focused on their mission, following the chosen strategy, measuring success correctly, using the available communication system, and whether they need help. Records of these reviews should be kept and used as input for the following reviews.

### Digitalizing continuous improvement

The author closes the book by observing that the digital era is transforming how continuous improvement is carried out, and that the logic of paths and katas fits perfectly into that wave. Each path — or even each individual kata — can be made available in digital form, accessible to anyone via tablet or smartphone, as a kind of "maps app" (the author draws the analogy with Google Maps) that guides the user step by step down the right path through the DMAIC "forest." Each path's checklists, once filled out on paper, can be converted into digital forms, which lets teams' progress be tracked in real time, help be requested on demand, and data be captured and shared instantly with other projects and other units across the organization.

This approach also fits naturally into the "pillar" logic many organizations use to structure their operational excellence systems (continuous improvement, autonomous maintenance, planned maintenance, talent management, standardized work, quality, safety/environment, office), all underpinned by a leadership foundation and organized around shared goals and key indicators. Within that structure, this chapter's content — paths, katas, chartering, roles, training, and reviews — can, on its own, become the organization's entire continuous improvement pillar content, also serving as a starting point for its broader digital transformation.
