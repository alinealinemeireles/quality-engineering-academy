## Chapter 148: Resilience, Continuity, and Disruption Risk ✦

### The engineering question

> *"Our single-source supplier of the critical component had a fire. We have 11 days of stock and
> qualifying an alternative takes 14 weeks. What does this cost, and what should we have done
> beforehand?"*

Quality risk management has to include **disruptions**, because a disruption stops the organization
from supplying conforming product — which is precisely the management system's objective. ISO
22301:2019 provides the business continuity framework; this chapter covers its quantitative
application to industrial risk.

---

## 148.1 BIA: Business Impact Analysis

The BIA answers three questions, per activity:

1. **What's the impact over time if this activity stops?** — and the impact is rarely linear. It
   usually has thresholds: day 1 no impact (stock), day 3 delays, day 7 contractual penalties, day
   14 loss of customer.
2. **What's the maximum tolerable period of disruption (MTPD)?** — the point beyond which the damage
   becomes irreversible.
3. **What does this activity depend on?** — people, equipment, systems, suppliers, data,
   infrastructure.

**RTO (Recovery Time Objective)** — the target time to resume the activity. It must be **shorter**
than the MTPD, with margin.

**RPO (Recovery Point Objective)** — the maximum amount of data acceptable to lose, measured in
time. An RPO of 24 h means daily backup; an RPO of 15 min requires replication.

> **For quality and traceability data, the RPO is frequently underestimated.** Losing 24 h of
> traceability records can mean being unable to scope a recall — and having to recall everything
> instead of one lot. The cost of a wrong RPO isn't the cost of the data; it's the cost of the
> expanded recall.

---

## 148.2 The impact curve and the investment decision

The most common mistake is assuming impact is linear over time. Reality has discontinuities, and
that's where the decision lives:

| Downtime | Mechanism | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| 0-1 day | Absorbed by stock and buffer | ≈ 0 |
| 2-5 days | Delivery delays; overtime to catch up | Moderate, recoverable |
| 6-14 days | Contractual penalties; customer's line stops | High |
| > 14 days | Loss of nomination; customer qualifies an alternative | **Irreversible** |

The resilience investment decision is settled by comparing the **cost of the recovery capability**
(stock, second supplier, redundancy, priority contract) with the **expected loss avoided** —
integrated over the distribution of disruption durations.

---

## 148.3 Resilience strategies

| Strategy | Cost | Activation time | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Safety stock** | Tied-up capital + obsolescence | Immediate | Simple; protects against short durations |
| **Qualified second supplier** | Qualification + minimum volume | Days | Protects against long durations |
| **Identified alternative supplier (unqualified)** | Low | Weeks | Reduces the time, doesn't eliminate it |
| **Internal redundancy** | High capex | Immediate | Only for the truly critical |
| **Priority contract / reserved capacity** | Contractual premium | Days | Frequently undervalued |
| **Redesign for a common component** | Engineering | Months | The structural solution |

**The optimal combination is almost always hybrid:** stock covers the short term while the second
supplier ramps up. Analyzing each strategy in isolation systematically leads to over-investing in
one while leaving a window uncovered.

---

## 148.4 What to test, and why tests fail

An untested continuity plan is a hypothesis, not a plan.

| Test type | Effort | What it validates |
|---|---|---|
| Document review | Low | Plan coherence |
| *Tabletop* (in-room simulation) | Medium | Decisions, communication, roles |
| Partial functional test | High | One component (e.g., backup restore) |
| Full simulation | Very high | Everything, including what no one foresaw |

**The most underrated and most revealing test: restoring a backup.** Having a backup isn't the same
as having recovery capability. The failure rate of restores that were never tested is notoriously
high — and it's discovered at the worst possible moment.

---

## 148.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Assuming linear impact over time | Poorly sized investment | Model thresholds and the MTPD |
| RTO set without a BIA | An arbitrary target | Derive it from the real impact |
| RPO ignored for quality data | Recall can't be scoped | Set RPO by consequence |
| Backup never restored | Recovery fails in the crisis | Test the restore periodically |
| Tier-2 dependencies not mapped | Two suppliers, one sub-supplier | Map upstream |
| Plan never tested | Roles and decisions undefined in the crisis | Tabletop at least annually |
| Analyzing strategies in isolation | Uncovered window or over-investment | Evaluate combinations |

# (2) Distribuicao de duracoes de interrupcao e perda esperada

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(148)
N_SIM = 200_000

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (1)
print("=" * 78)
print("(1) BIA  -  curva de impacto de uma paragem da linha principal")
print("=" * 78)
patamares = [
    (0,   1,        0, "Absorvido por stock de produto acabado"),
    (1,   3,   25_000, "Atrasos; horas extra para recuperar"),
    (3,   7,   90_000, "Penalizacoes contratuais de OTIF"),
    (7,  14,  260_000, "Paragem da linha do cliente; penalizacao agravada"),
    (14, 30,  600_000, "Cliente ativa fornecedor alternativo"),
    (30, 999, 900_000, "Perda de nomeacao; dano reputacional"),
]
print(f"  {'dias':>10}{'custo/dia (EUR)':>18}   mecanismo")
print("  " + "-" * 78)
for d0, d1, c, mec in patamares:
    faixa = f"{d0}-{d1}" if d1 < 900 else f">{d0}"
    print(f"  {faixa:>10}{c:>18,.0f}   {mec}")
print("  " + "-" * 78)

def impacto(dias):
    """Custo acumulado de uma paragem de 'dias' dias (vetorizado)."""
    dias = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(dias, dtype=float))
    total = np.zeros_like(dias)
    for d0, d1, c, _ in patamares:

    return total

print(f"\n  {'paragem':>10}{'custo acumulado':>20}{'custo medio/dia':>20}")

print(f"  RTO deve ser definido com margem: RTO alvo = {int(MTPD*0.6)} dias.")

print("\n" + "=" * 78)

print("=" * 78)

sg_d, mu_d = 0.9, np.log(5.0)

perda_anual = np.zeros(N_SIM)

estrategias = {
    "Nenhuma (situacao atual)":        (0,       0,  0.0),
    "Stock +10 dias":                  (85_000, 10,  0.0),
    "Stock +20 dias":                  (170_000, 20, 0.0),
    "2o fornecedor qualificado":       (120_000,  3,  0.55),
    "Contrato de prioridade":          (45_000,   0,  0.30),
    "Stock +10d + 2o fornecedor":      (205_000, 10,  0.55),
    "Stock +10d + prioridade":         (130_000, 10,  0.30),
}

def perda_com(cobertura_dias, reducao, n_sim=N_SIM, rng=rng):
    n = rng.poisson(LAMBDA, n_sim); mx = n.max()
    if mx == 0:
        return np.zeros(n_sim)
    d = rng.lognormal(mu_d, sg_d, (n_sim, mx))
    d = d * (1 - reducao)                      # 2o fornecedor encurta a interrupcao
    d = np.clip(d - cobertura_dias, 0, None)   # stock absorve os primeiros dias
    mask = np.arange(mx)[None, :] < n[:, None]
    return (impacto(d.ravel()).reshape(d.shape) * mask).sum(axis=1)

print("     Varias combinacoes levam o P95 a ZERO.")
print("\n  3. A combinacao 'stock + 2o fornecedor' custa ~30% mais em valor")
print("     esperado do que o 2o fornecedor sozinho, e em troca elimina a cauda.")
print("     E um PREMIO DE SEGURO explicito, e a decisao de o pagar depende do")
print("     apetite de risco declarado (cap. 143) - nao da media.")
print("\n  Nenhuma estrategia isolada cobre as duas janelas temporais: o stock")
print("  protege os primeiros dias, o 2o fornecedor encurta a duracao total.")
print("  Avaliar cada uma separadamente, pela media, leva a escolher a que")

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — BIA of a critical line.**
*Build the impact curve and determine the MTPD.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The cell builds the curve as thresholds. The method:

1. **Identify the impact mechanisms**, rather than estimating a daily cost. Each mechanism has its
   own activation point: stock runs out, contractual penalty kicks in, customer stops, customer
   qualifies an alternative.
2. **Date each mechanism** — when it starts acting. It comes from the contracts, the actual stock
   levels, and the customer's awareness — not from a guess.
3. **Quantify the daily cost** within each threshold band.
4. **Identify the MTPD** — the point beyond which the damage is irreversible. In the example, the
   14 days at which the customer qualifies an alternative: from there on, resuming production
   doesn't recover the business.
5. **Set the RTO with margin** — 60% of the MTPD is a defensible rule of thumb.

**The three mistakes this exercise exposes:**

- **Assuming linearity.** A 30-day stoppage doesn't cost 30 times a 1-day one. The average daily
  cost grows, and linear analysis systematically undersizes the resilience investment.
- **Forgetting irreversibility.** The MTPD isn't where the cost gets high — it's where it stops
  being recoverable. They're different concepts, and the second one is what decides.
- **Not involving sales and legal.** The thresholds come from the contracts and the customer
  relationship, not from engineering. A BIA done by production alone misses the thresholds that
  matter.

</details>

**E2 — RTO and RPO for quality data.**
*Determine RTO and RPO for the traceability system.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**RTO — how long can the system be unavailable?**

Depends on what gets blocked. If shipping can't happen without the system (because the certificate
of conformance can't be issued), the RTO is the time until shipping meaningfully stops — typically
**4 to 8 hours**.

**RPO — how much data can be lost?**

Here's the calculation the cell performs, and the answer surprises anyone who only thinks in terms
of "the cost of the data."

The consequence of losing traceability data isn't losing records. It's **being unable to scope a
recall**. Without traceability for the lost window, you recall **everything** produced in that
window instead of the affected lot.

At 420 parts/hour and €38/part recall cost:

| RPO | Parts without traceability | Cost if a recall occurs |
|---|---|---|
| 24 h | 10,080 | €383,040 |
| 8 h | 3,360 | €127,680 |
| 1 h | 420 | €15,960 |
| 15 min | 105 | €3,990 |

With P(recall) = 6%/year, the expected annual exposure drops from ~€23,000 (daily backup) to ~€240
(continuous replication). The incremental cost of replication is typically a few thousand euros a
year.

**Recommendation:** RPO ≤ 1 h for traceability data, via replication. It's among the best-return
decisions in data risk management — and one of the least taken, because the calculation is rarely
done.

**Important note:** RPO and RTO are **different** requirements and can have different solutions. A
tight RPO requires replication; a tight RTO requires a standby system ready to take over. Having one
without the other leaves a gap.

</details>

**E3 — Supplier disruption.**
*Model a 14-day disruption and compare treatment alternatives.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The cell compares seven strategies, including combinations, and the result carries three lessons:

**1. Mean and tail rank differently — and this is the central result.** By total expected cost, the
second supplier alone wins (it attacks the duration, which is what generates the most expensive
thresholds). By the tail (P95), stock-based strategies are far superior: stock completely eliminates
short disruptions, which are the majority, and several combinations drive P95 to **zero**.

**2. The combination is an explicit insurance premium.** "Stock + second supplier" costs ~30% more
in expected value and in exchange eliminates the tail. Paying that premium is a legitimate decision
— but it has to be **declared** based on the stated risk appetite (Chapter 143), not made implicitly.
Always choosing by the mean means choosing to leave the tail uncovered.

**3. The priority contract is undervalued.** It costs a fraction of the second supplier and
materially reduces the expected duration. It's often the best-return option, and rarely considered
because it isn't a visible investment.

**The three checks before deciding:**

- **Tier-2 dependencies.** Do the two suppliers buy from the same raw-material producer? Are they in
  the same region subject to the same natural risk? Apparent diversification can be illusory — it's
  the same common-cause problem from Chapter 146.
- **Real activation time.** A "qualified" second supplier that hasn't produced in 18 months needs
  re-qualification. Activation time should be tested, not assumed.
- **Stock obsolescence.** Safety stock of a component subject to an engineering change can become
  scrap. The real cost includes that risk.

</details>

**E4 — Integration with the quality system.**
*How does continuity connect to the QMS and the contingency plan?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Continuity isn't a parallel system. It connects to the QMS at six concrete points:

**1. Context and interested parties.** Critical suppliers, infrastructure dependencies, and
customer continuity requirements are inputs to the context analysis.

**2. Risks and opportunities.** Disruption risks go into the same register as all the others, with
the same taxonomy, the same owner, and the same tolerance criteria. A separate register for
continuity duplicates effort and misaligns criteria.

**3. Control of externally provided processes.** Supplier evaluation and selection criteria should
include continuity capability — a documented, tested plan, with declared tier-2 dependencies.
Evaluating a supplier only on PPM and OTIF ignores the most expensive risk.

**4. Operational contingency plan.** Distinct from the strategic continuity plan. It answers: who
decides to stop shipping? which product can be diverted? how is the customer informed, and within
what timeframe? who has the authority to activate the alternative supplier?

**5. Change control.** Activating an alternative supplier is a process change. It requires
verification (PPAP or equivalent, even if accelerated) **before** the product ships. A crisis
doesn't suspend quality requirements — and decisions made under pressure are exactly the ones the
next audit will scrutinize.

**6. Improvement.** Every continuity test and every real disruption produces findings that feed the
corrective action process, with the same root-cause discipline.

**The element that's most often missing, and that decides in a crisis:** **pre-defined decision
authority**. In a crisis, what stalls the response isn't the lack of a plan — it's no one knowing who
can decide to divert production, activate an unqualified supplier, or inform the customer. That gets
defined beforehand, in writing, and tested in a tabletop.

</details>
