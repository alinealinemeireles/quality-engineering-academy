## Chapter 65: Product and Process Control Methods

### The control plan

The control plan is the central document that brings together, in summary, practical form, everything needed to monitor and control a manufacturing process. It consolidates information from various sources — design, process engineering, risk analyses — into a single quick-reference document, generally used directly on the shop floor. The control plan's format isn't rigidly standardized; conventional spreadsheets are perfectly acceptable, as long as they contain, at minimum, the following elements:

- Station or operation number and a description of the process performed at that point.
- Machines, equipment, or fixtures involved.
- Reference drawing numbers.
- The product or process characteristic to be controlled, including its tolerances.
- The evaluation method used (gages, sensors, visual inspection, etc.).
- Sample size and frequency.
- The control method (for example, x-bar and R control chart, check sheet, go/no-go gage, poka-yoke).
- The reaction plan to follow when the control method detects a problem.

The control plan doesn't come from nowhere: it's the final link in a chain that starts with the design failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) and flows into the process FMEA. Failure modes that can't be prevented through design get carried over to the process FMEA; some of those modes are prevented with poka-yoke devices or have their frequency reduced through designed experiments that optimize the process, while others, despite the best efforts, remain with unacceptable risk priority numbers (RPN) and need additional process controls. The control plan should be checked to make sure every critical and significant characteristic identified in the design and process FMEAs is actually covered by it.

An equally important point is that, by the time the control plan takes effect, every nondestructive measurement system listed in it should have already successfully passed the gage repeatability and reproducibility (gage R&R) requirement — a topic covered in another unit of this manual, dedicated to measurement system analysis. Sample sizes and frequencies should rest on statistically sound principles, always keeping in mind that sampling needs to be frequent enough to allow suspect product to be contained before it ships to the customer. It's up to the QE to select the control method best suited to the characteristic being monitored, balancing cost, response time, and detection capability.

### The reaction plan

Perhaps the most important — and most often poorly executed — aspect of the control plan methodology is the reaction plan. The reaction plan lists the steps the operator should follow when the control method indicates a problem. For example: what should happen when an X-bar chart goes out of control? Unfortunately, many references and training programs don't properly develop this concept, sticking to vague instructions like "adjust and recheck" or "recalibrate and reverify." Simplistic instructions like these can lead to process violation (overadjustment) and, on top of that, waste opportunities for permanent corrective action.

A good reaction plan addresses four critical elements:

1. **Containment.** As soon as the problem is identified, suspect product should be quarantined and segregated. This might include everything produced since the last acceptable sample. A good first-in-first-out (FIFO) inventory management system greatly simplifies the containment task, when needed. It's important to give the operator specific guidance on how to carry out containment, and it may also be wise to step up inspection until the problem is resolved.

2. **Diagnosis.** The failure's root cause needs to be determined. This often requires repeatedly asking "why?" — for example, if the failure happened because the operator wasn't properly trained, the next question is "why wasn't the operator properly trained?" This process should be repeated until an appropriate root cause is identified, one that leads to a permanent corrective action. It helps to build in lessons learned from previous failures to ease diagnosis, and to give the operator written guidance on likely causes, specifying the diagnostic steps and tests they should run as part of the preliminary effort. If the root cause still isn't identified, the plan should specify who should be called in to help, like the product engineer or the QE themselves.

3. **Verification.** You shouldn't simply assume the corrective action solved the problem — it needs to be proven. Additional samples should be collected after the corrective change is implemented, to verify the problem was actually fixed. Whenever possible, the reaction plan should specify how many additional samples are needed before resuming normal operation.

4. **Disposition.** The final step, obvious but mandatory, is determining an appropriate disposition for the material that was contained in the reaction plan's first step. Typical dispositions include scrap, rework, sort, use as-is, and return to supplier. Written instructions are recommended for carrying out sorting or rework.

Once the control plan's initial version is released to production, operators should take ownership of the document and treat it as a living document, constantly reviewing and updating it with new information. There should also be a feedback mechanism built into the process: as new or unexpected failure modes are discovered on the line, the control plan is updated and that information feeds back into the corresponding FMEAs. Keeping the documentation current makes advanced quality planning (AQP) easier for future programs.

### Identifying critical control points

Identifying critical control points is a discipline borrowed from hazard analysis and critical control points (HACCP), traditionally associated with food safety, but today applied broadly across many other industrial sectors. A critical control point is a process step or point at which a major or serious product failure can be introduced. HACCP methodology's goal is to prevent known hazards and reduce the risk of them occurring at points in the production cycle.

A structured process, adapted from Rodriguez-Perez (2012), describes twelve steps for successfully developing a HACCP plan:

1. Form a HACCP team.
2. Fully describe the product, including its specifications.
3. Determine the product's intended use.
4. Build a process flow diagram.
5. Confirm the flow diagram on-site.
6. Identify and analyze the hazards.
7. Determine the critical control points.
8. Establish critical limits for each critical control point.
9. Establish a monitoring procedure.
10. Establish a corrective action.
11. Verify the HACCP plan.
12. Document the HACCP process.

To determine the critical control points, each process step is examined to check whether any of the identified hazards can occur there and whether control measures exist to address it. The HACCP team assesses which process steps are critical control points: if the hazard can be adequately controlled at that point, if it isn't better controlled at another step, and if it's essential to process safety, then that step is a critical control point for the specified hazard. When there's a step where a hazard can occur and no control measures are in place, that high-risk condition needs to be addressed as a priority — either by adding a control or by redesigning the process step.

### Developing and validating work instructions

Work instructions provide details for staff directly responsible for operating the process. They need to be documented and posted, or easily accessible, right at the workstation. Assembly instructions list every task to be performed in sequential order. Setup instructions list the machine's proper settings, like feed rates, temperatures, and pressures, and should also list any tasks or inspections that need to be carried out during production startup to check whether the process is properly adjusted.

Work instructions should be clear and understandable. Liberal use of sketches, charts, photographs, and other visual aids is strongly encouraged, since it reduces ambiguity and dependence on the operator's subjective interpretation. The effort to eliminate opportunities for error in the process should necessarily include work instructions as one of its tools — after all, a poorly written instruction is itself a source of variation and nonconformance. Because of this, organizations producing a variety of similar products should consider creating unique instructions for each model, instead of using generic examples, favoring lookup tables for bills of materials and setup instructions with specific cross-references for each configuration.

Validating work instructions, though not treated as a separate formal step in the classic references, flows naturally from the control plan's own life cycle: just as the control plan is a living document, revised as new failure modes are discovered, work instructions should be tested in practice — ideally by operators who don't yet know them — to confirm they consistently produce the intended result, without ambiguity and without relying on undocumented tacit knowledge. Any discrepancy found during this validation should feed back into both the work instruction and, where applicable, the control plan itself and the process FMEA, closing the loop on continuous improvement of shop-floor documentation.

### Pre-Control: when a simpler alternative to charts is acceptable

Pre-control is a process-monitoring technique simpler than classic control charts, originally developed by Rath & Strong in the 1950s as an easy-to-run alternative for low-volume settings or frequent setups, where building and maintaining a traditional X-bar/R chart is impractical.

**How it works.** The tolerance zone (between LSL and USL) is split into three regions using the specification limits themselves — not statistical limits calculated from $\sigma$:

- **Green zone** — the middle half of the tolerance (between $\text{target} \pm \tfrac{1}{4}$ of the specification width).
- **Yellow zones** — the two outer quarters, between the green zone and each specification limit.
- **Red zone** — outside the specification limits (nonconforming).

**Operating rules.**

1. **Initial qualification:** 5 consecutive parts in the green zone to release the process to production.
2. **During production:** periodically measure **2 consecutive parts**. If both fall in the green zone, keep producing. If one falls in yellow, measure the second; if both fall in the same yellow zone, adjust the process. If they fall in opposite yellow zones, the process has excessive variation — stop and investigate. Any part in the red zone stops production immediately.

**When it's acceptable to use.** Low-volume processes, frequent setup changes, manual operations with no capacity to maintain charts, or as an introductory technique for operators before implementing classic SPC — as long as the process already has a high $C_{pk}$ (typically $C_{pk} \geq 1.33$ is recommended before applying pre-control, since the zones depend directly on the tolerance, not the process's actual variation).

**Where it falls short — and why it doesn't replace the control chart.** Pre-control **doesn't detect trends**, doesn't distinguish special causes of variation from common ones, provides no statistical estimate of capability or error risk (there are no alpha/beta rates tied to its rules), and is insensitive to small shifts in the mean — exactly the kind of signal an X-bar chart detects well. Treat it as a **simple operational screening tool**, not a substitute for the statistical SPC covered in the rest of this unit.
