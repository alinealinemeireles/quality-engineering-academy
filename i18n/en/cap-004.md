## Chapter 4: Other Process Improvement and Quality Methods

### Other Formal Quality or Process Improvement Programs

#### Lean Process Management

Lean principles often go hand in hand with Six Sigma principles. Although Lean originally developed as a concept for reducing waste in a manufacturing setting, the ideas behind Lean Process Management can be applied to any process involving the movement or creation of goods or services. That's true even if those services are virtual or digital, as in a computerized workflow process.

One way Lean resembles Six Sigma is that it's concerned with continuous improvement; like Six Sigma, Lean provides waste-elimination tools so that daily controls and improvements can be made to processes. In fact, one of Lean's continuous improvement tools is called Kaizen, a Japanese word that loosely translates to "change for the better." The purpose of every change in a Kaizen setting is to eliminate waste and/or create more value for the customer on an ongoing basis.

Lean Process Management can be deployed within a project setting or in day-to-day production. Like Six Sigma, Lean is more about an overall quality culture than a single quality event. Many organizations use Lean principles to make improvements to processes. By simply putting a few Lean principles in place, managers can drastically increase output and cut costs across their departments.

Since Lean principles are so effective and fit so well with Six Sigma principles, for the purposes of this book, we'll often treat Lean as part of the Six Sigma methodology.

#### Total Quality Management (TQM)

Total Quality Management, or TQM, is a phrase well known to anyone who worked in business during the last quarter of the 20th century. The TQM approach to quality is one of the first formal methods implemented in business settings in the United States. Originally developed in the 1950s, Total Quality Management didn't become popular among companies in the country until the 1980s. At one point, TQM became so popular among executives and other leaders that it became something of a joke among certain work teams, who believed a lot of effort and expense was spent on quality with no equivalent benefit resulting from it. In fact, if you recall the previous chapter, GE's Jack Welch felt this way.

Although Total Quality Management programs were, at times, somewhat mediocre in terms of results, the method was an essential step toward current improvement and quality methods, like Six Sigma. TQM wasn't without results: as with any method, the results depended heavily on how the program was implemented and on the organization's culture. For that reason, TQM and its variations are still in use across many industries today. Some requirements for a successful TQM program include:

- A rigid commitment to quality at every level of the organization, especially among leaders
- Empowered employees who can make quality decisions while working within the process, without having to constantly seek leadership approval for those decisions
- A reward and recognition structure to promote quality work, so employees have a reason to make quality-focused decisions
- Strategic planning that factors in quality and quality-improvement goals when making long-term decisions
- Systems that let organizations make improvements and monitor quality

Successful TQM initiatives require eight key elements: ethics, integrity, trust, training, teamwork, leadership, recognition, and communication. You can think of these elements as if they were part of the components needed to build a lasting, high-quality building. Ethics, integrity, and trust become quality's foundation. Training, teamwork, and leadership are the bricks quality organizations are built from. Honest, open, concise communication is the mortar that holds everything together, and recognition is the roof that covers everything, giving employees a reason to pursue and sustain quality.

One of the biggest advantages of the TQM mindset is that it started forcing organizations to see themselves as a single entity, rather than several loosely related entities or departments. Before the quality methods developed in the second half of the 20th century, many organizations were run through heavily siloed departments. One department often didn't understand what another was doing, causing a lot of rework and waste. Each department might pursue higher levels of quality or process improvements, but in the end, the organization was only as strong as its weakest element.

TQM started shifting departmental thinking on a massive scale: organizations began adopting enterprise-wide approaches to decision-making, quality, and customer service. Business leaders started seeing companies as a series of interconnected processes operating toward a single end goal. Within TQM's boundaries, ideas for business process reengineering began to develop.

Organizations that use TQM often experience benefits like:

- Higher employee engagement and morale
- Reduced production or product costs
- Shorter cycle times
- More satisfied customers

### Business Process Reengineering (BPR)

Six Sigma, Lean, and TQM are all concerned with making continuous changes, both large and small, that move an organization closer and closer to a model of perfection. In Lean's case, that model is a zero-waste process; in Six Sigma, the model is statistically 6 sigma. In TQM, organizations generally define their own version of perfection before working toward it. Business Process Reengineering, or BPR, is less concerned with incremental quality gains and more concerned with radical change across an entire organization or process architecture.

Business process reengineering, also called business process redesign, is generally concerned with the technical processes that occur across an entire organization. These processes can include systems, software, data storage, cloud and web-based processes, and computer-based workflows operated and maintained by human users. Because of BPR's heavy integration of automation and computing elements into processes, organizations undertaking BPR efforts need to rely heavily on internal and external technical resources. Internal resources provide programming, integration, and troubleshooting services as processes are developed or redesigned. External resources might be BPR consultants, contract programmers and developers, or vendors bringing in new software products.

As you might imagine, BPR initiatives can be expensive, which is why they're generally only deployed when an organization expects an exponential gain or has determined its current processes are obsolete or seriously compromised.

BPR projects tend to follow a common roadmap, though there isn't a defined set of principles the way there is with Six Sigma. Most projects go through planning, design, and implementation phases. During planning, teams use process mapping and process architecture principles to define company-wide processes in their current state. Teams look for improvement opportunities and brainstorm new architectures for processes across the organization.

During the design phase, BPR teams use validation techniques to make sure the solutions they're planning will work within the business structure. They also start building tools and programs to integrate the changes; technical teams might use the Scrum methods described later in this chapter at this point in the process.

Finally, organizations implement the changes they've made. Since the changes tend to be programmatic in nature, implementation generally includes a rigorous change-management and testing procedure. Testing in technical settings includes steps like:

- Sandbox testing of basic functionality
- Quality assurance testing by trained technical resources
- Beta testing, during which experienced subject-matter experts verify every aspect of a program in a limited live environment
- A rollout of the program to the company, often run in a phased approach, during which technical resources are available to immediately resolve troubleshooting issues
- A conversion to regular operation, in which technical resources are available at normal capacity to handle occasional problems

### Rummler-Brache

As process improvement methods became increasingly popular in the 1980s and beyond, individuals often took pieces of one method or another and folded them into new improvement or quality programs. In this way, companies outside the manufacturing sector started implementing pieces of methods that incorporated elements of Lean and Six Sigma. One such program is known as Rummler-Brache.

Rummler-Brache was created in the 1980s by Geary Rummler and Alan Brache. They developed what remains a proprietary program used by their own consulting firm, but details of the method have been published and used by others. The method seeks to generate positive change in processes and organizations using a set of practical tools for solving business issues and process problems.

One of Rummler-Brache's core components is known as the Nine Boxes Model. The model is built from a matrix of three performance levels and three performance dimensions. The performance levels are the performer, the process, and the organization. The dimensions are management, design, and goal. Rummler-Brache approaches improvement in six phases:

- **Improvement planning.** During the first phase, leadership and subject-matter experts commit to making improvements and start identifying opportunities for change.
- **Definition.** During the second phase, the project's goals and scope are defined and teams are formed to build improvements.
- **Analysis and Design.** Teams use analysis to understand the current problem and to define and validate viable solutions.
- **Implementation.** Teams implement process changes. Depending on the type of change, this can include programming changes, staff retraining, changes to machines or equipment, or policy changes.
- **Process management.** Teams monitor the process during and immediately after the change to make sure the improvements work as planned.

### Scrum

Scrum is a project development method specific to Agile programming initiatives in technical departments. Scrum is used when teams want to build new technical products or integrate new developments into existing products within a short timeframe. Typically, Scrum projects run between two and four weeks, which is traditionally a very tight timeframe for programming projects. Scrum was developed because programming and development teams needed a way to meet the ongoing technical design and improvement needs of other departments without substantially increasing programming or testing hours or hiring more technical staff. Scrum can also be used to speed up the time to production or market for software products and applications.

Scrum is a concept related to the other process improvement initiatives discussed in this book, because many projects today require some kind of technical resource or change. While project teams work to validate and measure, technical departments often simultaneously deploy Scrum concepts to meet the improvement project's development needs within the timeframe.

Scrum projects feature three main phases:

- **The pregame.** Development teams review the available data and business requirements. They use this information to sketch out the concept for the new product or update. This often involves translating business and process concepts into computing and technical concepts.
- **The game.** Teams start developing the product through programming sprints. Sprints are smaller development phases completed in sequence, generally with a review and validation of the work before moving on to the next sprint. By validating the work during development, teams can build functional products faster.
- **The postgame.** Even though validation happens during development, teams still need to follow quality assurance, testing, and change-management procedures. Quality preparation for the product's launch is handled in the final phase.

### The Customer Experience Management Method (CEM)

Like Rummler-Brache, the Customer Experience Management Method, or CEM Method, was created by process improvement consultants to meet needs at organizations outside manufacturing. CEM combines a few process improvement tools with customer relationship management. It was developed in the 1990s by the Virgin Group and became popular throughout the '90s and early 2000s.

The CEM Method takes an outside-in approach to process improvement, focusing on what the customer wants or needs and how each of an organization's processes meets that need. CEM's main purpose is to align processes across the organization with customer satisfaction goals. So, even processes with no direct connection to customers get defined in customer terms.

For example, shipping processes are obviously directly related to end customers, so it's easy to define how those processes can best serve them. Shipments should arrive on time, be accurate against orders, and shipping costs should be affordable.

Internal human resources processes are harder to tie to customer-facing goals. However, employee morale and functionality are directly related to how well those employees can serve customers. You can make a customer-facing statement about practically any of an organization's processes this way. If organizations can't tie a process to the customer, they should ask themselves whether the process is necessary or is struggling.

Like Six Sigma, CEM relies heavily on data. Organizations can't make decisions about customer goals and process success without collecting and analyzing customer feedback. CEM's advantage is that organizations can deploy customer-focused tactics company-wide, which often results in huge gains in customer satisfaction, loyalty, and spending. One downside of this method is that traditionally inward-facing departments, like human resources, legal, and accounting, often struggle to implement a customer-focused culture shift.

### JumpStart

JumpStart differs from the other programs and methods described in this chapter in that it's an accelerated method for identifying problems and solutions in a single session. JumpStart can be used within almost every other method described in this book as a way to spark discussions about processes or identify possible solutions. It can also be used as a management tool to help teams reach viable solutions outside project settings or in the absence of project resources.

Since JumpStart doesn't set aside time for rigorous verification or statistical analysis on its own, teams shouldn't use this method to implement sweeping changes or try to improve processes that could seriously impact the customer experience or the bottom line. One downside of using JumpStart on its own is that changes are sometimes made with a "wait and see" mindset, which is safe for many internal team changes, but often risky for department-wide or company-wide processes, or for making changes to processes closely tied to regulatory or compliance rules.

JumpStart generally starts when leaders at some level identify an area of concern or opportunity. The manager, supervisor, or other delegate identifies a team of employees they believe will offer proper insight into the issue at hand. In most cases, JumpStart doesn't work to define the problem: the group is close enough to the issue to already know what's wrong. Instead, the group spends several hours brainstorming the problem's root causes and working out possible solutions.

Six Sigma and other process improvement tools can be deployed during JumpStart sessions. Fishbone diagrams and solution selection matrices, both covered in later chapters, can be used to validate assumptions using just the knowledge of the people in the room and some quick research.

JumpStart's benefit is that it lets teams build and implement small-scale solutions quickly, often delivering problem resolution the same day. It also lets teams identify issues that need to be handled in a more comprehensive project setting.

### When to Use Six Sigma

Some organizations use several project improvement methods. As a Six Sigma specialist, you may occasionally have to make the case for your own method. Here are a few reasons to choose Six Sigma over the other methods described in this chapter.

**Facing the unknown.** Six Sigma is designed so you can start a project even when you don't know the cause of the problem. In some cases, teams aren't even sure exactly what the problem is — they just know some metric isn't performing as desired. For example, an organization might see a drop in profits that doesn't correct itself over several consecutive quarters. Six Sigma methods can start searching for the problem's causes, prioritize them, and work toward solutions.

**When problems are broad and undefined.** Even when a problem is understood, if it's broad in scope and poorly defined, improvement projects that aren't tightly managed can grow in scope to the point of becoming unmanageable. In that situation, teams try to solve bigger and bigger problems. As a result, no problem gets fully solved. Six Sigma includes controls to prevent this kind of scope creep, so teams can make incremental improvements that continuously refine a process over time. We'll talk more about scope creep in later chapters.

**When solving complex problems.** If processes are complex and feature many variables, it's hard to determine how to approach a solution, let alone define and measure success. Statistical analysis and process control knowledge let teams tackle problems involving huge amounts of data and many variables. Through analysis and graphical representation, complex ideas can be distilled into specific hypotheses, assumptions, and conclusions.

**When costs are closely tied to processes.** Since Six Sigma's statistical process control component lets teams make more accurate assumptions than almost any other method, it's very well suited to situations closely tied to revenue or cost. When a single small change can result in millions of dollars in gains or losses, teams need to validate assumptions with an extremely small margin of error. Guesswork, basic research, and even years of experience can't do this as precisely as properly implemented Six Sigma methods.
