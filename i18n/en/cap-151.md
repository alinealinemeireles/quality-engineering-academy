## Chapter 151: Food Industry Risk — HACCP, ISO 22000, and FSSC 22000 ✦

### The engineering question

> *"In the automotive industry, a defect costs money. Here, a defect can kill someone — and it's
> detected after consumption. How does that change the control architecture?"*

It changes everything. In food safety, three characteristics make the classic quality approach
insufficient:

1. **The consequence is irreversible.** There's no reworking a poisoned consumer.
2. **Verification is destructive and slow.** A microbiological result takes 24-72 h; the product has
   already shipped.
3. **The hazard is frequently invisible.** *Listeria*, *Salmonella*, allergens, and mycotoxins have
   no detectable appearance, smell, or taste.

The architectural consequence is decisive: **control has to be preventive and process-based**, not
product inspection. That's HACCP's logic — and it's, historically, the same reasoning that led SPC to
replace final inspection in manufacturing, applied to a domain where the alternative isn't just
expensive, it's impossible.

---

## 151.1 The normative framework in 2026

| Reference | Nature | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Codex Alimentarius CXC 1-1969, Rev. 2020** | International guide | General Principles of Food Hygiene; **the 7 HACCP principles** are in its Annex |
| **ISO 22000:2018** (2nd ed.) | Certifiable management system standard | FSMS with high-level structure; integrates HACCP + PRP + Annex SL |
| **ISO/TS 22002-x** | Technical specifications | Sector-specific prerequisite programs (manufacturing, catering, farming, packaging…) |
| **FSSC 22000 (V7, 2026)** | GFSI-recognized certification scheme | ISO 22000 + ISO/TS 22002-x + additional requirements |
| **Market legislation** | Mandatory | EU: Reg. (EC) 852/2004, 178/2002. US: FSMA. Brazil: RDC/ANVISA. Varies by jurisdiction |

> **The distinction that causes the most confusion:** the **Codex** gives the principles; **ISO
> 22000** is the certifiable management system standard; **FSSC 22000** is the GFSI-recognized
> certification scheme that most retailers require. Certifying to ISO 22000 does **not** equate to
> having GFSI certification — and that's frequently the real contractual requirement.

---

## 151.2 The seven HACCP principles, and what gets done wrong in each

| # | Principle | Common mistake in practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hazard analysis** | Listing generic hazards without assessing severity × probability **for this product and process** |
| 2 | **Determine the CCPs** | Declaring everything important a CCP. A CCP is a point where control is **essential** to prevent or eliminate the hazard |
| 3 | **Establish critical limits** | Limits with no **scientific validation**; copied from another plant or a manual |
| 4 | **Monitoring** | Unjustified frequency; measurement that isn't continuous where it should be |
| 5 | **Corrective actions** | Define what to do to the process, but not the **product disposition** since the last conforming check |
| 6 | **Verification** | Confused with monitoring. Verification is confirming the **system** works |
| 7 | **Documentation and records** | Records filled in after the fact — the most common and most serious audit finding |

### CCP, oPRP, and PRP: the distinction that structures the system

| Level | Definition | Control failure | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PRP** | Basic, general hygiene conditions | Doesn't trigger a decision about a specific lot | Cleaning, pest control, training, maintenance |
| **oPRP** | Operational PRP: controls a significant hazard, but a failure **doesn't necessarily** lead to unsafe product | Case-by-case assessment | Raw-material supplier control; allergen segregation |
| **CCP** | Point where control is **essential**; loss of control **produces** potentially unsafe product | **Suspect product, mandatory hold** | Pasteurization; metal detection; heat treatment |

**Why the distinction matters operationally:** a deviation at a CCP forces the product to be held and
assessed. Classifying too many points as CCPs generates constant holds, the system becomes
unmanageable, and — the real consequence — people start treating CCP deviations as routine. A system
with 30 CCPs is a system where no CCP is taken seriously.

## 151.3 Validating a critical limit: the quantitative part

A critical limit isn't a choice. It's a **scientific conclusion** about the logarithmic reduction of
a microbial population.

**The base model — decimal reduction:**

$$ \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{N_0}{N}\right) = \frac{t}{D_T}, \qquad D_T = D_{ref} \times 10^{\,(T_{ref}-T)/z} $$

- **D_T** — time, at temperature T, to reduce the population to 1/10 (one decimal reduction).
- **z** — temperature increase that reduces D by a factor of 10.
- **F value** — equivalent treatment time at the reference temperature.

**The regulatory requirement is expressed in logarithmic reductions.** For example, a **6.5 log**
reduction of *Salmonella* in poultry products, or **5 log** of the relevant pathogen in juices.

The code cell solves the complete problem: given the requirement in log, at what temperature and for
how long? And — what's almost never done — **what margin is needed once you propagate the
uncertainty** of D, of z, of the real temperature, and of the initial distribution.

> **The mistake quantitative analysis exposes:** validating the critical limit with the **nominal**
> values of D and z. The biological variability of D between strains is substantial, and the real
> temperature at the coldest point of the product is not the sensor's temperature. A limit validated
> at the nominal can fail in a non-negligible fraction of lots.

---

## 151.4 Allergens: the hazard that isn't destroyed

Allergens are the only food hazard **no heat treatment eliminates**. Control is exclusively through
**segregation, sequencing, and validated cleaning** — and correct labeling.

The four points of failure, in order of real frequency:

1. **Labeling error** — the number-one cause of allergen recalls in almost every market. It's an
   **information** error, not a process one, and so it escapes classic process control.
2. **Cross-contamination from shared lines** — residue from the previous product.
3. **Formulation error** — wrong ingredient, or an uncommunicated supplier change.
4. **Uncommunicated supplier change** — the same common cause from Chapter 150, in another domain.

**Cleaning validation between allergens** is a quantitative problem: the residual residue is
quantified (ELISA, ATP as an indirect indicator) and compared against the reference-dose threshold,
weighted by the dilution in the next product. It's quantitative risk analysis, not visual
inspection.

---

## 151.5 Recall and traceability: the "one step back, one step forward" math

The ability to **scope** a recall is a direct function of lot design and traceability.

The quantity that decides is the **amplification ratio**: how many units have to be recalled for
every unit actually affected. It depends on:

- **Lot size** — bigger lots dilute production cost and amplify recall cost.
- **Record granularity** — by lot, by production hour, by pallet, by unit.
- **Downstream mixing** — if a silo blends several lots, upstream traceability is lost.
- **Data RPO** (Chapter 148) — lost data = the whole window gets recalled.

The code cell calculates the expected recall cost as a function of lot design — and produces an
uncomfortable result: **by minimizing expected cost, the larger lot wins almost every time.** The
switchover cost occurs on every lot; the recall cost occurs in a small fraction of years, and the
average favors the large lot.

That's why, in food safety, **lot size is not an expected-cost minimization decision — it's a risk
appetite decision** (Chapter 143), for the same reason as in Chapter 145: a recall is a
low-frequency, high-severity risk, and for those the mean is the wrong metric. The organization
declares the maximum recall it can absorb, and the lot is sized to respect that constraint. The
premium paid in additional changeovers becomes explicit and quantified.

The alternative that resolves the dilemma is almost always the best one: keep the **physical** lot
large and make **traceability** finer (record by production hour). It scopes the recall without
multiplying changeovers — as long as the data RPO allows it.

## 151.6 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too many CCPs | Unmanageable system; deviations treated as routine | Apply the Codex decision tree rigorously |
| Critical limit with no scientific validation | Doesn't meet the required log reduction | Validate with D, z, and uncertainty propagation |
| Validating at the nominal value of D and z | Fails on a fraction of lots | Monte Carlo with biological variability |
| Confusing monitoring with verification | The system is never verified | Monitoring = real time; verification = the system works |
| Corrective action with no product disposition | Suspect product shipped | Always define disposition since the last conforming check |
| Records filled in after the fact | Loss of credibility and legal defensibility | Record at the moment, preferably automatic |
| Allergens treated as chemical contamination | Wrongly designed control | Segregation, sequencing, validated cleaning, labeling |
| Large lots for production convenience | Recall amplified by orders of magnitude | Size the lot by expected recall cost |
| Certifying ISO 22000 assuming it equals GFSI | Doesn't meet the customer's requirement | FSSC 22000 (or BRCGS/IFS) for GFSI recognition |

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Classifying control points.**
*In a ready-meal plant, classify: (a) final heat treatment; (b) receiving raw vegetables; (c)
temperature control of the refrigerated chamber; (d) cleaning the line between a gluten product and
a gluten-free one.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**(a) Final heat treatment — CCP.** A control measure exists (Q1: yes); it eliminates the hazard to
an acceptable level (Q2: yes) → CCP. Validated critical limit (time/temperature), continuous
monitoring with automatic logging, corrective action with defined product disposition.

**(b) Receiving raw vegetables — oPRP.** A control exists (Q1: yes); it doesn't eliminate the hazard
(Q2: no); contamination can exceed acceptable levels (Q3: yes); a later step — heat treatment —
eliminates it (Q4: yes) → oPRP. Supplier specification, certificates, sampling verification.

**(c) Refrigeration — depends on the product, and this is the exercise's point.**
- If the product is **ready to eat** and *Listeria monocytogenes* can grow, refrigeration is the
  last line of defense: there's no later step that eliminates it (Q4: no) → **CCP**, with a critical
  limit on temperature and cumulative time.
- If there's **cooking by the consumer** that eliminates the hazard → **oPRP**.

The same physical operation, different classifications. The classification derives from the
**hazard and the product**, not the equipment — and it's the most common mistake to copy the HACCP
plan from another plant.

**(d) Cleaning between gluten and gluten-free — CCP.** The allergen **isn't eliminated by any later
step**. Cleaning is the only barrier, and loss of control produces product potentially unsafe for a
celiac consumer. It requires **validated cleaning** (with residue quantification by ELISA, not
visual inspection) and a critical limit expressed in ppm of gliadin.

**E2 — Validating a critical limit.**
*Production proposes shortening pasteurization time from 30 s to 22 s at 72 °C, claiming sufficient
margin. Evaluate.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**You don't evaluate it with the nominal value.** The code cell shows why.

**Step 1 — check the nominal reduction.** With D(70 °C) = 0.30 min and z = 6.5 °C, D(72 °C) ≈ 0.146
min = 8.8 s. In 22 s: 22/8.8 ≈ **2.5 log**. If the requirement is 6.5 log, the process **already
fails at the nominal** — the proposal is unacceptable before any variability analysis.

**Step 2 — if the nominal passed, propagate the uncertainty.** The four sources the code models:

- **biological variability of D** between strains (lognormal, substantial);
- **uncertainty in z**;
- **temperature bias** — the coldest point of the product isn't at the sensor's temperature. This is
  typically the dominant and most-ignored contributor;
- **residence-time variation** — the distribution of times in a continuous system.

**Step 3 — compute P(reduction < requirement)** and compare against the acceptance criterion. For a
hazard with a public-health consequence, the criterion has to be very demanding — typically < 1 in a
million.

**Step 4 — the answer to production**, with the number:

> *"At 72 °C, 22 s gives ~2.5 log at nominal, against the required 6.5 log. Even ignoring all
> variability, the process doesn't comply. For 6.5 log at 72 °C, ~57 s is needed at nominal, and
> ~X s with margin for P(failure) < 1 ppm. If the goal is to increase throughput, the way is to raise
> the temperature — every 6.5 °C divides the required time by 10 — as long as organoleptic quality
> allows it."*

**The methodological point:** present the alternative, not just the refusal. Production has a real
capacity problem; quality engineering solves it within the safety constraint.

</details>

**E3 — Allergens.**
*Design allergen control on a line shared between a product with peanuts and one without.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Control hierarchy, in order of effectiveness — and the first is almost never considered:**

1. **Elimination.** A dedicated line for the peanut product. It's the only solution that removes the
   hazard instead of managing it. Expensive, and frequently the cheapest once the expected recall
   cost is calculated.
2. **Sequencing.** Produce the **allergen-free** product **first**, and the one with the allergen
   **at the end of the cycle**, before full cleaning. Reduces exposure without investment.
3. **Validated cleaning.** If sharing occurs, cleaning is a CCP:
   - A **validated**, not just verified, cleaning procedure;
   - Residue quantification by peanut-specific **ELISA** (ATP is a general-cleanliness indicator,
     **not** an allergen one — it's a common mistake to use it as proof);
   - Critical limit in ppm, derived from the reference dose and the dilution in the next product;
   - Result logged before releasing the line.
4. **Raw-material segregation.** Separate storage, unambiguous visual identification, dedicated
   staff and utensil flow.
5. **Labeling control** — and here's the exercise's critical point.

**The number-one cause of allergen recalls is a labeling error, not cross-contamination.** The wrong
label on the wrong line, a formulation change without a label change, or an old label version still
in stock.

The effective control is **automatic verification**: reading the label's barcode and matching it
against the production order, blocking the line on a mismatch. It's an engineering poka-yoke
(Chapter 26) — a passive barrier, not an administrative one (Chapter 147).

6. **Precautionary labeling ("may contain")** — last resort, and **only** when the residual risk has
been quantified and can't be eliminated. Used as a substitute for control, it loses its meaning and
the allergic consumer stops taking it seriously.

</details>

**E4 — Recall and lot design.**
*Production wants full-day lots to reduce changeovers. Quantify the counterargument.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**The first result is uncomfortable and has to be acknowledged:** by minimizing **expected cost**,
production is right. The changeover cost grows linearly with the number of lots and occurs
**always**; the recall cost materializes in only 4% of years. The average favors the large lot, and
the code cell shows it without disguise.

**But lot size in food safety isn't an expected-cost-minimization decision.** It's a **risk
appetite** decision (Chapter 143), for the reasons in Chapter 145: a recall is a **low-frequency,
high-severity** risk, and for those the mean is the wrong metric. The right question isn't "which lot
minimizes the average cost?" but:

> *"What's the largest recall this organization can absorb without compromising viability or the
> relationship with the retailer?"*

Once that limit is declared, the lot size is determined — and the **premium** paid in changeover cost
is explicit and quantified. The cell calculates that premium for several declared limits. That's the
right way to bring the decision to leadership: not "small lots are better," but *"respecting the
€200,000-per-recall limit costs €333,000/year in additional changeovers; the alternative is
declaring a higher limit."*

**The alternative that resolves the dilemma, and that's almost always the best answer:**

Keep the **physical** lot large and make **traceability** finer — record by production hour, by
pallet, or by unit. It scopes the recall without multiplying changeovers. It's a **data** solution,
not a process one, and it costs a fraction.

**The condition for it to work:** the data RPO (Chapter 148). With an RPO of 24 h, the effective lot
becomes a full day's production, **regardless** of the physical lot design — and the entire
investment in small lots gets wiped out by a single IT-system failure. Traceability and lot design
are the same problem seen from two sides, and handling them in different departments is how you
guarantee they end up misaligned.

**A note on the costs used:** the example uses €6.20/unit recalled. The real cost of a recall
includes investigation, communication, notifying the authority, temporary delisting, loss of
retailer trust, and subsequent regulatory attention. Multiplying by 3-5 is conservative — and shifts
the break-even point substantially. Running the analysis with an understated cost is the most common
way to reach the wrong conclusion with correct arithmetic.

</details>
