## Chapter 86: Tree Diagram and Process Decision Program Chart (PDPC)

### What a Tree Diagram Is

The tree diagram's purpose is helping people discover, visualize, and communicate logical hierarchical relationships between critical events, or between goals and the means for achieving them. Tree diagrams are useful in situations where you want to define a hierarchical relationship between events, whether desirable or undesirable. Visually, the tool resembles an org chart: a higher-level event or goal branches into lower-level events or means, which can in turn branch into even more detailed levels, down to whatever level of detail the team finds useful.

The tree diagram isn't a single format, but a family of tools that share this hierarchical logic:

- **The fault tree (FT)** relates an undesirable "top event" — generally a failure — to a sequence of events leading to it, using logic operators like "AND" and "OR" gates to connect lower-level events to higher-level ones. Once the logic is laid out, probabilities can be quantified and the top event's risk estimated. The fault tree is a more focused tool than FMEA and works well for independent events, and is widely used in reliability, safety, and risk analysis.
- **The event tree** starts from an initial event and works "forward," with binary (yes/no) branches that form a hierarchy of possible responses down to a final outcome.
- **The systematic diagram** describes a sequence of goals and their chained means, letting you visualize possible alternatives for achieving a high-level goal.
- **The goal tree** is similar to the systematic diagram, but built around a high-level goal, linking sub-goals, functions, and "success trees" through logic symbols or gates down to the main goal.
- **The concept fan** is a tool aimed at creativity: it starts from a generic purpose or functional requirement and expands it "backward," generating alternative concepts capable of meeting that purpose.

### When to Use It — PDPC — Tree Diagram

The tree diagram is called for whenever the team needs to break down a broad, abstract goal (for example, "reduce manufacturing costs for product XX") into concrete, verifiable actions, or whenever it's necessary to logically, hierarchically map every possible path leading to an undesirable event (like an unplanned line stoppage). It's the right tool when the problem has a "deployment" nature: from a goal to several sub-objectives, or from an unwanted effect to its structural causes.

### How to Build a Fault Tree, Step by Step

1. **Identify the top event** — the undesirable event you want to prevent.
2. **Identify the second-level events** — the events that could lead to the top event.
3. **Develop the logical relationships** between the top event and the second-level events, using operators like "AND" or "OR" gates to connect them.
4. **Identify and connect the lower-level events**, repeating the previous two steps until you reach the desired level of detail.
5. **Quantify the tree (optional step)**, developing probability-of-occurrence estimates for the events and arriving at a probability estimate for the top event.

It's worth noting a fault tree doesn't necessarily contain every possible failure mode; it can account for or model human error, hardware and software failures, and acts of nature, but tends to work best for independent events — common causes are harder to model, especially for quantification purposes.

### Practical Example — PDPC — Tree Diagram

An industrial plant frequently suffers unscheduled line stoppages. The team builds a fault tree with the top event "unintended line stoppage," broken down into four second-level branches connected by "OR" gates (that is, any one of them is enough to cause the top event): production error or defect, missing part/material on the line, part/material failure on the line, and line equipment failure. The "missing part/material on the line" branch further breaks down into "part out of stock" and "order failure," each tracing back to even more specific causes, like supplier delay, internal communication error, or a lost requisition (passback) card. By visualizing this whole logical chain, the team can prioritize prevention actions at the points of greatest leverage, instead of just reacting to the most recent symptom.

### What PDPC Is

The process decision program chart's (PDPC) purpose is helping people organize and assess events and contingencies tied to a process, with an eye toward its implementation and/or early operating phases. PDPC is useful for proactively assessing, at a high level, a process's implementation — whether during the planning phase or at the start of operation. The team can use PDPC to "argue through" or mentally walk through the implementation, anticipating events that could occur and disrupt the process, or to guide early operations in the event of deviations from the plan. In short: PDPC helps anticipate deviations from what's expected and prepare effective contingencies for handling them.

PDPC can take various formats — one resembles an annotated tree diagram; another resembles an annotated process flowchart. In either format, PDPC's distinguishing feature is giving the user an overview of the possible contingencies tied to the process's implementation and/or operation.

### When to Use It

PDPC is the right tool when the team is about to implement a new process, a new procedure, or a significant change and wants, before "going live," to systematically map everything that could go wrong and already have a prepared response — instead of improvising reactions in the heat of the moment. It's also useful for guiding the first few weeks of a newly implemented process's operation, serving as a quick-reaction guide for the line team.

### How to Build a PDPC, Step by Step

1. **Identify the process's purpose.** Clearly understanding the purpose guides the whole PDPC's development, from the standpoint of possible contingencies and their impact on the desired outcome.
2. **Identify the process's basic activities and related events**, using a tree or process-flow format to lay out the activities in the expected sequence.
3. **Note the basic activities and events**, with brief descriptions of what's normally expected to happen.
4. **Overlay the possible (conceivable) deviations**, adding branches or events that represent the identified deviations from the expected activities/events.
5. **Note the possible deviations**, with brief descriptions relating to the deviations mapped in the previous step.
6. **Identify and note the contingency activities**, describing what will be done to avoid or neutralize each mapped deviation.
7. **Weigh the possible contingencies.** In this final step, the team reviews the PDPC as a whole, considers the process's purpose, and selects/flags the most appropriate contingencies, arriving at a complete contingency plan, with defined priorities for handling possible deviations from the planned implementation and/or operation.

### Practical Example

A company is redesigning its receiving, storage, and line-replenishment subprocesses. Before rolling out the changes, the team builds a PDPC that maps the expected flow — from receiving to order delivery/tracking — and overlays historically known deviations onto that flow: damaged goods, missing items, need for urgent shipment, and line delay. For each deviation, the PDPC ties in a contingency (for example, a specific protocol for "hot items" that go straight from receiving to the line, bypassing centralized storage) and a financial-impact estimate, letting management prioritize which contingencies deserve immediate investment. Doing this exercise before the new process goes live keeps the team from being caught off guard by the same recurring problems from the old process.
