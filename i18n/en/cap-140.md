## Chapter 140: Uncertainty Quantification and Monte Carlo for Quality Engineering ✦

### The engineering question

> *"The assembly clearance depends on five dimensions, each with its own tolerance and its own
> distribution. The worst case says assembly is impossible. Reality says we assemble 99.7% of the
> time. Who's right, and how much will I scrap?"*

Every measurement and every engineering prediction carries uncertainty. The question isn't whether
it exists, but whether it's **quantified and propagated** through to the decision. This chapter
covers the two faces of the same problem: **measurement uncertainty** (GUM) and **propagating
variation through a model** (tolerance stack-up, Monte Carlo).

---

## 140.1 Measurement uncertainty: the GUM framework

The GUM (*Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement*) structures uncertainty into:

- **Type A** — evaluated by statistical methods on repeated observations (standard deviation of the
  mean);
- **Type B** — evaluated by other means: calibration certificate, manufacturer's specification,
  instrument resolution, informed judgment.

The classification **isn't about the nature of the uncertainty**, it's about the evaluation method.
A common mistake is treating Type B as "less rigorous" — it isn't; it's often the dominant
component.

**Combined uncertainty** (for a model y = f(x₁,…,x_n) with independent inputs):

$$ u_c^2(y) = \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right)^2 u^2(x_i) $$

The ∂f/∂xᵢ terms are the **sensitivity coefficients** — they tell you how much each input's
uncertainty weighs in the result. With correlated inputs, you add the cross-covariance terms.

**Expanded uncertainty:** U = k·u_c, with k = 2 for ~95% confidence (assuming approximate
normality). U is what gets reported on the certificate.

**Useful Type B conversions:**

| Source | Assumed distribution | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|
| Instrument resolution d | Rectangular | d/(2√3) |
| Limits ±a with no other information | Rectangular | a/√3 |
| Certificate with U and k=2 | Normal | U/2 |
| Limits ±a with more likely central values | Triangular | a/√6 |

**The decision rule that ties the GUM to quality:** when comparing a measurement against a
specification limit, the **guard band** derives from U. Accepting only if the measurement falls
within specification by a margin ≥ U is the conservative conformance rule (ISO 14253-1).

---

## 140.2 Tolerance stack-up: three methods, three philosophies

For a clearance y = Σ ±xᵢ with tolerances Tᵢ:

| Method | Formula | Assumption | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Worst case** | T = Σ Tᵢ | All at the extreme simultaneously | Maximum conservatism; extremely tight tolerances |
| **RSS** | T = √(Σ Tᵢ²) | Independence, normality, processes centered at Cp≈1 | Realistic at volume; a residual fraction falls outside |
| **Monte Carlo** | simulation | Whatever real distributions you want | Full distribution; handles nonlinearity |

**When to use each:**

- **Worst case** — safety-critical, few units, catastrophic failure cost, or when the assembly must
  be guaranteed 100% (a safety mechanism).
- **RSS** — volume production, a linear relationship, independent components, roughly normal,
  centered distributions.
- **Monte Carlo** — whenever there's **nonlinearity**, non-normal distributions, **correlated**
  components, off-center processes, or when you want the **full distribution** to compute the
  out-of-tolerance fraction and percentiles.

> **The RSS trap:** it assumes **independence**. Components machined on the same machine, in the
> same setup, or from the same material lot are **correlated** — and positive correlation makes the
> real stack-up **larger** than the RSS predicts. This is the scenario where RSS is optimistic and
> the assembly fails more often than predicted.

---

## 140.3 Monte Carlo done right: the five elements

1. **Justified distributions.** Don't assume normal for convenience. A dimension controlled by a
   process with a centered Cpk = 1.0 is roughly normal; a dimension resulting from selection
   (*binning*) is truncated; a clearance is bounded at zero.
2. **Modeled correlation.** If the components come from the same source, use a correlation matrix
   or a copula. Ignoring correlation is the mistake that most often invalidates the result.
3. **Adequate number of replicates.** To estimate P99.865 with reasonable precision you need ≥ 10⁵
   replicates. Check stability by repeating with different seeds.
4. **Sensitivity analysis.** The main value of Monte Carlo isn't the final number — it's knowing
   **which component dominates**. A tornado chart answers "where does tightening the tolerance pay
   off."
5. **Fixed seed and versioned code.** For reproducibility and for audit.

---

## 140.4 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Using worst case in volume production | Impossible tolerances; unnecessary cost | RSS or Monte Carlo |
| Using RSS with correlated components | Underestimates the stack-up; the assembly fails | Model the correlation; MC |
| Assuming every process is centered | Underestimates the out-of-spec fraction | Include the mean shift |
| Monte Carlo with no sensitivity analysis | You lose the most useful information | Tornado / sensitivity indices |
| Too few replicates for extreme percentiles | Unstable estimate | ≥10⁵; check with several seeds |
| Ignoring measurement uncertainty in the conformance decision | Out-of-spec product gets accepted | Guard band derived from U |
| Treating Type B as less important | The dominant component gets ignored | Evaluate every source |

us = []
for nome, tipo, val, div, _ in fontes:

uc = np.sqrt(sum(u**2 for _, _, u in us))

print(f"  RSS       : folga varia +-{tol_rss:.3f} mm -> "
      f"[{nom-tol_rss:.3f}, {nom+tol_rss:.3f}]")

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Uncertainty budget and conformance.**
*The tolerance is ±0.050 mm and the expanded uncertainty U = 0.0116 mm. A part measures 25.045 mm.
Do you accept it?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**It depends on the agreed decision rule — and that rule has to be defined beforehand, not decided
case by case.**

Specification: [24.950, 25.050]. Measurement: 25.045. U = 0.0116.

**Rule 1 — Simple acceptance (shared risk):** accept if the measurement is within specification,
ignoring U. 25.045 < 25.050 → **accept**. But the part's real range is [25.033, 25.057], which
**exceeds** the USL. Taking the standard uncertainty u = U/k = 0.0058 and an approximately normal
distribution centered on the measurement, the probability the part is actually out of spec is
≈**19%** — not negligible.

**Rule 2 — Conservative acceptance with a guard band (ISO 14253-1):** accept only if the measurement
falls within specification by a margin ≥ U. Acceptance limit = 25.050 − 0.0116 = **25.0384**. Since
25.045 > 25.0384 → **reject** (or classify as indeterminate).

**Which to use?**

- **Conservative** when the supplier is demonstrating conformance to the customer, or when the
  characteristic is safety-related. The risk of accepting an out-of-spec part falls on whoever has
  to bear it.
- **Simple** when it's contractually agreed — it's common, but it shares the risk implicitly, which
  generates disputes.

**The point the exercise is meant to establish:** the uncertainty consumes 2U/T = 0.0232/0.100 =
**23%** of the tolerance. That means nearly a quarter of the design tolerance is being spent on
measuring, not manufacturing. A better measurement system would give that margin back to the
process. It's the same diagnosis %GRR would give, expressed in metrology's language.

</details>

**E2 — Worst case or RSS?**
*An emergency braking mechanism has 4 components. Production: 500 units/year. Which method?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Worst case.** Three reasons converge:

1. **Consequence of failure.** Emergency braking is safety-critical. RSS explicitly accepts a
   fraction of assemblies falling out of tolerance — even if small. In a safety application, that
   fraction isn't acceptable, and the "it's only 0.1%" argument doesn't survive an accident
   investigation.

2. **Low volume.** With 500 units/year, the cost of tight tolerances is manageable. RSS pays off
   economically at high volume, where the difference in tooling cost is large and gets spread over
   many parts.

3. **The law of large numbers doesn't help.** With a small n, "on average it doesn't happen"
   arguments are weak: the concern is the individual unit, not the aggregate behavior.

**A complete, defensible approach combines all three methods:**

1. **Worst case** to set the design tolerances and prove the assembly is guaranteed even at the
   extreme.
2. **Monte Carlo** to quantify the expected real distribution and support the risk analysis
   (FMEA/FTA document) — it shows the real margin is even bigger than the guaranteed one.
3. **100% verification** of the critical clearance at assembly, with the value logged. In
   safety-critical work, analysis doesn't replace verification.

**A note on method:** if the worst case leads to technically impossible tolerances, the correct
response is **not** to switch to RSS. It's to revisit the design — reduce the number of components
in the chain, introduce an adjustment at assembly, or use dimensional selection (*matched
assembly*).

</details>

**E3 — Correlation.**
*Why does RSS underestimate the variation when components come from the same machine?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Because the variance of a sum includes **covariance terms** RSS omits:

Var(ΣXᵢ) = ΣVar(Xᵢ) + 2·Σ_{i<j} Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ)

RSS is exactly the first term. It assumes Cov = 0 for every pair.

**When components come from the same cell**, they share sources of variation — machine thermal
drift, tool wear, material lot, operator adjustment. That creates **positive** correlation: when one
is above nominal, the others tend to be too.

**The effect depends on the signs in the equation:**

- Components with the **same sign** and positive correlation → positive covariance → variation
  **larger** than RSS. This is the case in the cell example (B, C, D, E all subtracted).
- Components with **opposite signs** and positive correlation → negative covariance → variation
  **smaller** than RSS. In that case, correlation helps: the errors cancel out.

**A practical, counterintuitive consequence:** moving production from two different cells to a
single cell — seemingly a standardization improvement — can **worsen** the out-of-tolerance
assembly rate, by introducing correlation where there wasn't any. This explains assembly problems
that "appear out of nowhere" after a supplier or line consolidation.

**What to do:**

1. **Measure** the real correlation using historical component data (the step almost nobody takes).
2. Simulate with **Monte Carlo using the estimated correlation matrix**.
3. If the correlation is material, either move production of the coupled components apart, tighten
   the tolerance of the dominant ones, or redesign so the coupled components enter with opposite
   signs.

</details>

**E4 — Sensitivity and the investment decision.**
*The tornado chart shows component A explains ~50% of the variance (49.6%). Tightening its
tolerance from ±0.10 to ±0.07 costs €40,000 in tooling. Current scrap costs €95,000/year. Is it
worth it?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**The calculation has to be done, not estimated by a rule of three.** The relationship between
tolerance and PPM is strongly **nonlinear** — it's the tail of a distribution.

**Step 1 — Simulate.** Run Monte Carlo with A's tolerance reduced to ±0.07, keeping everything else
the same. Suppose the out-of-spec fraction drops from ~1,640 PPM to ~270 PPM (the code cell does
this calculation).

**Step 2 — Convert to euros.** A ~84% reduction in the out-of-spec fraction. If the current scrap
(€95,000/year) is fully attributable to this clearance, the annual savings are ≈ €79,800.

**Step 3 — Return.** Payback = 40,000/79,800 ≈ **6 months**. Yes, it's worth it.

**But before approving it, four checks:**

1. **Is all the scrap really from this cause?** €95,000/year might include other causes. Only the
   fraction attributable to this clearance counts. This is the most common mistake in this kind of
   justification.
2. **Is a ±0.07 tolerance achievable?** It requires Cp ≥ 1.0 at the new tolerance — verify with
   capability data from the proposed process, not with the tooling vendor's promise.
3. **Is there a cheaper alternative?** Before investing in tooling: **center** A's process (if it's
   off-center, the gain might be free), or **reduce the correlation** between components, or adjust
   the clearance's nominal. A mean shift costs a setup adjustment, not €40,000.
4. **Recurring cost.** A tighter tolerance can mean longer cycle time, more tool wear, more
   inspection. The €40,000 is the investment; you need to add the additional unit cost.

**The chapter's lesson:** Monte Carlo isn't there to produce a final number. It's there to make
"where does tightening pay off?" a quantitative question — and to show that tightening the wrong
component (E, in the example, which contributes little) spends money without changing the result.

</details>
