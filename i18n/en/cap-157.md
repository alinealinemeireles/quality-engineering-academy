## Chapter 903: Voice of the Customer, Satisfaction and the Link to Lean Six Sigma

### The engineering question

*The annual satisfaction survey came back at 87%. Leadership is happy. Does that mean the company
is listening to the customer?*

Not necessarily. And the distinction between the two things — **what the customer says** and
**how the customer rates what they received** — is the starting point for this entire chapter.

---

## 1 · The fundamental difference

> **Voice of the Customer = what the customer says, expects, needs, and values.**
> **Satisfaction = how the customer rates the experience or outcome they received.**

Imagine an industrial company that manufactures packaging for cosmetics. The customer says:

> "I need to receive the packaging without scratches, in the agreed quantity, and on time."

That's the **Voice of the Customer**. The company turns it into measurable requirements:

| Voice of the Customer | Measurable requirement |
|---|---|
| "Without scratches" | ≤ X defects per million |
| "Correct quantity" | 100% of the ordered quantity |
| "On time" | OTIF ≥ 98% |
| "Good appearance" | Visual inspection criteria |
| "Fast response" | Complaints answered ≤ 48 h |

After receiving the product, the customer responds: *"I'm satisfied with the service and the
quality."* That's **satisfaction** — a later evaluation, of an experience already lived.

Both feed the same system: **requirements → indicators → analysis → continuous improvement**. But
they're measured at different times, by different instruments, and confusing the two is the most
common mistake in quality management systems.

---

## 2 · How this shows up in ISO 9001

There's a conceptual difference worth noting: **ISO 9001 doesn't require** a process formally
called "Voice of the Customer." What it requires is that the organization **determine and monitor
customer perception** of the degree to which their needs and expectations have been met, and that
it **determine the requirements** related to products and services, monitoring performance against
them.

That's where the logical chain the standard assumes comes from, and it matters more than any
single questionnaire:

**Customer needs/expectations → product/service requirements → processes capable of meeting the
requirements → results → customer perception/satisfaction → analysis → improvement.**

### The common mistake

A company that reports "annual satisfaction survey: 87%" might think it's meeting the requirement
well. But that doesn't necessarily mean it's listening to the customer — because there may be, in
parallel and with no connection to the satisfaction analysis: complaints, returns, delays, quality
problems, change requests, lost customers, commercial complaints, and technical-support issues. A
mature system **cross-references** all these sources; a bureaucratic system just files the survey.

---

## 3 · VoC is much bigger than a satisfaction survey

Industrial Voice of the Customer has three families of sources, and the satisfaction survey is
just **one** of the inputs in the first:

| Direct sources | Indirect sources | Behavioral data |
|---|---|---|
| Interviews | Complaints | Purchase frequency |
| Customer meetings | Returns | Volume purchased |
| Surveys and questionnaires | Nonconformities | Churn |
| Visits | Warranty claims | Order reduction |
| Workshops | Technical support | Average payment term |
| Sales feedback | Customer audits | Repeat complaints |
| Development meetings | Rejections and lost customers | — |

**VoC ≠ satisfaction survey.** The survey is one of the sources; treating it as the only source is
like judging a process's health by Cpk alone, ignoring the control chart.

---

## 4 · Where Lean Manufacturing comes in

Lean has the concept of **customer value**. The central question — *what is value to the
customer?* — completely changes the approach to improvement.

The customer values quality, lead time, availability, price, flexibility. The plant, meanwhile,
has excess inspections, waiting, rework, motion, inventory, and duplicated processes. Lean asks:
*which of these activities actually add value for the customer?* — and VoC is what answers that
question, not the intuition of whoever designed the process.

### Example

The customer says: *"I need to receive the product in 5 days."* The current process is:

**Order → wait → scheduling → wait → production → inspection → wait → shipping**, with a lead
time of 12 days.

The customer isn't interested in knowing the plant has excellent machine utilization. They want
the right product, with quality, on time. VoC defines **what value is**; Lean eliminates the waste
that keeps that value from being delivered. It's the same logic as Chapter 27 (VSM) and Chapter
901 (Makigami) — applied here specifically to the voice of the customer as the starting point.

---

## 5 · Where Six Sigma comes in: VOC → CTQ

Six Sigma formalizes this translation with its own vocabulary: **Voice of Customer → Critical to
Quality**. The customer says *"I want fast delivery"* — subjective. It becomes *"order delivered
within 5 days"* — a **CTQ**. Then: **CTQ → specification → indicator → process capability**,
chaining directly into Chapter 156 (Cp/Cpk).

| VoC | CTQ | Metric |
|---|---|---|
| I want fast delivery | Lead time | ≤ 5 days |
| I want defect-free product | Quality | ≤ 500 ppm |
| I want the correct quantity | Quantity | 100% |
| I want a fast response | Service | ≤ 24 h |
| I want a consistent product | Variability | Cp/Cpk |

This is one of Six Sigma's greatest strengths: **turning a subjective customer need into a
measurable process characteristic.** It's also exactly the definition of CTQ from Chapter 6 — this
chapter shows where the CTQ *comes from*.

---

## 6 · Satisfaction, decomposed

Satisfaction enters the chain later — but it's never a single thing. A customer can be satisfied
with quality and dissatisfied with lead time; satisfied with the product and dissatisfied with
service or price. A single average hides this. A mature satisfaction survey **decomposes the
experience** by dimension:

```plotly
voc-dimensoes
```

The overall average of 7.9 looks comfortable. Decomposed, it reveals that **documentation** and
**lead time** are the weakest dimensions — information the average alone would never give, and
which points exactly to where to investigate first.

> **Satisfaction isn't a synonym for "quality."** It's the sum of several independent ratings, and
> each one can have a completely different root cause.

## 8 · How to turn this into processes inside a manufacturing company

Instead of a single "Customer Satisfaction Procedure" — which ends up too narrow — the recommended
architecture has five distinct, chained processes:

| Process | Objective | Defines |
|---|---|---|
| **P-01 · Voice of the Customer Management** | Identify, collect, analyze, and turn needs and expectations into requirements and opportunities | Sources, responsibilities, frequency, collection, classification, analysis |
| **P-02 · Customer Requirements Management** | Translate VoC into measurable requirements | Specifications, CTQs, acceptance criteria, change management |
| **P-03 · Satisfaction Monitoring** | Measure customer perception | Survey methodology, indicators, sampling, analysis, targets |
| **P-04 · Complaints Management** | Treat the complaint as a structured VoC source, not an isolated incident | Logging, classification, investigation, root cause, CAPA, customer feedback |
| **P-05 · Continuous Improvement** | Convert the previous four processes into sustainable change | Inputs from VoC, satisfaction, complaints, audits, risks and opportunities |

The five feed into **Management Review**, which receives VoC + satisfaction + performance +
complaints + risks + improvements — not each in isolation, but the five cross-referenced. It's ISO
9001 requirement 9.3 (Chapter 93) in practice, not in theory.

---

## 9 · Complaints management: the most wasted VoC source

A complaint isn't *"the customer's problem"* — it's a structured, free source of information about
the Voice of the Customer. The most common mistake is handling it case by case, without
aggregating. A hundred complaints from a packaging plant, aggregated by cause:

```plotly
voc-pareto
```

The Pareto shows what a case-by-case analysis never would: **69% of complaints come from just two
causes** — damaged packaging and delay. Notice the overlap with the chart in section 6: satisfaction
by dimension had already flagged **lead time** as one of the lowest scores. Two completely
different VoC sources — one perceptual (survey), one objective (complaint) — point to the same
problem. That's not a coincidence, it's the system working: when independent sources converge, the
priority becomes obvious.

> **A satisfaction indicator ≠ a performance indicator.** 98% OTIF is performance. "Customer
> satisfied with lead time" is perception. A process can have good performance and poor
> perception — or the reverse, when the customer doesn't notice a real improvement. You always
> need both.

## 10 · NPS, CSAT, and CES: three complementary metrics

Sections 6 and 9 showed *that* satisfaction needs to be decomposed and *that* complaints need to be
aggregated. What's missing are the three instruments industry actually uses to turn "customer
perception" into a number tracked month over month: **NPS**, **CSAT**, and **CES**. They're
complementary, not competing — each answers a different question.

### The three, side by side

| | **NPS** | **CSAT** | **CES** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Full name** | Net Promoter Score | Customer Satisfaction Score | Customer Effort Score |
| **Created by** | Fred Reichheld (Bain & Company), with Satmetrix | No single author — grew out of marketing research from the 1970s-80s; formalized in the ACSI index (Claes Fornell, University of Michigan) | Matthew Dixon, Karen Freeman, and Nicholas Toman (CEB, now Gartner) |
| **When** | 2003, article *"The One Number You Need to Grow,"* *Harvard Business Review* | ACSI launched in 1994; the CSAT question was already common before that | 2010, article *"Stop Trying to Delight Your Customers,"* *Harvard Business Review* |
| **Typical question** | "On a scale of 0 to 10, how likely are you to recommend [company/product] to a colleague?" | "How satisfied are you with [product/service/order]?" | "Did the company make it easy for me to handle my issue?" (CES 2.0, agreement scale) |
| **What it measures** | Loyalty and propensity to recommend — a reading of the **relationship**, not a single episode | Satisfaction with a **specific experience or episode** (a purchase, an interaction, a delivery) | The **effort** the customer had to put in to get something resolved — not whether they were delighted, whether it was easy |

The difference that causes the most confusion: CSAT asks "did you like it?"; CES asks "was it
easy?"; NPS asks "would you recommend us?" A customer might answer yes to all three, or yes to one
and no to the others — and it's exactly that divergence that carries the useful information.

### How each one is calculated

**NPS.** Respondents (0-10 scale) split into three groups:

| Group | Score | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Detractors** | 0-6 | Dissatisfied; can hurt the brand through negative word of mouth |
| **Passives** | 7-8 | Satisfied but indifferent; vulnerable to competitors |
| **Promoters** | 9-10 | Loyal and enthusiastic; actively promote the brand |

$$NPS = \%\text{Promoters} - \%\text{Detractors}$$

The result ranges between **−100** and **+100** and **isn't a percentage**, even though it's
calculated from percentages — it's the difference between two of them. Example: 200 responses, 110
promoters (55%), 60 passives (30%), 30 detractors (15%). $NPS = 55 - 15 = 40$.

**CSAT.** The most common scale is 1-5 (or 1-10); "satisfied" is usually defined as the top two
scores on the scale:

$$CSAT = \frac{\text{number of satisfied responses}}{\text{total number of responses}} \times 100\%$$

Example: 200 responses on a 1-5 scale, of which 150 marked 4 or 5. $CSAT =
150/200 \times 100\% = 75\%$.

**CES.** The CES 2.0 version (2013, the most widely used today) asks for agreement with *"The
company made it easy for me to handle my issue,"* on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7
(strongly agree) — in this version, **higher is better** (easier). The metric is the simple
average of the responses:

$$CES = \frac{\sum \text{effort scores}}{\text{number of respondents}}$$

Example: 50 responses totaling 285 points. $CES = 285/50 = 5.7$ — close to the top of the scale,
indicating a low-effort process.

> **Watch out for the original version of CES.** The 2010 article used a 1-5 scale where 1 = "a
> lot of effort" and 5 = "little effort" (or the reverse, depending on the adaptation) — **always
> confirm the direction of the scale** before comparing CES across different surveys or vendors.
> It's the most common interpretation mistake with this metric.

### When to use each one

| Situation | Recommended metric | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Measure the health of the long-term brand relationship | **NPS** | Captures loyalty, not just the last episode |
| Assess a specific delivery, interaction, or transaction | **CSAT** | A direct question about that episode |
| Assess a support, exchange, or problem-resolution process | **CES** | What predicts post-sale retention is effort, not delight |
| Prioritize among the three, with limited resources | **CES first** | CEB/Gartner studies show that reducing effort has more impact on retention than trying to "delight" the customer |

### Where they fit into what this chapter already covered

NPS, CSAT, and CES **don't replace** the VoC → CTQ → process architecture already built in
sections 1-9 — they're **instruments for measuring perception**, the same role already occupied by
"Perception / Satisfaction" in the closed loop in section 7:

- **CTQ ↔ CES.** A poorly defined CTQ ("fast response") usually shows up first as a low CES — the
  customer feels the friction before the company measures the response time.
- **Satisfaction dimensions (section 6) ↔ CSAT by dimension.** The decomposed satisfaction table
  is, in practice, a CSAT applied to each dimension separately instead of a single question.
- **Complaints Pareto (section 9) ↔ NPS Detractors.** Always ask for the reason behind a 0-6
  score — it automatically becomes input data for the same Pareto.
- **Linkage matrix (section 14) ↔ "Satisfaction" column.** That's exactly where a CSAT or CES
  value per row fits in.

### Evolution over an improvement cycle

```plotly
voc-nps-csat-ces
```

Notice the lag: the improvement (implemented between M4 and M5) shows up **first** in CES — the
customer feels the process is easier almost immediately — **then** in CSAT, and only **after that**
in NPS, which is the slowest metric to react because it measures a relationship built over several
interactions, not a single episode. Judging an improvement project by NPS the following month is
the most common mistake in this area: NPS is the last to rise, not the first.

### Reading them as a KPI

<div class="kpi-row">
<div class="kpi bad"><div class="kpi-k">NPS &lt; 0</div><div class="kpi-v">Critical</div><div class="kpi-s">more detractors than promoters</div></div>
<div class="kpi warn"><div class="kpi-k">NPS 0-30</div><div class="kpi-v">Acceptable</div><div class="kpi-s">typical for most B2B industries</div></div>
<div class="kpi ok"><div class="kpi-k">NPS 30-70</div><div class="kpi-v">Good to excellent</div><div class="kpi-s">above that is uncommon in any sector</div></div>
<div class="kpi ok"><div class="kpi-k">CSAT ≥ 80%</div><div class="kpi-v">Good benchmark</div><div class="kpi-s">varies by sector — always compare against your own historical series</div></div>
</div>

> **Reference ranges are starting points, not universal targets.** The absolute NPS value varies
> enormously by country, sector, and how the question is asked. What matters more than the
> isolated value is the **trend of your own series over time** — which is why all three metrics
> should be tracked on a trend chart (like the one above), with the same discipline of rational
> subgrouping and fixed frequency used for any process indicator (Chapter 66).

**Interpretation cautions — the three most costly mistakes:**

1. **Response bias.** Satisfaction surveys suffer from *non-response bias*: very dissatisfied or
   very satisfied customers respond more; the "neutral and busy" customer stays out of it. A low
   response rate (< 10-15%) demands extra caution before treating the number as representative.
2. **Small sample size per period.** An NPS built on 15 responses in a month swings a lot from pure
   sampling noise — before reacting to a drop, check whether the difference is bigger than the
   natural variation expected for that *n* (the same logic as the control limits from Chapter 67
   applies here, even if informally).
3. **Comparing numbers from surveys with different methodologies.** Changing the scale, the
   question, the channel (email vs. SMS vs. in person), or the timing of the survey (right after
   the purchase vs. 30 days later) changes the number by itself, regardless of any real process
   improvement.

## 11 · Where DMAIC comes in

The customer complains: *"The product arrives late."* Here's the same problem, now run through
DMAIC (Chapters 34 to 38):

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Define** | VoC: "I need to receive it on time." CTQ: OTIF ≥ 98%. |
| **Measure** | Measure lead time, production time, waiting, transport, delays by supplier and by planning. |
| **Analyze** | Finding: 63% of delays come from the wait between production and shipping. |
| **Improve** | Batch-size reduction, leveling, flow changes, kanban, scheduling improvements. |
| **Control** | Weekly OTIF, with limit and target, monitored on a chart. |

The result is the chain **VoC → CTQ → DMAIC → KPI → Control** — an integration that none of the
parts, in isolation, would produce.

---

## 12 · Where PDCA comes in

PDCA closes the system one level above DMAIC — it's the management cycle, not the project cycle:

**Plan** — identify the customer need. **Do** — implement the improvement. **Check** — verify KPI,
satisfaction, complaints, quality, and lead time, all together. **Act** — standardize or correct.
And it starts again.

DMAIC solves *one* specific problem with statistical rigor; PDCA is the heartbeat that keeps the
whole system turning between projects.

## 13 · Full example: packaging plant

The customer says: *"I need visually perfect packaging, delivered on time."*

## 14 · The linkage matrix

A simple, powerful QMS tool: linking, in a single row, the Voice of the Customer to the action
that resolves it.

| VoC | Requirement | CTQ | Process | KPI | Target | Source | Satisfaction | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Defect-free product | Specification | PPM | Production | PPM | < 300 | Complaints/inspection | 9.1 | DMAIC |
| Fast delivery | Agreed lead time | OTIF | Logistics | OTIF | > 98% | ERP | 8.7 | Lean |
| Fast response | Service | Response time | Sales | hours | < 24 h | CRM | 8.4 | PDCA |
| Correct documentation | Documentation requirement | Errors | Quality | % errors | < 1% | Complaints | 8.9 | CAPA |

The matrix creates an explicit link between **customer → quality → process → data → improvement**
— exactly what an auditor looks for when entering through the "How much?" leg of the turtle
diagram (Chapter 155).

## 15 · Six questions, six approaches

| Concept | Main question |
|---|---|
| **ISO 9001** | Are we determining requirements and monitoring customer perception? |
| **VoC** | What does the customer actually need and value? |
| **Lean** | What actually adds value for the customer, and where are we wasting? |
| **Six Sigma** | How do we turn the customer's need into measurable CTQs and reduce variation? |
| **Satisfaction** | How does the customer rate what they received? |
| **Process management** | Which process needs to change to deliver what the customer values? |

The sixth row isn't decorative: without it, the first five stay at diagnosis and never reach real
change.

---

## 16 · The most important point

Avoid building a system where **Satisfaction survey → report → file**. That's bureaucracy. A
mature system is:

**VoC → requirements → CTQ → indicators → processes → performance → satisfaction → gaps → Pareto
→ root cause → Lean / Six Sigma / PDCA → improvement → new standard → new measurement → new VoC.**

That's the QMS's real *feedback loop* — and it's what lets a manufacturing company evolve from a
traditional quality management system into a **data-driven, customer-centered Quality Management
System**, integrating ISO 9001, Lean Manufacturing, Six Sigma, and process management into a
single architecture, instead of four parallel initiatives that never talk to each other.

---

### Common mistakes

1. **Confusing VoC with the satisfaction survey.** The survey is one source among many.
2. **Reporting satisfaction as a single number.** Without decomposing it by dimension, the data
   hides exactly what would be actionable.
3. **Treating a complaint as an incident, not as data.** Without an aggregated Pareto, every
   complaint gets resolved in isolation and the pattern never shows up.
4. **Stopping at the report.** Satisfaction with no link to a Pareto, root cause, and an
   improvement project is paper compliance.
5. **Measuring only performance, or only perception.** High OTIF with low satisfaction (or the
   reverse) is a sign that something isn't being measured — not a contradiction to ignore.
