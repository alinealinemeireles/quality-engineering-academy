## Chapter 98: Reliability and Maintainability

### Predictive and Preventive Maintenance Tools

Maintenance actions or policies can be classified into three broad categories, each suited to a different set of conditions:

**Corrective maintenance** requires no repairs, replacements, or preventive maintenance until failures actually occur, which allows the maximum operating time between repairs. Although this policy maximizes continuous run time, it's neither economical nor efficient, since it can result in a catastrophic failure requiring extensive repair time and cost.

**Preventive maintenance** requires the machine to be maintained on a predetermined schedule, regardless of whether there's an apparent problem. On a scheduled basis, machines are taken out of operation, disassembled, inspected for defective parts, and repaired as needed. This way, actual repair costs can be reduced, but production loss can increase if the machine is complex and requires days or even weeks of maintenance; preventive maintenance can also, paradoxically, create machine problems that didn't exist before the intervention. It's important to note that preventive maintenance is only applicable when two conditions are satisfied simultaneously: first, the cost of repairing the system after it fails must be greater than the cost of maintaining it before failure; second, the system's failure rate function must be monotonically increasing with time. If the system's failure rate is decreasing with time, the system is likely improving naturally, and any preventive action or replacement is a waste of resources; likewise, performing preventive maintenance when the failure rate is constant is inappropriate, since replacing or maintaining the system before failure doesn't affect its probability of failing in the next instant, given that the system is "as good as before" (this is exactly the "memorylessness" property of the exponential distribution, discussed below).

**Predictive maintenance** (also called condition-based, or on-condition, maintenance) is the third policy, and it allows for considerable savings when a machine's failure can be predicted and the machine can be taken off line so that only the necessary repairs are made. Predictive maintenance can also be performed when a machine's failure modes can be identified and monitored with increasing intensity, and when the machine can be shut down at a fixed control limit, before critical failure levels are reached. This policy yields two main benefits: the first is that taking the machine off line at a predetermined time minimizes production loss, since production can be rescheduled around the stoppage; the second is that, since defective components can be predetermined, replacement parts can be ordered and labor scheduled in advance, and monitoring sensors eliminate time spent on diagnostics, reducing total repair time.

For predictive maintenance to work, three tasks need to be accomplished: first, find a condition parameter that describes the machine's state — it can be vibration, sound, temperature, corrosion, crack growth, wear, or lubricant condition; second, monitor that condition parameter and assess the machine's current condition from the measured data; and third, determine the condition parameter's threshold value and its two components, the alarm value and the breakdown value. A machine that reaches the alarm value is a sign it's undergoing intense wear, and at that point the failure's type and stage should be identified to prepare the maintenance procedure; if the machine reaches the breakdown value, it must be shut down for maintenance.

### Reliability and Maintainability Indices

Before discussing the numerical indices, it's useful to recall four fundamental functions derived from traditional calculus that let you quantify reliability: the reliability function R(t), the cumulative failure distribution function F(t), the failure density function f(t) (also called the probability density function, or pdf), and the hazard rate function h(t) (also called the instantaneous failure rate). Suppose N identical components are tested and that, over a time interval t, we observe x failures and (N − x) survivors. The reliability function at t is R(t) = (N − x)/N, that is, the fraction of all components that survived longer than t; the cumulative distribution function is F(t) = x/N, the fraction of units that failed by time t; and the two functions are complementary, so that F(t) + R(t) = 1. The failure density function is the derivative of F(t) with respect to time, and the hazard rate is defined as h(t) = f(t)/R(t) — the number of failures per unit time divided by the number of components still under test per unit time at that instant.

From these functions come the main indices used in reliability and maintainability engineering practice:

**MTTF (Mean Time To Failure)** is the appropriate metric for non-repairable systems — those that, once they fail, are discarded or replaced, not fixed. Estimated MTTF is the arithmetic mean of the observed failure times of n identical units tested. For the particular case of a constant failure rate (the exponential model, discussed below), MTTF is simply the inverse of the failure rate: MTTF = 1/λ.

**MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures)** is the analogous metric for repairable systems — those that, after a failure, are fixed and returned to operation. It's important not to confuse MTTF with MTBF: the former describes the expected time to a non-repairable item's first and only failure, while the latter describes the expected time between successive failures of a system that's repeatedly repaired and put back into service.

**MTTR (Mean Time To Repair)** is defined as the average time needed to repair a failure, not including the time spent waiting for parts or tools to begin the repair. Maintainability, in turn, is defined as the probability that a failed system will be restored to its operating condition within a specified time.

**Availability** is defined as the probability that the system is operating correctly at a given instant t. Steady-state availability, which describes the system's long-run behavior as t tends to infinity, is given by the ratio A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). Note that this formula makes explicit the trade-off between reliability (high MTBF, meaning few failures) and maintainability (low MTTR, meaning fast repairs): a system can achieve high availability either by failing rarely or by being repaired very quickly when it does fail — and, in practice, a good reliability engineering program seeks to improve both sides of this equation simultaneously.

**Failure rate** is another name for the hazard rate function h(t) discussed above — the instantaneous rate at which failures occur in a population of items still in operation at a given instant.

### Reliability Models

**Exponential distribution.** When time to failure is well modeled by an exponential distribution with parameter λ, the cumulative distribution function is F(t) = 1 − e^(−λt), the failure density function (pdf) is f(t) = λe^(−λt), the reliability function is R(t) = e^(−λt), and the resulting hazard rate is constant and equal to h(t) = λ. This is the only reliability model whose failure rate is constant over time, a property known as "memorylessness": the probability of an item surviving a future interval doesn't depend on how long it has already survived. This is exactly the property that explains why preventive maintenance makes no sense when the failure rate is constant — replacing a "good" component with a "new" one doesn't change its probability of failing in the next instant at all. The exponential distribution is the typical model for describing a system's useful-life phase, in which failures occur randomly over time, attributed to the inherent, composite failure rate of the system's components.

**Weibull distribution.** The Weibull failure rate is the most widely used time-dependent failure rate model, and it describes both decreasing and increasing failure rate regions. Its hazard rate is expressed as h(t) = (γ/θ) t^(γ−1), where γ and θ are, respectively, the shape and scale parameters of the two-parameter Weibull distribution. The appeal of the Weibull hazard rate function comes from the fact that it can represent several other well-known functions as particular cases: when γ = 1, the Weibull hazard rate becomes constant, reducing exactly to the exponential model; when γ = 2, the resulting hazard rate is linear in time and its pdf becomes the Rayleigh distribution; and for values of γ around 3.4, the Weibull distribution can be used to approximate the normal distribution. This flexibility — being able to take on a decreasing (γ < 1), constant (γ = 1), or increasing (γ > 1) failure rate by varying a single shape parameter — is what makes Weibull the most widely used distribution in reliability engineering practice for modeling real failure data, far more versatile than the exponential alone. Other probability distributions can also be used to adequately describe failure times, including gamma, beta, log-logistic, lognormal, extreme value, and the normal distribution itself, depending on the physical failure mechanism involved.

### The Bathtub Curve

One of the earliest and best-known failure rate models is the bathtub curve, so named for its characteristic shape. The failure rate over time can be divided into three distinct regions:

The first region is characterized by a **decreasing failure rate with time** and is conventionally called the infant mortality phase, or the product's, component's, or system's early-life region. Experience shows this region lasts approximately 10,000 hours (about a year) for most electronic components. Failures in this region are generally attributed to defects in manufacturing processes, assembly, and product shipping — that is, items with latent manufacturing defects tend to fail early, and once that weak population is eliminated, the failure rate drops.

The bathtub curve's second region is the **constant failure rate region**, characterized by the failure rate inherent to the components making up the product. In this region, failures occur randomly over time, corresponding to the behavior described by the exponential distribution — this is the product's "useful life" phase, in which it operates stably and failures, when they occur, are random, non-systematic events.

The third region is called the **wear-out region**, characterized by an increasing failure rate over time. Most electronic components don't exhibit this region sharply, with the exception of electromechanical devices like relays. Most — if not all — mechanical components subject to rotating and reciprocating motion, on the other hand, do effectively wear out over time, as exemplified by cutting tools' behavior, fatigue loading in structures, and wear from friction between mating surfaces.

The bathtub curve's two extreme regions (infant mortality and wear-out) are described by time-dependent failure rate functions — typically modeled by the Weibull distribution with a shape parameter less than 1 (infant mortality, decreasing rate) or greater than 1 (wear-out, increasing rate) — while the central useful-life region is described by the exponential model's constant failure rate (Weibull with shape parameter equal to 1). It's exactly this regime transition that underlies the practice of environmental stress screening, used to weed out infant-mortality failures ahead of time before the product reaches the customer, as well as the practice of scheduled replacement of components approaching the end of their expected life, once the organization has characterized, using real field data, which region of the bathtub curve that specific component is in.

### Series, Parallel, and Standby Systems

A product is considered a system when it's made up of components connected according to some design rule to produce the product's desired functions. A typical series system is made up of n components (or subsystems) connected end to end, so that any component's failure results in the failure of the entire system; under the assumption of independence between component failures, a series system's reliability is the product of its components' reliabilities, which means a series system's reliability is always lower than the reliability of its "weakest" component. In a parallel system, components or units are connected in parallel, so that the failure of one or more paths still allows the remaining path(s) to function adequately — the system only fails when every unit fails, which means a parallel system's reliability is always higher than that of any of its individual components. "k-out-of-n" systems require that at least k out of a total of n units function for the system to operate adequately, a direct application of the binomial distribution. Standby systems (standby redundancy), meanwhile, differ from "hot" parallel systems because the backup components only come into operation after the main component fails, remaining inactive (and presumably not subject to wear) while they wait — this distinction between active redundancy (hot standby, in which every unit shares the load equally) and standby redundancy (standby proper) is relevant to design decisions aimed at increasing the system's overall reliability without necessarily increasing its ongoing operating cost. The mathematical details of these system configurations, including their combined-reliability calculation formulas, are covered in greater depth in dedicated reliability engineering courses; for CQE certification purposes, what matters is recognizing each architecture's qualitative logic and its relationship to the product's overall robustness.
