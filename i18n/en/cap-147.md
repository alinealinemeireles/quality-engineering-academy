## Chapter 147: Controls, Barriers, Residual Risk, and Effectiveness Monitoring
### The engineering question

> *"The register says the risk is 'low' because we have three controls. Has anyone actually checked
> whether the three of them work?"*

This is the most consequential gap in risk management as practiced. Residual risk gets classified
based on controls whose effectiveness was **never measured**. A control with 40% real effectiveness
and 95% assumed produces a risk classification wrong by more than an order of magnitude — and the
organization operates believing it's protected.

---

## 147.1 Barrier taxonomy

| Dimension | Types | Engineering note |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | Preventive (before the event) / Mitigating (after) | Preventive is always preferable |
| **Nature** | Elimination > Substitution > Engineering > Administrative > PPE | The classic control hierarchy |
| **Activation** | Passive (always present) / Active (requires detection + action) | Passive is far more reliable |
| **Human dependence** | Autonomous / Assisted / Dependent | Effectiveness drops sharply with human dependence |

**The rule that orders everything:** a **passive, engineering** barrier (a chamfer that prevents
reversed assembly) has effectiveness close to 1 and doesn't degrade. An **active, people-dependent**
barrier (an operator who must check and log) typically has effectiveness between 0.7 and 0.95 and
degrades with fatigue, pressure, and turnover.

A Bow-Tie with seven administrative barriers looks protected and isn't.

---

## 147.2 Effectiveness: the quantity that has to be measured

**A barrier's effectiveness** = P(the barrier works | the event it's meant to stop occurred).

**How to measure it, in order of evidence quality:**

1. **Real-occurrence data.** Out of the N times the event occurred, in how many did the barrier stop
   it? It's the best evidence and the rarest — because, fortunately, events are rare.
2. **Challenge test.** Deliberately introducing nonconforming standard parts into the flow and
   measuring the detection rate. It's the most underrated method and the most informative. An
   attribute MSA study is exactly this, applied to an inspection barrier.
3. **Execution-compliance audit.** Checking whether the barrier is being carried out as defined. It
   measures **availability**, not effectiveness — but availability is an upper bound.
4. **Structured expert judgment**, when there's no alternative. Declared as such, with its
   uncertainty interval.

> **The least-measured and most-assumed quantity in industrial risk management is barrier
> effectiveness.** Nearly every risk register in the world classifies the residual with numbers no
> one ever verified.

---

## 147.3 Residual risk: the correct arithmetic

With **independent** barriers of effectiveness e₁, e₂, …:

$$ P_{\text{residual}} = P_{\text{inherent}} \times \prod_i (1 - e_i) $$

Two mandatory corrections:

**1. Independence.** If the barriers share a cause (same person, same procedure, same power
supply), apply the beta factor from Chapter 144. The naive product underestimates the residual.

**2. Availability × effectiveness.** A barrier that exists but is unavailable 20% of the time has
effective effectiveness e_effective = availability × conditional_effectiveness. Barriers "under
review," "with a broken sensor," or "whose procedure no one has read" have availability well below
1.

---

## 147.4 Continuous effectiveness monitoring

A barrier isn't a state — it's a process, and it degrades. Monitoring should be designed like any
other control:

| Barrier | Effectiveness indicator | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| SPC | % of alarms investigated within 24 h; % of charts with active rules | Weekly |
| Visual inspection | Detection rate in a challenge test with standard parts | Quarterly |
| Poka-yoke | Documented functional check (does the device still work?) | Per shift / setup |
| Measurement system | %GRR, ndc, calibration current | Annual / on change |
| Layered audit | % of audits performed; repeated findings | Monthly |
| AI model | PSI, calibration, performance on a labeled sample | Continuous (Ch. 138) |

**The poka-yoke test almost no one runs:** periodically checking, with a deliberately bad part, that
the device still detects it. A broken poka-yoke is indistinguishable from a working one if it's
never challenged — and the organization keeps counting on it in the risk register.

---

## 147.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Assuming effectiveness without measuring | Residual risk underestimated by orders of magnitude | Challenge test; occurrence data |
| Counting barriers instead of weighing them | Seven weak barriers look like strong protection | Multiply (1−eᵢ) with real values |
| Ignoring availability | An unavailable barrier counts as fully present | e_effective = availability × effectiveness |
| Non-independent barriers treated as independent | Residual underestimated | Beta factor; diversify |
| Poka-yoke never challenged | A broken device with no one aware | Periodic functional check |
| Relying on administrative barriers | They degrade with pressure and turnover | Prefer engineering and passive barriers |
| Not reassessing after a change | Barrier designed for a different context | Reassess on every process change |

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Measured versus assumed effectiveness.**
*The register classifies a risk as "low" based on three controls. What questions do you ask?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Six questions, in order, and most registers fail at the very first one:

1. **What's each control's effectiveness, and how was it measured?** If the answer is "we assumed
   95%," the residual risk has no basis. Ask for the evidence: occurrence data, a challenge test, or
   at least structured judgment declared as such.

2. **What's the availability?** Is the control operational 100% of the time? Are there shifts when
   it's not carried out? Periods when the equipment was down? Effective effectiveness is the
   product.

3. **Are the three controls independent?** Do they share a person, procedure, training, power
   supply, equipment vendor? If so, they aren't three barriers.

4. **What type of barrier is each one?** Three administrative barriers don't equal one passive
   engineering barrier. The table in section 147.1 orders them.

5. **When was it last verified?** A poka-yoke never challenged could have been broken for months,
   and the organization keeps counting on it.

6. **What escalation factors exist?** (Chapter 144) — what would make each control fail? Missing
   training, overload, deferred maintenance, turnover. Did those factors occur last quarter?

**The code cell quantifies the cumulative effect, and it's multiplicative:** using **measured**
instead of assumed effectiveness changes the residual risk by a factor of ~464; adding the **common
cause** among the human barriers multiplies it by a further ~1.9. The typical risk register is wrong
by three orders of magnitude — and always wrong in the same, optimistic, direction. It isn't a
marginal difference: it's the difference between "low" and "unacceptable."

</details>

**E2 — Availability.**
*A poka-yoke has 99% effectiveness but was disabled 15% of the time last quarter. What's the
effective effectiveness?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

e_effective = 0.85 × 0.99 = **0.84**

The residual risk goes from (1−0.99) = 0.01 to (1−0.84) = **0.16** — **16 times higher**.

**Why poka-yokes get disabled**, and each reason demands a different response:

- **False positives** — the device blocks good parts and the operator disables it to keep
  producing. It's the most common case and the most serious, because the disabling is effectively
  permanent. The fix is to correct the device, not reprimand the operator.
- **Maintenance** — disabled during an intervention and not reactivated. Fix: include reactivation
  in the startup checklist.
- **Product change** — doesn't work with the new variant. Fix: include a poka-yoke check in the
  engineering-change process.
- **Undetected failure** — the device isn't blocking anything and no one knows. Fix: periodic
  functional check with a standard part.

**What to do, in practice:**

1. **Measure availability** — a log of deactivations, or better, automatic monitoring of the
   device's state.
2. **Include availability in the residual calculation** and reclassify the risk.
3. **Treat the deactivation as a nonconformance of the control system**, with corrective action on
   the cause.
4. **Make deactivation hard and visible** — require authorization, generate a log, trigger an alarm.

**The underlying point:** a control that can be disabled for convenience isn't an engineering
barrier — it's an administrative barrier in disguise, and should be classified as such in the
Bow-Tie.

</details>

**E3 — Challenge test.**
*Design a test to measure the effectiveness of a visual inspection.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Test design:**

1. **Prepare standard parts** with representative defects: at the acceptance limits (marginal), of
   the most frequent types, and of the most severe ones. They must be indistinguishable from normal
   parts in every other respect.
2. **Introduce them into the normal flow**, randomly in time and unannounced. If the inspector knows
   they're being tested, you measure peak performance, not the usual one (Hawthorne effect).
3. **Record** the detection rate by defect type and severity.
4. **Repeat periodically** — effectiveness degrades with fatigue, turnover, and mix changes.

**Sizing** (the table in the cell): with 20 parts, the confidence interval has ~0.35 width — useless
for a decision. For an actionable estimate, **50 to 100 parts** per relevant defect type.

**Three details that distinguish a well-run test:**

- **Stratify by defect type.** Overall effectiveness hides that one type is detected 95% of the
  time and another 40%. It's the most useful information the test produces.
- **Also measure false positives.** Introduce good parts and check whether they're rejected. An
  inspector who rejects everything has 100% detection and is useless — the same problem as the 0.5
  threshold in Chapter 138.
- **Ethical and management considerations.** The test measures the **system**, not the person. It
  should be communicated as such, with the results used to improve the procedure, lighting, visual
  aids, and training — never for individual evaluation. A test used punitively stops producing true
  information the second time around.

**Link to MSA:** this test is, formally, an **attribute measurement system study** (Chapter 61).
With several inspectors and repeats, you compute kappa and agreement with the standard — and get,
at the same time, the barrier's effectiveness and the measurement system's capability.

</details>

**E4 — Prioritizing reinforcement.**
*With four barriers and budget to reinforce one, which do you choose?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The cell computes it, and the criterion is **return per euro invested**, not isolated effectiveness.

**The method:**

1. Compute the current annual expected loss (with **measured** effectiveness and availability).
2. For each barrier, simulate a **realistic** reinforcement — not "raise it to 100%," but what the
   planned investment actually achieves.
3. Recompute the expected loss.
4. Rank by (annual savings) / (reinforcement cost).

**The result overturns common intuition.** In this example, the highest ROI belongs to the
**poka-yoke** — which is already the **most** effective barrier (0.97). It wins because reinforcing
it costs only €8,000. The shipping audit, which has the **worst** measured effectiveness (0.45),
comes in third, because reinforcing it costs 1.5 times more.

ROI depends on **three** factors, and none of them can be read by intuition:
- **how much risk reaches** that barrier (depends on its position in the chain);
- **how much the reinforcement improves it**;
- **how much the reinforcement costs**.

**Three considerations the pure calculation doesn't capture, and that should enter the decision:**

1. **Diversity.** Reinforcing a barrier that shares a common cause with others pays off less than
   the independent calculation suggests. Sometimes, **adding** a barrier of a different nature pays
   off more than reinforcing an existing one — because it reduces the effective β.
2. **Durability.** An engineering improvement (poka-yoke) holds; an administrative improvement
   (training) degrades within 6-12 months without reinforcement. The 3-year ROI ranks differently
   than the 1-year ROI.
3. **Effect on other risks.** A barrier may protect against several risks in the register. Computing
   it per isolated risk underestimates its total value.

**The methodological conclusion, which is the whole chapter's conclusion:** this analysis is only
possible because the effectiveness values were **measured**. With assumed effectiveness, every
barrier looks nearly perfect, the calculated savings of any reinforcement is tiny, and the result is
always "not worth investing" — which is exactly the wrong conclusion, produced by made-up data.

</details>

### Note: why measured risk and felt risk diverge (Slovic)

The whole chapter up to this point has been about measuring the real effectiveness of controls. It's worth noting, if only briefly, a complementary point that industrial risk management frequently overlooks: **how people perceive a risk rarely matches its measured statistical magnitude.**

Paul Slovic's classic work on the "psychometric paradigm" of risk perception shows that two factors dominate perception, regardless of the actual probability:

- **"Dread"** — how much the risk is perceived as catastrophic, uncontrollable, with fatal consequences distributed involuntarily (e.g., an explosion at a chemical plant vs. the same expected number of injuries spread across the year in commuting accidents).
- **"Unknown risk"** — how much the risk is new, not directly observable, and poorly understood scientifically by the affected public.

**Why this matters to someone managing quality risk.** A residual risk correctly calculated as "low" (in this chapter's terms) can still trigger a strong reaction from employees, customers, or regulators if it scores high on dread or unfamiliarity — and, conversely, statistically relevant but familiar risks (e.g., cuts from hand tools) tend to be underestimated by the people who live with them daily. Effective risk communication — to the plant, to the customer, to the regulator — needs to treat these two dimensions (measured magnitude and perception) as **distinct problems**, each with its own strategy: the first is solved with data and effective controls; the second is solved with transparency, context, and involving the affected people in the assessment itself.
