## Chapter 24: SMED: Single-Minute Exchange of Die
### The engineering question

*How much of the setup time could happen while the machine is still running?*

SMED (*Single-Minute Exchange of Die*, Shigeo Shingo) isn't a "work faster" technique. It's a reclassification of activities. The gain comes from moving work from inside to outside the stoppage window — before trying to speed up anything at all.

### The fundamental distinction

| | **Internal** setup | **External** setup |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Can only be done with the machine stopped | Can be done while the machine is running |
| Examples | Unbolting and removing the die, centering the tool | Fetching the die, preheating, prepping bolts, checking the order |
| Effect on downtime | Counts | Doesn't count |

**Shingo's empirical observation:** in most changeovers that have never been analyzed, **30 to 50% of the downtime is taken up by activities that are external by nature** — hunting for tools, fetching material, waiting for the forklift, checking paperwork. These convert with zero investment.

### The Four Stages

**Stage 0 — Measure.** Film the entire changeover, from the last good part to the next good part. Time every activity. This is the one point in SMED where most teams fail: without film, the discussion runs on opinions.

**Stage 1 — Separate.** Classify each activity as internal or external. Just classify. Typically cuts 20–30% just from organization (prep checklist, pre-assembled setup cart, tools at the point of use).

**Stage 2 — Convert.** Turn internal into external. Die preheating, interchangeable cassettes, pre-set jigs, duplicating low-cost components, standardizing die heights to eliminate centering.

**Stage 3 — Streamline.** Only now do you speed up what's left over as internal: quick fasteners (quarter-turn clamps instead of bolts), eliminating adjustments in favor of stops and scales, parallel operations with two operators, eliminating bolting wherever possible.

> **Shingo's rule on bolts:** a bolt only tightens on the last turn. Every turn before that is waste. Clamps, cams, and pins do the same job in a fraction of the time.

**On adjustments.** In changeovers no one has worked on, *trial-and-adjust* (producing parts until it's right) is usually the largest share — and it's the easiest to eliminate conceptually: replace adjustment with **deterministic positioning** (stops, graduated scales, values logged per part number). An adjustment is an admission that the correct position isn't known.

### Worked example: analyzing a real changeover

```py-r
--- python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

atividades = pd.DataFrame([
    # (atividade, tempo_s, classificacao_atual, potencial)
    ("Última peça boa -> paragem",              30,  "Interno", "Interno"),
    ("Ir buscar o molde ao armazém",           420,  "Interno", "Externo"),
    ("Procurar ferramentas",                   180,  "Interno", "Externo"),
    ("Desapertar 8 parafusos do molde antigo", 300,  "Interno", "Interno-racionalizar"),
    ("Retirar molde antigo (ponte)",           240,  "Interno", "Interno"),
    ("Limpar a base",                          150,  "Interno", "Interno"),
    ("Colocar molde novo (ponte)",             260,  "Interno", "Interno"),
    ("Centrar e alinhar",                      330,  "Interno", "Interno-racionalizar"),
    ("Apertar 8 parafusos",                    320,  "Interno", "Interno-racionalizar"),
    ("Ligar mangueiras de refrigeração",       140,  "Interno", "Interno"),
    ("Aquecer o molde",                        600,  "Interno", "Externo"),
    ("Carregar programa e parâmetros",         120,  "Interno", "Externo"),
    ("Produzir peças de ajuste e afinar",      900,  "Interno", "Interno-racionalizar"),
    ("Aprovação da primeira peça",             180,  "Interno", "Interno"),
], columns=["Atividade", "Tempo_s", "Atual", "Potencial"])

t0 = atividades["Tempo_s"].sum()
print(f"Tempo de setup atual (last good -> first good): "
      f"{t0/60:.1f} min ({t0} s)\n")

# Ganho do estágio 1+2: tudo o que é convertível sai da janela de paragem
externo = atividades.loc[atividades["Potencial"] == "Externo", "Tempo_s"].sum()
t1 = t0 - externo
print(f"Estágio 1+2 — converter para externo: -{externo/60:.1f} min")
print(f"   Setup após conversão: {t1/60:.1f} min  ({1-t1/t0:.0%} de redução, "
      f"sem investimento significativo)\n")

# Estágio 3: racionalizar o que restou de interno
fatores = {"Desapertar 8 parafusos do molde antigo": 0.25,   # grampos rápidos
           "Apertar 8 parafusos": 0.25,
           "Centrar e alinhar": 0.20,                        # batentes/pinos-guia
           "Produzir peças de ajuste e afinar": 0.15}        # parâmetros registados
atividades["Tempo_final_s"] = atividades.apply(
    lambda r: 0 if r["Potencial"] == "Externo"
    else r["Tempo_s"] * fatores.get(r["Atividade"], 1.0), axis=1)
t2 = atividades["Tempo_final_s"].sum()
print(f"Estágio 3 — racionalizar o interno restante:")
print(f"   Setup final: {t2/60:.1f} min  ({1-t2/t0:.0%} de redução total)\n")

print(atividades[["Atividade", "Tempo_s", "Potencial", "Tempo_final_s"]]
      .assign(Tempo_final_s=lambda d: d["Tempo_final_s"].round(0))
      .to_string(index=False))

# --- Impacto no EPEI e no lote económico ---
print("\n--- Consequência no tamanho de lote ---")
tempo_disp_dia_s, procura_dia = 27000, 480
for nome, ts in [("Antes", t0), ("Depois", t2)]:
    # nº de trocas que a capacidade disponível suporta por dia (10% do tempo em setup)
    trocas = (0.10 * tempo_disp_dia_s) / ts
    print(f"{nome:>6}: setup {ts/60:5.1f} min -> "
          f"{trocas:4.1f} trocas/dia possíveis -> "
          f"lote mínimo ≈ {procura_dia/max(trocas,0.01):5.0f} peças")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.5))
ax.barh(["Atual", "Após estágio 1+2", "Após estágio 3"],
        [t0/60, t1/60, t2/60], color=["#a5203a", "#d17a00", "#31803a"])
ax.set_xlabel("Tempo de paragem (min)")
ax.set_title("SMED — redução por estágio")
for i, v in enumerate([t0/60, t1/60, t2/60]):
    ax.text(v + 1, i, f"{v:.0f} min", va="center")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

**Engineering reading.** Notice two things.

First: **the biggest isolated gain came from doing nothing technical** — fetching the die, hunting for tools, heating up, and loading the program are activities that can happen while the machine is running. None of them requires investment.

Second: **SMED's real effect isn't the time saved, it's the lot size that becomes viable.** With nearly 70 minutes of setup, the factory is forced into large lots to dilute the changeover cost — and large lots generate stock, lead time, and late defect detection. By dropping to just over 20 minutes, the minimum economic lot shrinks to about a third and leveling the mix becomes possible. This is the link between SMED, heijunka, and kanban — and the reason SMED has to precede any attempt at heijunka.

### Common mistakes

1. **Starting with stage 3.** Buying quick fasteners before separating internal from external is spending money on the small part of the problem.
2. **Not filming it.** Without recording, the team argues over what it thinks happens.
3. **Measuring from "changeover start" instead of last-good-part to first-good-part.** That excludes the fine-tuning, which is usually the largest share.
4. **Treating adjustment as inevitable.** Adjustment is a lack of deterministic positioning.
5. **Cutting setup time and keeping the lot size the same.** The whole gain sits in idle capacity and no one sees value. SMED only pays for itself once the lot size drops afterward.
