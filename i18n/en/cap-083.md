## Chapter 83: FMECA — Failure Mode, Effects, and Criticality Analysis

### What Criticality Adds to FMEA

When the **criticality** dimension is explicitly built into an FMEA, the tool's name changes to FMECA — Failure Mode, Effects, and Criticality Analysis. This tool's historical origin traces back to the U.S. military standard MIL-STD-1629A, which defined two central concepts that remain in use even after that specific standard was canceled in 1998 (now referenced by replacement and related standards, like AIAG's own FMEA-4, SAE J1739, and the international standard IEC 60812). Per MIL-STD-1629A, **criticality** is "a relative measure of the consequences of a failure mode and its frequency of occurrence," and **criticality analysis** is "a procedure by which each potential failure mode is ranked according to the combined influence of severity and probability of occurrence."

In other words, while traditional FMEA already considers severity and occurrence as two of its three assessment axes (alongside detection), FMECA formalizes and specifically highlights the **severity × occurrence** combination as an independent measure of criticality, deliberately separated from the ability to detect the failure before it happens. This distinction matters conceptually: detection is a characteristic of the organization's current controls — something that can, in theory, be improved quickly through investment in inspection or testing — while criticality (severity × occurrence) is a more intrinsic characteristic of the failure mode and its cause itself, and generally can only be reduced through actual design or process changes. That's why many FMEA prioritization methodologies — as we saw in the chapter on RPN — recommend looking at criticality before looking at the full RPN, precisely because criticality captures the essence of risk more robustly, and is less prone to the distortions RPN can produce when one of the three factors dominates the others.

### The Qualitative Approach

FMECA can be run qualitatively or quantitatively. In the **qualitative** approach, risk is typically categorized into five descriptive levels: frequent, reasonably probable, occasional, remote, or extremely unlikely — a scale reminiscent of the occurrence categories already seen in traditional FMEA, but applied more directly to assessing the failure mode's overall criticality, without necessarily requiring precise numeric failure-rate data. This qualitative approach is especially useful in a project's early phases, when historical reliability data isn't yet available, or in sectors where gathering precise quantitative failure-rate data would be prohibitively expensive or time-consuming.

### The Quantitative Approach

In the **quantitative** approach, failure-rate data, failure-effect probability data, individual part failure data, and operating-time data are needed as input to one or more formal calculation protocols — protocols historically defined, for example, in Military Handbook 217, the classic reference for electronic component failure rates. A failure mode's criticality number (often represented as Cm) is typically calculated as the product of four factors: the part or item's failure rate (λp, generally expressed in failures per million operating hours, or obtained from reliability databases), the failure-mode ratio (α, the proportion of the item's total failure rate attributable to that specific failure mode), the conditional probability of the failure's effect (β, the probability that, given the failure mode occurred, it actually produces the critical effect under consideration), and the operating time (t, the mission duration or relevant operating period). The result, Cm = λp × α × β × t, provides a numeric measure directly comparable across different failure modes, allowing an objective priority ranking — and is often combined with the Poisson distribution to estimate the probability of a given number of failures occurring within a specified operating interval, when failures follow an approximately random, constant process over time. It's worth noting this quantitative treatment rests on reliability concepts — failure rate, time to failure — explored in greater depth in this manual's unit dedicated to reliability and maintainability.

### The Criticality Matrix

A FMECA's central output is a **criticality matrix**, which ranks potential failures against their severity and, typically, their probability of occurrence, visually identifying a prioritization scheme for corrective action. On this matrix, failures plotted further from the origin — that is, with higher combinations of severity and probability — represent higher potential risks and therefore demand more urgent corrective action. A classic, instructive example of this application, cited by Burke and Silvestrini, is the FMECA of a traveling lawn sprinkler: for each product component — the worm bearing, the bearing shaft, the thrust washer, among others — the team lists the possible failure, its cause, the failure type (mechanical, hydraulic, wear, or customer misuse), a numeric estimate of occurrence probability, a severity estimate for the effect on the product, the actual effect, and finally, proposed improvement alternatives. This kind of table, systematically applied to every component of a product, is what actually builds the final criticality matrix.

Given its historical roots in military and aerospace standards, FMECA remains especially relevant — and, in many cases, contractually required — in sectors where the consequences of a critical failure can be catastrophic and involve intense regulatory scrutiny: aerospace, defense, nuclear energy, and, increasingly, high-risk medical devices. In these contexts, explicitly formalizing criticality — going beyond what a traditional RPN-based or Action-Priority-table-based FMEA normally offers — provides an additional level of analytical rigor and documented traceability, often required by sector-specific standards and regulatory agencies.
