## Chapter 1: Quality Philosophies and Foundations

### A Quick Overview of the Quality Gurus

Since these pioneers are already discussed in greater depth elsewhere in this manual, what's warranted here is just a quick-reference summary, placing each one's specific contribution.

**Walter A. Shewhart** is considered the father of statistical process control: his invention of the control chart, in 1924, provided for the first time a scientific, economically justifiable basis for distinguishing common causes from special causes of variation — the fundamental distinction on which all of SPC is built.

**W. Edwards Deming**, Shewhart's intellectual disciple, became the name most associated with Japan's post-war quality transformation. His central diagnosis was that about 85% of quality problems originate in the system (that is, are management's responsibility) and only 15% are attributable to individual workers — which is why he insisted real change could only come from leadership. This philosophy was synthesized in his famous 14 points for management, which range from creating constancy of purpose and driving out fear in the workplace to abolishing arbitrary numerical targets and instituting a vigorous program of education and retraining.

**Joseph M. Juran** complemented Deming by teaching Japanese managers the language and techniques of managing for quality. Juran is the author of the quality trilogy, a model of three interrelated managerial processes: quality planning (the annual process of institutionalizing control and management review), quality control (monitoring techniques aimed at correcting sporadic problems, analogous to handling special causes), and quality improvement (a structured breakthrough sequence for solving chronic problems, analogous to handling common causes). Juran also popularized the concept of the "vital few" (originally "trivial many"), applying the Pareto principle to quality, and defined quality as "fitness for use" — in direct contrast to Philip Crosby's definition.

**Philip Crosby** popularized the concept of zero defects and defined quality as "conformance to requirements," a more operational, less subjective view than Juran's. Crosby founded the Crosby Quality College to spread his methodology.

**Kaoru Ishikawa**, a Japanese engineer who helped organize Deming's seminars in Japan, is known for two lasting legacies: the cause-and-effect diagram (also called the Ishikawa diagram or fishbone diagram) and the creation of quality circles, groups of workers who voluntarily meet to identify and solve quality problems in their own work area.

**Armand V. Feigenbaum** coined the term "total quality control" and published the first edition of his book of the same name in 1951, championing the idea — revolutionary at the time — that quality is the responsibility of the entire organization, not just the quality control department.

Beyond these six central names, a few other notable contributors are worth a quick mention: Genichi Taguchi, who developed distinctive design-of-experiments methods for building robust systems; Eliyahu Goldratt, creator of the theory of constraints; and George Box, Eugene Grant, Frank Gryna, and Dorian Shainin, among others, who left important contributions to statistical and continuous-improvement methods.

> **Context note.** Percentages like "85%" or "94%" attributed to Deming are pedagogical maxims about systemic responsibility, not universal estimates to be applied without data. Use them to discuss the influence of management and the system, not to dispense with investigating causes.

### Continuous Improvement Methodologies in Context

In the decades following World War II, several structured continuous-improvement methodologies emerged, each incorporating its own set of quality tools. Statistical process control (SPC) remains one of the central areas of statistical technology applied to quality, with the control chart as its main tool — a subject covered in detail in this manual's unit dedicated to statistics and SPC. Total quality management (TQM) is a structured approach, built on the principles of Feigenbaum, Deming, and Juran, for managing quality improvement across the whole organization; it peaked in the 1980s and early 1990s, before losing ground to lean and Six Sigma. Goldratt's theory of constraints (TOC) starts from the premise that a complex system is limited by a small number of constraints, and that effective management consists of identifying those constraints and elevating them — an idea popularized in his business novel *The Goal*. Lean, whose name points to simplicity ("doing the work in the leanest way possible"), originated as lean manufacturing but today applies to practically any service sector, with just-in-time (JIT) as one of its most characteristic examples. Finally, Six Sigma combines effective communication, organization of effort, financial accountability, and robust statistical techniques to produce sustained improvements over time, and today dominates much of quality practice at large organizations. Since these methodologies — TQM, lean, Six Sigma, DMAIC, and the basic quality tools — are already covered in depth elsewhere in this manual, this chapter doesn't repeat them; what matters here is how they fit into the profession's historical evolution and how they relate to one another.
