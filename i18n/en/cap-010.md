## Chapter 10: Leadership Principles and Techniques

### Developing, building, and organizing teams

Since around 1980, quality and teamwork concepts have walked hand in hand: today teamwork is considered a core management component across practically any sector — government, space exploration, healthcare, education, and, of course, for-profit business. Compared to a generation ago, control of a project or process has shifted from the lone individual to the team level. A team-based environment might be born as part of a formal strategic plan, or as a response to a specific problem the organization faces; either way, there should be a structured process for planning and running this team-based initiative, generally through a steering committee. A member of management — the sponsor — typically takes responsibility for launching and guiding each team; this is often the person with authority over the process or area the team is working on.

There are three main types of team widely used in practice. The process improvement team is temporary, tasked with developing a new process or improving an existing one, is often cross-functional (bringing together representatives from various departments involved in the process in question), and disbands at the project's end. The work group is made up of staff who work permanently in a given area or department, tasked with continuously monitoring and improving the process's performance — it generally meets regularly (for example, weekly) to review indicators and set actions; the work group's leader is often the person who already has supervisory responsibility over that area. Organizations committed to top-down improvement, backed by work groups, often adopt a linking team structure, in which each level of the hierarchy — facility, department, unit, process — has its own team, and each team leader also sits on the team at the level immediately above, creating a continuous chain of vertical communication. Finally, the self-directed work team (SDWT) is a group of individuals with broader, deeper responsibility for the day-to-day management and improvement of their process area, highly trained in topics like quality, safety, maintenance, and scheduling, and with considerable autonomy to make their own decisions, albeit within certain limits, like spending authority.

Selecting team members should factor in, beyond the person's direct involvement in the process to be improved, elements like personality style (avoiding overly homogeneous teams, for example, as measured by instruments like the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator), possibly including someone from outside the area to bring a more objective view, and involving suppliers or customers when their input would be especially valuable. A team's success also depends on adequate support mechanisms: physical space and equipment (or virtual collaboration tools, for remote teams), training in the needed skills (planning and running meetings, data analysis, consensus-based decision-making), an active management sponsor willing to resolve cross-departmental conflicts, and broader systemic changes — after all, dropping a team into an organization that wasn't designed for this way of working is a recipe for failure; if the prevailing management style is autocratic and people are rewarded for competition rather than cooperation, teams are unlikely to be an effective mechanism.

Every new team is, in a sense, a newly formed mini-organization, and so it tends to progress (and, occasionally, regress) through the classic group development stages described by Tuckman. In the forming stage, members bring their individual identity and particular perspective, still unsure of how they'll behave in the new situation; the team generally clarifies its mission and defines roles and norms of acceptable behavior. In the storming stage, members grasp the real scope of the challenge, still think primarily as individuals, and try to shape decisions in their own favor; clashes, tests of the leader's authority, and attempts to alter the team's mission are typical. In the norming stage, the focus shifts from personal concerns to the collective challenge; interpersonal conflicts and outside loyalties lose force as members recognize their interdependence, becoming more willing to discuss differences of opinion in order to understand them. In the performing stage, the team matures to the point of functioning as a cohesive, fluid unit, with a good mutual understanding of strengths and weaknesses, able to handle internal conflict well, and making significant progress toward its goals. A fifth stage, adjourning, was added later to the original model and represents the team's dissolution — a moment that often combines satisfaction over what was achieved with a certain sadness over the "death of the team." It's worth noting these stages aren't strictly linear: a team can regress from a more advanced stage to an earlier one if assumptions about other members turn out to be wrong, or if the team's composition changes (for example, due to a member's transfer).

### Leading quality initiatives

The QE is often called on to lead specific quality initiatives — improving an existing product or service, solving supplier performance problems, addressing field failures, implementing new measurement technology, or getting an ISO 9001 certification, for example. Regardless of whether the project involves a formal team, a few recommendations apply fairly broadly: make sure the project's mission is clear, including expected outcomes, deadlines, limitations, and reporting structure; identify the other stakeholders involved in the project and know their level of interest and commitment; define the technical process and timeline to be followed (for example, a structured seven-step problem-solving process, or a Six Sigma project's own DMAIC); execute the project according to that process, keeping management informed; and evaluate the results achieved against the original mission, making sure everyone involved gets proper recognition.

From the purely leadership-quality standpoint — regardless of the specific technique used — a few additional guidelines apply: make sure everyone understands the mission, the goals, and how the team fits into the bigger picture; recognize that every person and every area involved has their own priorities, perspectives, and skills, and look for ways to integrate them effectively; be aware of your own strengths and weaknesses as a leader, and look for ways to offset the weaknesses by drawing on others' skills, while also offering opportunities for others to develop new competencies; understand each team member's strengths, weaknesses, and personality; communicate constantly and clearly, since, in the absence of information, people tend to fill the gaps with their own biases or fears; and lead by example, emphasizing and practically demonstrating the importance of high-quality work.
