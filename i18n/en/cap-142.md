## Chapter 142: Designing Sampling Plans and Statistical Risk
### The engineering question

> *"I don't want to look up a table. I want to design the decision rule that minimizes my total
> cost, given what I know about the process and what each type of error costs."*

Chapter 141 covered the standard. This chapter covers the **design**: how to build and compare
plans — single, double, sequential, by variables — and how to choose based on **expected cost**,
not habit.

---

## 142.1 A scheme's performance metrics

Beyond the OC curve, three metrics describe what happens when the scheme runs continuously:

**AOQ — Average Outgoing Quality.** The average quality that actually ships, assuming rejected lots
are 100% inspected and the NC found are replaced:

$$ AOQ(p) \approx p \cdot P_a(p) \cdot \frac{N-n}{N} $$

**AOQL — Average Outgoing Quality Limit.** The **maximum** of AOQ over every p. It's the strongest
guarantee a rectifying scheme offers: *whatever happens to the supplier's process, the average
outgoing quality never exceeds the AOQL.*

The shape of the AOQ curve is telling: it rises (more NC comes in, still gets accepted), reaches a
maximum, and drops again (the scheme starts rejecting nearly everything, and 100% inspection cleans
the lots up).

**ATI — Average Total Inspection.** The average number of parts inspected per lot:

$$ ATI(p) = n + (1 - P_a(p))(N - n) $$

It grows from n (everything accepted) to N (everything rejected and 100% inspected). It's the
**cost** metric.

---

## 142.2 Single, double, multiple, and sequential plans

| Type | How it works | Typical ASN vs. single |
|---|---|---|
| **Single** | One sample n, accept if x ≤ c | Reference (100%) |
| **Double** | Sample n₁; decide or draw n₂ | ~75-90% |
| **Multiple** | Up to 5-7 successive samples | ~60-75% |
| **Sequential (SPRT)** | Decides part by part | Varies a lot: ~50-85% when quality is clearly good or bad; **can exceed 100%** near the indifference point (see the code table) |

**The trade-off:** on average fewer parts inspected — but **not always**, and that's the subtlety
the table above hides. It adds operational complexity and variability in effort. The **ASN**
(Average Sample Number) is the metric to compare, and it depends on p — sequential plans save a lot
when quality is clearly good or clearly bad, and save little in the middle zone.

**Sequential probability ratio test (SPRT).** After each part, the cumulative likelihood ratio is
computed and compared against two limits derived from α and β:

$$ A = \frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}, \qquad B = \frac{\beta}{1-\alpha} $$

Accept if the ratio ≤ B, reject if ≥ A, continue between the two. In practice, acceptance and
rejection lines are used as a function of the number inspected.

---

## 142.3 Sampling by variables

When the characteristic is **measured** (not just classified), sampling by variables achieves the
same protection with **substantially smaller samples** — in this chapter's example, reductions of
46% to 76% (that is, between ~1/2 and ~1/4 the size), depending on the required operating ratio.

The procedure (s-method, single upper limit):

1. Measure n parts; compute x̄ and s.
2. Compute Z = (USL − x̄)/s.
3. Accept if Z ≥ k, where k is the plan's acceptance constant.

**The price to pay, and it's substantial:**

- **It requires normality**, and is sensitive to deviations — far more than attribute sampling.
  Checking the distribution isn't optional.
- **One characteristic per plan.** With five characteristics, five plans.
- It requires a **capable measurement system** (validated MSA). If measurement variation is large,
  the sample-size advantage evaporates.
- It's **harder to explain** to whoever operates it, and easier to apply wrong.

---

## 142.4 The choice criterion: total expected cost

The correct decision doesn't come from any table. It comes from:

$$ \text{Cost}(p) = \underbrace{ATI(p) \cdot c_{insp}}_{\text{inspection}} + \underbrace{N \cdot AOQ(p) \cdot c_{failure}}_{\text{NC that pass}} + \underbrace{(1-P_a(p)) \cdot c_{rejection}}_{\text{disruption}} $$

Integrated over the plausible distribution of p (obtained from history or from the Bayesian
posterior of Chapter 136), this gives the scheme's expected cost. Comparing schemes by this
criterion is what turns sampling from a ritual into an economic engineering problem.

**The result is almost always the same, and it's worth anticipating it:** when c_failure is high and
p isn't very low, **no sampling plan is economically adequate.** The answer is to improve the
process or automate 100% verification — not to optimize the plan.

---

## 142.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing the plan out of habit or from a table | Total cost not optimized | Compare by expected cost |
| Ignoring ATI when comparing plans | You compare protection without comparing cost | Evaluate OC and ATI together |
| Using sampling by variables without checking normality | Real protection very different from the nominal one | Test the distribution; use attributes if it fails |
| Applying a variables plan to several characteristics with a single sample | Overall risk not controlled | One plan per characteristic |
| Confusing AOQL with a per-lot guarantee | Wrong expectation | AOQL is an outgoing average, not a per-lot limit |
| Sequential sampling with no operational discipline | The rule gets abandoned in practice | Assess feasibility before adopting |
| Optimizing the plan instead of improving the process | Managing the symptom | Look at total cost; compare against improving p |

def asn_duplo(p, n1, ac1, re1, n2, ac2):
    """Amostra 1: aceita se <=ac1, rejeita se >=re1, senao tira a 2a amostra."""

def sprt_simular(p, aql, ltpd, alfa=0.05, beta=0.10, n_max=400, n_sim=4000, rng=rng):
    """SPRT de Wald para proporcao (vectorizado). Devolve (ASN, P(aceitar))."""
    A = np.log((1 - beta) / alfa)
    B = np.log(beta / (1 - alfa))

    para_rejeitar = cum >= A

        tem = m.any(axis=1)
        idx = np.where(tem, m.argmax(axis=1), n_max - 1)
        return tem, idx

                     np.where(tr, ir, n_max - 1)) + 1
    aceita = np.where(ta & (~tr | (ia <= ir)), True,
                      np.where(tr, False, cum[:, -1] < (A + B) / 2))
    return float(n_par.mean()), float(aceita.mean())

def plano_variaveis(aql, ltpd, alfa=0.05, beta=0.10):
    """Plano por variaveis, sigma desconhecido, limite unico (aproximacao)."""
    z_aql, z_ltpd = stats.norm.ppf(1 - aql), stats.norm.ppf(1 - ltpd)

    n = ((z_a + z_b) / (z_aql - z_ltpd)) ** 2 * (1 + k ** 2 / 2)
    return int(np.ceil(n)), k

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — AOQL as a guarantee.**
*The customer asks: "what guarantee does your sampling scheme give me?" How do you answer?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**The wrong answer:** "AQL 1.0%." That's the **plan's reference level**, not a guarantee.

**The correct answer**, if there's rectification (rejected lots are 100% inspected and NC replaced):

> *"With the rectifying scheme in place, the average outgoing quality doesn't exceed the AOQL of X
> PPM, whatever the incoming process quality is. This is the worst case of the outgoing average,
> reached when incoming quality is around Y%."*

The code cell computes X and Y for the plan n=125, Ac=3, N=2000.

**The three caveats that must accompany the statement:**

1. **It's an average over the series**, not a per-lot guarantee. An individual lot can be worse.
2. **It depends on rectification.** If rejected lots are returned instead of 100% inspected, the
   AOQL concept doesn't apply — the guarantee disappears.
3. **It assumes effective inspection.** If the 100% inspection is 90% effective (which attribute
   MSA often reveals), the real outgoing quality is worse than the computed AOQL.

**If the customer wants a PER-LOT guarantee**, the instrument is the **LQ from ISO 2859-2**, and the
wording is: *"any lot with quality equal to or worse than the LQ has at most a 10% probability of
being accepted."* It's a different, per-lot stronger statement, and usually more expensive.

</details>

**E2 — Choosing between single and sequential.**
*The test is destructive and costs €85 per part. Which type of plan do you prefer?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Sequential (SPRT)**, and the justification is economic and direct.

With an expensive destructive test, every part saved is worth €85. But the answer requires looking
at the cell's table, not assuming sequential always saves.

The SPRT's ASN has a **U shape** as a function of p: it saves when quality is clearly good (~68
parts at p = 0.5%, versus 80 for the fixed plan) and clearly bad (~38 parts at p = 8%), but
**inspects more** in the middle zone (~114 parts at p = 2%).

**The decision depends on where the supplier's process usually sits.** For a supplier stable at
0.5%, the savings are ~12 parts/lot = **€1,020/lot**. For a supplier oscillating around 2%,
sequential **costs €2,890/lot more**. Without knowing the historical distribution of p, adopting
sequential is a bet.

**The conditions that must be checked before adopting it:**

1. **Operational feasibility.** Parts must be testable **one at a time**, with the decision made
   after each test. If the lab processes in batches of 20, sequential doesn't work.
2. **Stopping discipline.** The rule has to be followed every time. The temptation to "do two more
   to be sure" invalidates the nominal α and β risks.
3. **Truncation limit.** Without truncation, the SPRT can theoretically continue indefinitely in the
   middle zone. Define n_max (typically 2-3× the single-plan n) and a decision rule at that point.
4. **Auditable record.** Every continue/stop decision has to be documented, with the cumulative
   statistic's value.

**An alternative worth considering first:** if the characteristic is **measurable** (and in a
destructive test it usually is — strength, torque, burst pressure), a **variables plan** reduces
the sample even further than sequential, and can be combined with it. Checking normality then
becomes the critical step.

</details>

**E3 — Variables and normality.**
*A variables plan requires n = 20 instead of n = 80. The engineer adopts it. What checks are
missing?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Five, and none are optional:

**1. Normality — the most critical one.** The variables plan estimates the out-of-spec fraction
from x̄ and s, **extrapolating into the tail** of the normal distribution. If the real distribution
is skewed or has a heavier tail, the tail estimate is wrong — and the tail is exactly what matters.
The code cell quantifies this error. Check with ≥ 100 historical observations (Anderson-Darling,
probability plot), not with the plan's 20.

**2. Measurement system.** The plan uses the mean and standard deviation of the **measurements**,
which include the measurement system's variation. If %GRR is high, s is inflated, Z ends up
smaller, and the plan rejects good lots. With attributes, the problem exists but is of a different
nature. **Validated MSA is a prerequisite.**

**3. One characteristic per plan.** If the product has 4 critical characteristics, that's 4 plans
and 4 decisions — and the overall risk of accepting a lot with at least one bad characteristic isn't
each plan's α. With attributes, a single inspection covers every type of nonconformance.

**4. Process stability.** Sampling by variables assumes the sample comes from a single, stable
distribution. If the lot mixes two setups, the distribution is bimodal, s is inflated, and the plan
behaves unpredictably.

**5. Contractual agreement.** If the customer specified ISO 2859-1 (attributes), swapping in ISO
3951 (variables) requires formal agreement. They aren't equivalent and can't be swapped
unilaterally.

**Practical recommendation:** adopt variables when (a) normality is demonstrated with historical
data, (b) MSA is validated, (c) there's one dominant characteristic, and (d) the savings justify the
added complexity. Otherwise, **attributes is the robust choice** — it works with no distributional
assumptions, which is its main advantage and the reason for its staying power.

</details>

**E4 — Total cost.**
*Analyze the two expected-cost tables from the cell (homogeneous process and mixed process). What
management conclusion do you draw?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The pair of tables produces this chapter's most important — and most uncomfortable — result.

**(4a) With homogeneous quality, sampling is almost never optimal.**

- p ≤ 1%: **not inspecting** is cheaper. The inspection cost doesn't pay for itself.
- p ≥ 2%: **100% inspection** is cheaper. Exposure to NC dominates everything.
- Sampling always sits between the two, without beating either.

The reason is structural: a lot **accepted** by sampling ships with its NC fraction intact.
Sampling doesn't improve the lot it accepts — it only decides whether to sort it or not. If every
lot is the same, that decision has no information to exploit.

**(4b) With lot-to-lot variation, sampling clearly wins.**

With 90% of lots at 0.2% and 10% at 5%, the n=125/Ac=3 plan costs ~€1,645/lot, versus €2,448 with
no inspection and €5,000 at 100%. A savings of ~33% versus the best alternative.

Here there's information to exploit: lots **aren't all the same**, and the sample tells them apart.
The plan catches the 5% lot, rejects it, and triggers sorting, while cheaply passing the good lots
through.

**The three management conclusions:**

1. **Acceptance sampling lives off lot-to-lot variation.** That's its value proposition, and it's
   the reason it was invented — at a time when lot-to-lot variation was enormous. Where the process
   is stable and uniform, it adds no economic value.

2. **Before optimizing the plan, measure the lot-to-lot variation.** If the NC fraction is roughly
   the same across every lot, the discussion about AQL and inspection level is secondary — the real
   decision is between not inspecting and inspecting everything. If lot-to-lot variation is large,
   the plan matters a lot, and it's worth designing it carefully.

3. **The improvement path is clear.** Reducing lot-to-lot variation makes sampling progressively
   less useful — and that's what justifies the evolution toward **skip-lot** and, afterward, toward
   **evidence of supplier process control** (control charts, capability, PPAP), which is where IATF
   and automotive best practices have already set the bar. Acceptance sampling is a transitional
   technology, suited to an immature supply relationship.

**The question the table lets you ask, and it's the most useful one in a management meeting:** *"how
much is it worth to reduce the proportion of bad lots from 10% to 2%?"* The answer reads out in
euros per lot, and turns a technical AQL discussion into a supplier-development investment
discussion.
