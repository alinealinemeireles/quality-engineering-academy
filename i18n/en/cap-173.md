## Chapter 173: Seasonal Decomposition and ARIMA/SARIMA Models

<h3 id="the-engineering-question">The engineering question</h3><p><em>Weekly production climbs every month, right around closing. Is that real process seasonality, or just noise that happens to repeat on a certain cadence? And if I want to forecast the next 8 weeks of production, is a simple moving average enough, or do I need a model that explicitly separates trend, seasonality, and what's left over?</em></p>
<p>Decomposing a time series into its components — trend, seasonality, residual — isn't a chart decoration before modeling: it's the diagnostic that tells you whether a forecasting model even makes sense, and what kind of model fits the data you actually have. Only after that diagnostic does the classic <strong>ARIMA/SARIMA</strong> family come in — the core of traditional statistical time series forecasting — and, as this chapter shows, the diagnostic itself sometimes reveals that you <em>don't</em> have enough history for the model you'd like to fit.</p><h3 id="decompose-before-modeling">Decompose before modeling</h3><p>Any observed time series can, in principle, be written as the sum (additive model) or the product (multiplicative model) of three components:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="Y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t \quad \text{(additive)} \qquad Y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t \quad \text{(multiplicative)}"></div>
<p>where <span class="math-inline" data-math="T_t"></span> is the trend (long-term movement), <span class="math-inline" data-math="S_t"></span> is the seasonal component (a pattern that repeats on a fixed cycle), and <span class="math-inline" data-math="R_t"></span> is the residual — whatever is left after removing the first two. Use the additive model when the seasonal amplitude is roughly constant in absolute terms over time; use the multiplicative model when the amplitude grows proportionally to the series level (common in production that's ramping up).</p>
<p>Decomposition — whether the classical method (moving averages) or STL (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using LOESS, more robust to outliers) — doesn't produce a forecast by itself. It's an <strong>exploratory</strong> step: you do it before choosing a model, to see with your own eyes whether a stable seasonal pattern actually exists, which direction the trend is heading, and how "noisy" the residual is. The <code>statsmodels</code> library provides this step via <code>seasonal_decompose</code> (classical) and <code>STL</code> (more modern).</p><h3 id="stationarity-arimas-hidden-prerequisite">Stationarity: ARIMA's hidden prerequisite</h3><p>ARIMA models assume, by construction, that the series is <strong>stationary</strong>: constant mean, variance, and autocovariance over time. A series with a clear trend (like most production series, which persistently grows or declines) violates that assumption and needs to be <strong>differenced</strong> before an autoregressive model makes sense.</p>
<p>The first-order difference operator is:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\nabla Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}"></div>
<p>Differencing once removes linear trends; differencing twice (rarely needed in industrial practice) removes quadratic trends. The number of differences needed is the ARIMA model's <span class="math-inline" data-math="d"></span> parameter. In practice, the <strong>Augmented Dickey-Fuller</strong> (ADF) test is the most common formal criterion for deciding whether a series is already stationary or still needs another round of differencing — the test's null hypothesis is "the series has a unit root" (it isn't stationary); a low p-value (typically &lt; 0.05) lets you reject that hypothesis.</p>
<p><strong>Don't skip this step.</strong> Fitting an ARIMA model directly on a series with an obvious trend, without differencing, is the most common mistake when learning this technique — the model tries to compensate for the trend with autoregressive terms, produces residuals that are still autocorrelated, and the forecast inherits the bias.</p><h3 id="the-arima-p-d-q-model">The ARIMA(p,d,q) model</h3><p>An ARIMA(<span class="math-inline" data-math="p,d,q"></span>) model combines three components:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>AR(<span class="math-inline" data-math="p"></span>)</strong> — autoregressive: the current value depends linearly on the <span class="math-inline" data-math="p"></span> previous values of the series itself.</li>
<li><strong>I(<span class="math-inline" data-math="d"></span>)</strong> — integrated: how many differences (previous section) were applied to make the series stationary.</li>
<li><strong>MA(<span class="math-inline" data-math="q"></span>)</strong> — moving average: the current value depends linearly on the <span class="math-inline" data-math="q"></span> previous forecast errors (not the observations — the <em>errors</em>).</li>
</ul>
<p>On the series already differenced <span class="math-inline" data-math="d"></span> times, the model is:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\nabla^d Y_t = c + \phi_1 \nabla^d Y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p \nabla^d Y_{t-p} + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q} + \varepsilon_t"></div>
<p>In practice, <span class="math-inline" data-math="p"></span> and <span class="math-inline" data-math="q"></span> rarely go above 2 or 3 in industrial-quality series, and the choice relies on the autocorrelation (ACF) and partial autocorrelation (PACF) plots already introduced in <a href="#/aula/cap-055">Chapter 55</a>: a sharp cutoff in the PACF after lag <span class="math-inline" data-math="p"></span> suggests an AR component; a sharp cutoff in the ACF after lag <span class="math-inline" data-math="q"></span> suggests an MA component. When in doubt, fit a few plausible candidates and compare by AIC — but always confirm afterward that the chosen model's residuals have no remaining autocorrelation (see "Common mistakes" below).</p><h3 id="seasonal-extension-sarima-p-d-q-p-d-q-m">Seasonal extension: SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)m</h3><p>When a series has a pattern that repeats on a fixed cycle — weekly, monthly, yearly — plain ARIMA isn't enough: it ignores that repeating structure. The seasonal extension, <strong>SARIMA</strong>, adds a second set of terms operating on the scale of the seasonal period <span class="math-inline" data-math="m"></span>:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\text{SARIMA}(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_m"></div>
<p>where <span class="math-inline" data-math="(P,D,Q)"></span> are the <em>seasonal</em> autoregressive, differencing, and moving-average orders (operating on multiples of <span class="math-inline" data-math="m"></span> observations), and <span class="math-inline" data-math="m"></span> is the number of observations per seasonal cycle — for example, <span class="math-inline" data-math="m=7"></span> for a weekly pattern in daily data, or <span class="math-inline" data-math="m=52"></span> for a yearly pattern in weekly data. In <code>statsmodels</code>, this is implemented by the <code>SARIMAX</code> class (the trailing "X" means the model also accepts exogenous variables, optionally).</p><h3 id="how-much-history-is-enough-to-estimate-seasonality">How much history is enough to estimate seasonality</h3><p>This is where the care that separates someone who just copies the recipe from someone who actually understands what they're doing comes in: <strong>estimating a seasonal component requires, at minimum, about 2 to 3 full cycles of history</strong> for the model to have enough information to separate seasonal signal from noise. With <span class="math-inline" data-math="m=52"></span> (yearly seasonality on weekly data), that means roughly 2 to 3 <em>years</em> of data — not months.</p>
<p>That's exactly the problem that shows up in the practical project's production dataset: with about <strong>18 months of history</strong> (a little over 1 annual cycle), there's no statistical basis for confidently claiming an <em>annual</em> seasonality exists — the model, forced to estimate <span class="math-inline" data-math="m=52"></span> with fewer than 2 cycles, is essentially fitting a pattern to a single example of it, which is indistinguishable from overfitting. The seasonality that <em>can</em> be estimated confidently with that same history is the shorter-cycle one — for example, a monthly pattern (<span class="math-inline" data-math="m \approx 4"></span> in weekly data), of which more than a dozen full repeats already exist. The rule of thumb is simple: before declaring "there's seasonality of period <span class="math-inline" data-math="m"></span>," check whether <span class="math-inline" data-math="n / m \geq 2\text{–3}"></span>; if not, treat the long-seasonality hypothesis as untestable with the available data, not as false or confirmed.</p><h3 id="worked-example-decomposing-and-fitting-a-sarima-with-short-history">Worked example: decomposing and fitting a SARIMA with short history</h3><h3 id="worked-example-decomposing-and-fitting-a-sarima-with-short-history">Worked example: decomposing and fitting a SARIMA with short history</h3><div class="codetabs"><div class="ct-bar" role="tablist"><button type="button" class="ct-tab on" role="tab" aria-selected="true" id="ct0198d728-t0">Python</button></div><div class="ct-pane" role="tabpanel" aria-labelledby="ct0198d728-t0"><div class="codeblock" data-lang="python"><div class="codebar"><span class="lang">Python</span><button class="btn-copy" type="button">Copiar</button></div><pre><code class="language-python">import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

# 78 weeks (~18 months) of weekly production, with mild trend and
# monthly-cycle seasonality -- the same history horizon as
# manufacturing-performance-analytics (Notebook 10.4)
n = 78
semana = np.arange(n)
tendencia = 1000 + 3.5 * semana
sazonal_mensal = 60 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * semana / 4.33)   # ~monthly cycle (4.33 weeks/month)
ruido = rng.normal(0, 35, n)
producao = tendencia + sazonal_mensal + ruido

idx = pd.date_range("2025-01-06", periods=n, freq="W-MON")
serie = pd.Series(producao, index=idx, name="producao_semanal")

# --- Step 1: exploratory (additive) decomposition, monthly period ---
decomp = seasonal_decompose(serie, model="additive", period=4)
print("Seasonal component amplitude (min/max):",
      round(decomp.seasonal.min(), 1), "/", round(decomp.seasonal.max(), 1))
print("Trend -- start vs. end of the series:")
print("  first 4 weeks:", round(decomp.trend.dropna().iloc[:4].mean(), 1))
print("  last 4 weeks: ", round(decomp.trend.dropna().iloc[-4:].mean(), 1))

# --- Step 2: is the history enough for ANNUAL seasonality (m=52)? ---
ciclos_anuais = n / 52
print(f"\nFull annual cycles available: {ciclos_anuais:.2f}")
print("Rule of thumb: you need 2-3 full cycles to estimate seasonality")
print("with confidence -- with 18 months, a seasonal SARIMA m=52 is NOT recommended.")

# --- Step 3: SARIMA with monthly seasonality (m=4), which the history supports ---
modelo = SARIMAX(serie, order=(1, 1, 1), seasonal_order=(1, 1, 0, 4),
                  enforce_stationarity=False, enforce_invertibility=False)
resultado = modelo.fit(disp=False)
print("\nAIC of the SARIMA(1,1,1)(1,1,0,4) model:", round(resultado.aic, 1))

previsao = resultado.get_forecast(steps=8)
media = previsao.predicted_mean
ic = previsao.conf_int(alpha=0.05)
print("\nForecast for the next 8 weeks (mean and 95% CI):")
print(pd.concat([media.rename("previsto"), ic], axis=1).round(1))</code></pre></div></div></div><p><strong>Engineering reading.</strong> Step 2 of the example is the most important part of the chapter, not step 3: before fitting any seasonal SARIMA, calculate how many full cycles of the intended seasonal period fit in your history.
