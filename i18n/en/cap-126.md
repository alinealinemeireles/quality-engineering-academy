## Chapter 126: Six Sigma in Distribution

### Benefits of Six Sigma in the Distribution Sector

**Developing a Universal Process**

Six Sigma is an effective methodology to employ, because it surfaces issues that even the process's stakeholders aren't aware of. Just because the company doesn't know about a problem doesn't mean it doesn't exist.

Consider a company with multiple distribution channels. It ships orders to brick-and-mortar retailers; at the same time, it has to fulfill online orders, and it also distributes items to locations that will host trade shows in the near future. It's easy to see where staff could get confused by the many destinations of these orders, as well as the various sizes and specifications unique to each distribution point. In this situation, the potential for things to get lost along the way is extremely high.

Using Six Sigma, the company can develop a universal process that works across all these different situations. This lets the company's staff handle every order the same way. Even if certain retailers require certain specifications, the vast majority of order preparation can be carried out in line with every other order, and any unique customization needed can be built into the process. Having this process well established also helps companies work with retailers and manage those retailers' expectations.

Establishing a universal process accomplishes far more than simply making life easier for the company's staff. It also provides insight into the company's true capacity, potentially opening the door to new distribution opportunities.

**Identifying the Best Retail Points**

Distribution is often viewed through a "bigger is better" lens. In other words, the more stores that carry an item, the better that item is performing. In reality, though, it's not that simple. It's entirely possible for a given item to be underperforming across several stores, turning a seemingly optimal distribution arrangement into a net loss for the company. Unfortunately, figuring this out amid order fulfillment and building relationships with partner retailers is very hard. As a result, companies often stick with what they've always done, not wanting to reduce the number of products available on shelves.

A Six Sigma project gives a company the opportunity to step back and assess best practices for that organization when it comes to distribution. It's possible that a given retailer sells a large volume of product but doesn't return as much money to the company. On the other hand, another retailer might generate more revenue for the company, but adequate supply can't be provided, because of commitments to other retailers.

Six Sigma not only shows a company the best channels to use for its distribution, it also provides the concrete data needed to encourage management to change its philosophies. Without evidence in hand, it would be hard to get leadership to consider alternative distribution methods. However, once the potential benefits — as well as the lost revenue tied to keeping the status quo — are laid out, it's easy to demonstrate why change is needed.

Another hidden benefit of using Six Sigma this way is that it can help the company identify its niche. It's entirely possible the company is marketing and distributing its products the wrong way. Six Sigma can help the company clearly see how distribution should be handled, both in terms of revenue and meeting demand.

**Determining the ROI of Distribution Points**

When product is scarce, it falls to the company to determine which distribution points should receive those few precious items. While reach is a consideration in this situation, the company is best served by using the channel that will provide the greatest return for the money invested. Without the chance for in-depth analysis, getting these numbers is nearly impossible.

Using Six Sigma gives a company the ability to calculate its return on investment for every distribution channel it uses. This is a great way for the company to see whether it's actually getting what it wants from its relationships with retailers and vendors. Going through this analysis will surface the best distribution channels when it comes to exposure and generating more revenue per dollar spent.

Once the company knows how much its products are worth to a retailer, it can renegotiate its terms with its distribution partners. That negotiation is much easier to stomach if the company knows exactly what it should be getting, along with the potential benefits and drawbacks of partnering with different retailers. This is one example of how a Six Sigma project opens doors in many other areas of the company's operations.

### Challenges of Using Six Sigma in the Distribution Sector

**Multiple Distribution Channels in a Single Project**

For a Six Sigma project to succeed, it needs accurate measurements taken within a manageable project scope. In other words, a large company can't consider the full breadth of its distribution process, since that would be unworkable in terms of data collection and process improvement.

This is problematic for companies with multiple distribution points. For example, the parameters for online distribution are completely different from those for physical storefronts. A company that wants to improve its efficiency and return on investment across every area may find it hard to narrow its focus to just one small area of distribution. It would find it even harder to extend any new implementations to its other distribution channels, since there's no guarantee the new model would work across multiple platforms.

Part of preparing for a Six Sigma project is using root-cause analysis to identify the core of the problems plaguing a company from a distribution standpoint. The company can then isolate the area with the biggest problem and the most lucrative potential benefit. Once the project is finished, the company can assess whether the solution would work with other distribution channels, but it should understand that every point is different. What works in one area isn't guaranteed to work in another.

**Dollars versus Brand Image**

Using Six Sigma to identify the most beneficial revenue streams, as well as the ROI incurred by accessing those channels, is an undertaking any company would do well to embrace. However, there can be a disconnect between the Six Sigma project's findings and the brand image the company wants to convey.

A company that makes goods aimed at upscale customers may not see great returns from working with specific retailers that match its key demographics. However, to reach that desired audience, the company must keep making its products available at those retailers. Similarly, the company may not want to sell its goods at more common retail points, even if its return on investment there is higher. The prestige factor is something that can't easily be quantified, but it's an important factor for many companies that want to control the image of their products and their brand as a whole.

Six Sigma can help companies find a middle ground that lets their products maintain a certain status, without putting the business in a financial hole. The problem arises when management doesn't accept these findings, believing instead that its products should be displayed in a specific way. This is just one example of why poor distribution arrangements continue to exist, despite data showing better options for the company.

**Limited Control in Third-Party Arrangements**

Today's online marketplace makes it easy for large and small companies alike to gain exposure. Sites like Etsy, Amazon, and Sears let companies sell on larger platforms, which, in most cases, is a mutually beneficial relationship. The retailer gets to offer a wider selection of products, while the company benefits from a much larger distribution platform than it could otherwise access.

However, that relationship can quickly sour if the retailer makes any changes that could negatively impact the wholesaler. Changes to service fees or reduced royalties can significantly impact the bottom line of companies distributing through these third-party sites. Even small changes, like a website layout change or a modified ordering process, can become catastrophic if customers react negatively enough to stop making purchases.

The impact of these changes sheds light on how much control gets ceded to retailers when companies distribute this way. Retailers will always seek to maximize their profits while minimizing costs, and every one of their actions reflects that mindset. Modifying a relationship with a manufacturer might mean only a small revenue bump for a company like Amazon, but for a small wholesaler, such a change could put that business in financial jeopardy. Worse, since the cost of losing a distribution point like Amazon is much greater than that of a diminished revenue stream, the small business has no choice but to comply with these changes.

Six Sigma can't help a company work around these issues. Even the most carefully laid plans can be completely derailed by the whims of a third-party retailer. The Six Sigma project can, and should, include developing contingency plans that can help a company anticipate and react to changes made by third-party sites. Unfortunately, even creating these plans is no substitute for a controlled distribution environment, one that lets the company plan for the future with confidence.

### Six Sigma Distribution Case Study: Therma Tru Doors

Headquartered in Butler, Indiana, Therma Tru Doors has been a leading force in the door industry for more than 50 years. In 1982, Therma Tru Doors unveiled to the public the first-ever fiberglass door. Over the years, Therma Tru Doors has kept innovating and expanding its audience, letting the company reach its current status as one of the leading manufacturers of doors of every kind in the world.

In 2001, Therma Tru Doors began running into trouble shipping products from the company's warehouse in Matamoros, Mexico, to Therma Tru Doors' plant in Indiana. After an initial review, it was decided that trailer-space utilization at the Mexico warehouse was the main culprit. A Six Sigma project was launched to find ways to solve the trailer-space problem.

Christopher B. Anderson and his project team set out to get baseline numbers and try to improve those figures. At the start, average trailer utilization was 57 percent. In other words, more than 40 percent of the space in a given trailer went unused. While the obvious goal was 100 percent utilization, Anderson and his team decided a reasonable target for the project would be 90 percent utilization. This represented an achievable goal that still left room for further improvement.

To assess the situation, Anderson sought input from the very people tasked with loading the trailers every day. These employees had great ideas about how the process could be more efficient, but they simply never had a chance to have their voices heard. In addition, Anderson noted that the large racks used to transport glass from Mexico to Indiana were too big to properly use the trailer's space. He also noticed that some items were shaped in a way that made them hard to stack or reliably organize within the trailer.

Improving the process required some outside intervention. Anderson brought in a warehouse supervisor from Oklahoma, who had already gone through his own trailer-utilization project, to visit Mexico and offer some suggestions. One of his key contributions was suggesting the company purchase additional pallets that better fit the various shapes and sizes that had previously made organizing the trailer difficult. This approach let more pallets fit in a trailer, letting the company move more materials using less space.

Solving the glass-rack issue presented a bigger challenge. Not only did these items take up more space than anything else, they were also extremely fragile. However, the solution to this problem came from within the company itself. Anderson asked frontline employees whether they had any ideas for how the glass could be better organized and transported. The employees said the racks could be reconfigured to allow double stacking, effectively doubling the amount of space available in each trailer.

The numbers showed a slight dip in trailer-space utilization during the first three months of implementation. However, that drop in productivity soon gave way to a massive increase in utilization. Within six months of identifying the solutions to these issues, the Mexico trailers were running at a 99 percent utilization rate. This went well beyond the project's goals. A process to monitor productivity and maintain control was put in place, so utilization would never fall below the stated goal of 90 percent capacity.

The distribution implications of this project were twofold. First, Therma Tru Doors was able to increase productivity for the average trip from Mexico to Indiana. That meant more goods could reach shelves faster, resulting in additional revenue while keeping costs to a minimum. In addition, staff at the Indiana plant had a better idea of what to expect when opening a trailer coming from Mexico. This let them take a more organized approach to their jobs, which also translated into greater efficiency. While distribution was the main focus of this Six Sigma initiative, the project's impact reached many departments in a positive way.

### Tips for Using Six Sigma in the Distribution Sector

While there are some inherent challenges to using Six Sigma to improve a company's distribution, it's still a valuable tool, one that can help any company become more efficient and better informed about the true performance of its distribution channels. A company that carries out a Six Sigma initiative will develop a foolproof process, one that will not only help internal workflow, but will also help companies understand the nuances of their operations when negotiating with retailers.

The best way to bring in Six Sigma is to do so with a clearly defined project scope. It's not possible to cover every distribution point, and trying to do so would spell a failed project. Careful analysis should be done to uncover underperforming areas, and those areas should get the Six Sigma project's focus.

Understanding the company's vision for the future is another vital element of any Six Sigma initiative. As stated earlier, distribution sometimes means moving into areas that aren't as financially rewarding. A company with a clear understanding of where it wants to go is well equipped to weigh the pros and cons of each distribution partnership. This lets the company pursue its vision, while trying to build workable relationships with its desired retailers.

Communication is key in every aspect of business, and that's certainly true in distribution. Those involved in the Six Sigma project should keep other departments informed about the project and clearly state how they might be affected by the initiative. These stakeholders should also understand that the project will ultimately help everyone, and each group should be told about the unique benefits it will receive as a result of its cooperation.
