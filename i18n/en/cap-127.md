## Chapter 127: Six Sigma in Manufacturing

### Benefits of Using Six Sigma in the Manufacturing Sector

**Examining Existing Processes**

The early phases of any Six Sigma project require the project team and its stakeholders to define the project scope and review the existing processes already in place. Since it's the failure of these processes that made the Six Sigma project necessary, it's imperative to review every facet of every action carried out by the company.

Manufacturing is more than simply flipping a switch on a machine. It's all the small things that make the process happen, and it's often the smallest things that make the biggest difference. Something as small as needing to clean a machine regularly can cause serious delays, particularly if it turns out the machine is being cleaned too often, or isn't being cleaned to a satisfactory level. The Six Sigma project might find that a more thorough cleaning, done less often, can significantly boost output.

These discovery steps highlight the need to break down every aspect of the manufacturing process. Six Sigma can help companies eliminate inefficiencies and redundancies, but it can only do so if these smaller parts of the steps are documented and examined. Some employees may feel these smaller parts of their job aren't worth mentioning; they may also be reluctant to talk about aspects of their job they don't handle particularly well. However, by discussing these issues, they can take a hands-on approach to developing a better process, one that not only boosts productivity but also makes frontline employees' jobs easier.

**Reworking Processes to Boost Productivity**

One of the hardest parts of monitoring the quality of a manufacturing operation is that, even if the company notices a part of the process that isn't working, it's very hard to fix that work. Since the company needs products to keep getting made, halting operations to make an efficiency-based adjustment is nearly impossible. That's why so many companies keep operating the way they've always done things, even if it comes at the cost of increased productivity.

Six Sigma gives companies the push to make major changes to processes where needed. When a proposed change is presented to a Six Sigma project's stakeholders, there's supporting data suggesting how much the company stands to benefit. This can help decision-makers weigh the true impact of the change. Even if there's a short-term dip in productivity due to reworking the process, the overall benefit will often outweigh the diminished efficiency many times over.

Of course, there's more at stake than simply overseeing a more efficient process. A process with a shorter turnaround time will naturally result in more products getting made than the previous method of operating. Over time, this increase in output can lead to a much larger quantity of products being created, giving the company the ability to sell the surplus or save on labor costs by cutting the number of hours the manufacturing plant runs each day.

**Identifying Cost-Saving Opportunities**

The big benefit of creating extra products is the financial impact of the additional inventory on hand, at essentially no cost. Since these additional items will be made using the same labor and mechanical resources as the typical load under the previous system, the only added cost comes in the form of raw materials. This productivity boost can have massive implications for the company.

As is the case in any business, internal improvements that save money should result in a less expensive product for the consumer. If a company finds a way to make a product cheaper, the company's margins on those products go up. Passing those savings on to the customer is a great way to gain market share and provide the company with additional revenue — income beyond what the company would have generated with the old process.

The average frontline employee may not care much about the price the customer pays for a product they make. But seeing the bigger picture is an important part of any Six Sigma initiative. If the company can show manufacturing employees how their money-saving efforts can benefit the business — and, ultimately, themselves — they'll be far more likely to support the project.

### Challenges of Using Six Sigma in the Manufacturing Sector

**Process Diversity**

To an outsider, manufacturing is often seen as a single, uniform function. However, as anyone who's seen a manufacturing process up close knows, the steps needed to create each unique product differ. Even multiple product lines of the same item, with different features, can have unique processes.

This presents a problem when it comes to Six Sigma. Since a Six Sigma initiative's typical project scope is limited to one process, a company with multiple products — and multiple inefficient manufacturing processes — may struggle to draw large-scale conclusions. In other words, the solutions they arrive at as a result of the project may not be able to be extended to other manufacturing processes.

In most cases, since at least one flawed process can be reworked, the project is still worth carrying out. While the entire solution can't be replicated across every other process run by the manufacturing team, the lessons learned along the way can apply to other activities that need improvement.

**Outdated Machinery**

Six Sigma can accomplish a lot, but analysis alone can't transform an organization. Strict adherence to the project's recommendations is needed to see results, and sometimes implementing a reworked process requires a financial capital investment. And even if a business can afford to hire a project team to run a Six Sigma initiative, there may not be enough money to do what's needed and see the project through to its ideal conclusion.

In manufacturing, it's not uncommon to see outdated, obsolete equipment still in use. Because of the prohibitive cost of upgrading machinery, many companies try to make do with equipment they've used for years. It's not hard to see how a Six Sigma project might identify this old machinery as the bottleneck in a flawed process.

That's where the dilemma lies. The project makes it abundantly clear the company stands to benefit enormously by upgrading its machinery. Employees, tired of using old equipment to carry out modern tasks, get excited seeing this new technology that will make their jobs easier. And yet, it may never come to pass, because the company simply can't afford to buy new machines.

In this case, the impact cuts both ways. Since the old equipment was pinpointed as the bottleneck, the company realizes its efficiency won't improve unless the upgrade happens. At the same time, employee morale is likely to drop, as they realize their jobs won't be getting easier anytime soon. The company then faces a serious dilemma — either keep running the process as is and keep struggling, or take on significant debt to buy a machine that might not pay for itself for many years.

While the project achieved its preliminary goal of pinpointing the weak link in a process, it ultimately fails because the solution is impossible to achieve. It's a risk manufacturing companies take every time they embark on a Six Sigma project.

**Encouraging Buy-In**

At the executive level, implementing Six Sigma at a manufacturing facility always seems like a good idea. At that level, the only thing apparent is the benefit that will be gained from carrying out a project and improving an inefficient process. However, at lower levels, employees may not be as enthusiastic about the idea of a Six Sigma project.

One of the key aspects of any Six Sigma initiative is that it needs full buy-in for the project to succeed. Just as a process is only as good as its weakest link, a Six Sigma project is only as effective as its least invested participant. And in a sector like manufacturing, it's easy to see that the frontline employees operating the machines are the people with the real power in any project.

The Six Sigma initiative can only work if lower-level workers want it to succeed. While it might not be obvious to executives, these employees may have good reasons not to want a more efficient process. In a world where outsourcing and downsizing are feared across every organization, manufacturing employees may worry that a more efficient process means their services are no longer needed. That may be all the motivation these individuals need to rebel against this initiative, which looks a lot more like a threat than a resource.

It falls to the project team to explain to these employees that any changes stemming from a Six Sigma project will ultimately benefit them. Beyond that, these workers need to understand that they're not only needed after the solution is implemented, they'll also be an important part of creating the solution. At the end of the day, the ultimate goal of a Six Sigma project built around a manufacturing process is to make frontline employees' jobs and lives easier. The sooner they understand this, the more receptive they'll be to change — and the more likely they'll be to make positive contributions to the project.

### Six Sigma Case Study: Machines versus Human Capital

A manufacturing company headquartered in the American Midwest was plagued by an inefficient manufacturing process. The company's manufacturing plant ran a 24-hour operation, which wore employees down, many of whom had to work 12-hour shifts on weekends.

The main role of the manufacturing employees at the plant was cleaning machines that malfunctioned. Given the sticky nature of the company's products, this happened as often as ten times an hour. While the employees were skilled at identifying problems and cleaning them up, the company felt it could benefit from a more efficient approach, and it was decided that a Six Sigma project would be the best way to identify ways the company could improve its manufacturing process.

Under the existing setup, the company was operating at a 65 percent efficiency rate. The company's ultimate goal was to reach 70 percent. If the company could hit that number, it could cut the plant's operating time to 16 hours a day, creating two eight-hour shifts, which would let employees approach their jobs with a more positive attitude. This model would also help the company save money; for every one percentage point of efficiency gained, the business would save $250,000.

To kick off the project, the company collected data across several data points — the machine in use, the type of product, the name of the individual operating the machine, and the time of day. After six months, the organization was able to start reviewing its numbers.

One thing that became obvious right away was that there were significant differences among the parties involved in the operation. Some product types performed much better than others, and the same was true for machine operators. The company's next step was figuring out how these datasets worked together.

The company ran a series of regression analyses to determine the relationships among these factors. The first analysis showed a strong correlation between efficiency and the number of machines each operator ran. To hit the 70 percent efficiency goal, each operator could only work on one machine at a time.

Another regression analysis was run, this time incorporating every data point gathered during the project's Measure phase. While several combinations of variables proved statistically significant, the two biggest factors were time of day and machine operator. Further investigation showed the main reason time was a contributing factor was the drastic drop in productivity that occurred during shift changes, twice a day. In addition, the project showed each operator tended to perform better on their preferred machine; however, since operators usually worked whichever machine was available, they often weren't on their favorite machines.

Given the data, the solution was fairly simple. Operators would be assigned to the machine on which they were most efficient. This single change not only brought efficiency up to the desired 70 percent goal, the company's efficiency improved to more than 73 percent after a six-week implementation period. Because of the change, the company was able to save $1.7 million, and employees got to enjoy the smoother schedule they had earned.

This case study is a classic example of a situation where many companies would have been tempted to throw money at new machinery. However, investing in human capital can often be just as effective as, if not more effective than, simply buying new machines. Companies should consider every option when making improvements, especially those that let their employees shine.

### Tips for Using Six Sigma in the Manufacturing Sector

Manufacturing is a vitally important aspect of any retail-based business's operations. It's not an area for inefficiency and cost-cutting. A happy, well-run manufacturing team can make the whole company more successful, through cost-saving measures, increased quality, and a larger inventory of products the company can sell.

When implementing Six Sigma, choosing a manageable, purposeful scope for the project is essential. While the solution can't be carried over everywhere, a project that reaches a successful conclusion will generate positive insights that can impact other divisions of the manufacturing operation. These findings will make subsequent Six Sigma projects within that company easier to manage.

Involving frontline manufacturing employees early, and often, will help them take ownership of the initiative. The project's success or failure rests in their hands, and they'll make the leadership team look very smart — if they're properly motivated to do so.

Tracking all the data is an important component of any Six Sigma project. This is especially true in the manufacturing sector, where something small can end up being the bottleneck that derails an entire process. No data is too insignificant to track, and, as the case study showed, no potential solution is too simple to consider.
