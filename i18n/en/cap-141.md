## Chapter 141: ISO 2859-1:2026 — Acceptance Sampling in Depth
### The engineering question

> *"The customer requires AQL 1.0. What protection does that actually give me? And what happens if
> my process drifts to 2.5%?"*

This chapter is required on the Quality Engineering path. Not because acceptance sampling is the
best strategy — **it isn't**; SPC and capability are superior — but because it still governs the
contractual relationship between customer and supplier in most industries, and because
misinterpreting it is one of the most persistent sources of commercial conflict.

> **Reference standard.** **ISO 2859-1:2026** (published January 2026) includes: switching to
> **skip-lot** from normal or reduced inspection; producer and product qualification requirements;
> methods for randomly selecting the lots to inspect; and, in Annex E, how to build the OC curve and
> ASN curve of any plan (exactly what this chapter's code does). The standard recommends using it
> together with ISO 2859-0:1995, which contains examples.

---

## 141.1 What the standard is, and what it isn't

ISO 2859-1 is a sampling **scheme** indexed by the AQL, not a standalone plan. That's the key to
everything else: the protection the standard offers **doesn't come from the individual plan**, it
comes from combining the plan with the **switching rules** applied to a **continuous series of
lots**.

| Standard | Object | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **ISO 2859-1:2026** | AQL schemes, lot by lot, continuous series | Ongoing supply |
| **ISO 2859-2:2020** | LQ for **isolated lots** | A single lot, no series; per-lot guarantee |
| **ISO 2859-3:2005** | Generic skip-lot | Reduced inspection with history |
| **ISO 2859-4:2020** | Assessment of stated quality levels | Auditing a claim |
| **ISO 2859-5:2005** | Sequential plans | Minimizing the average sample |
| **ISO 28590:2017** | Introduction and series-selection guide | Choosing the right part |

> **The sector's most expensive mistake:** applying Part 1 to an isolated lot. Without a series,
> there are no switching rules and the real protection disappears — you're left with a plan that
> still has a high Pa even for quality clearly worse than the AQL. For an isolated lot, the correct
> standard is **Part 2 (LQ)**.

---

## 141.2 The OC curve: the only honest reading of a plan

For a plan (n, c) — accept if x ≤ c nonconforming in the sample of n:

$$ P_a(p) = \sum_{x=0}^{c} \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x} \quad \text{(binomial, type B curve)} $$

When the lot is finite and n/N isn't negligible, the correct distribution is the
**hypergeometric** (type A curve):

$$ P_a(p) = \sum_{x=0}^{c} \frac{\binom{D}{x}\binom{N-D}{n-x}}{\binom{N}{n}}, \quad D = pN $$

**The two points that define the curve:**

- **AQL** (producer's point): quality treated as satisfactory; high Pa (α = producer's risk).
- **LTPD / LQ** (consumer's point): quality treated as unacceptable; low Pa (β = consumer's risk,
  typically 0.10).

> **The statement that sums up the chapter:** *the AQL is not a per-lot promise.* A lot with 3%
> nonconforming has a non-negligible probability of being accepted by a plan with AQL 1.0. What the
> AQL promises is that a **process** held at that level will see most of its lots accepted over
> time. The code cell quantifies exactly this.

## 141.3 The inverse problem: designing instead of looking up

The junior engineer asks *"what's the sample size?"* and goes to the table. The experienced
engineer asks:

> *"What protection do I want against what level of bad quality, and what risk am I willing to
> accept?"*

That turns the problem from a **table lookup** into **designing a decision rule**. Given two points
(AQL, α) and (LTPD, β), you look for the pair (n, c) that satisfies both at once:

$$ P_a(\text{AQL}) \ge 1-\alpha \quad \wedge \quad P_a(\text{LTPD}) \le \beta $$

An exact solution doesn't always exist — the OC curve is discrete in c. You then look for the
smallest n that satisfies both, which is a simple search problem, solved in the code.

**The operating ratio** R = LTPD/AQL determines c: the more demanding the required discrimination
(low R), the larger c has to be — and the larger the sample. A "square" OC curve (perfect
discrimination) would require 100% inspection.

---

## 141.4 The switching rules: where the real protection lives

This is the standard's most overlooked and most important mechanism.

| From | To | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | **Tightened** | 2 rejections in 5 consecutive lots (original inspection) |
| Tightened | Normal | 5 consecutive lots accepted under tightened inspection |
| Tightened | **Suspension** | 5 non-accepted lots in a sequence of consecutive lots under tightened inspection (original inspection) |
| Normal | Reduced | Switching score ≥ 30 + production at a steady rate + responsible authority considers it desirable |
| Reduced | Normal | 1 non-accepted lot, or irregular/delayed production, or other conditions at the responsible authority's discretion |
| Normal or Reduced | **Skip-lot** | Previous 10 lots accepted with **zero** nonconforming items + steady production + producer and product qualified (9.5.2) + responsible authority considers it desirable |
| Skip-lot | Normal | 1 inspected lot not accepted, or irregular/delayed production, or other conditions at the responsible authority's discretion |

**The switching score** (9.3.3.2) starts at zero at the start of normal inspection. For single
sampling plans with Ac ≥ 2, add 3 for each lot that would have been accepted if the AQL had been one
step tighter; with Ac 0 or 1, add 2 for each accepted lot; any other result resets it to zero.

**Suspension is where it has teeth.** If tightened inspection accumulates 5 non-accepted lots,
inspection under the standard **stops**, and can't resume until the supplier demonstrates
improvement to the responsible authority — and when it resumes, it restarts under tightened
inspection. This is what stops a bad supplier from feeding lots through until one happens to pass.

Without the switching rules, the AQL scheme degenerates into a fixed plan and the effective
protection over time drops drastically — the code quantifies this difference by simulation.

---

## 141.5 Skip-lot in ISO 2859-1:2026

Skip-lot lets you skip inspecting a fraction of lots when demonstrated quality justifies it. In ISO
2859-1:2026, skip-lot is the **fourth state** of the Part 1 scheme, with its own rules (9.5):

- **Frequency:** the lots to inspect are chosen **at random** with probability **0.4 (2 in 5)**,
  without the producer knowing in advance which ones; at least one lot must be inspected in each
  agreed period (2 months by default). Annex C (normative) gives the random-selection procedures.
- **Producer qualification:** a documented system for controlling product quality and design
  changes, with inspection and recording of every lot; the ability to detect and correct shifts; no
  recent change that could adversely affect quality. ISO 9001 certification **counts, but doesn't
  replace** the product qualification assessment.
- **Product qualification:** stable design; no critical nonconformities; general inspection levels
  I, II, or III; no period on tightened inspection during qualification; essentially continuous
  production for at least **6 months**, with at least one lot per month; quality at the AQL or
  better during the stability period; 10 or more consecutive lots accepted on original inspection.
  Fractional acceptance number plans can't be used for skip-lot.
- **Entry and exit:** you enter with the previous 10 lots accepted with **zero** nonconforming
  items; you drop back to normal inspection at the first inspected lot not accepted, and a new
  sequence of 10 lots with zero nonconforming items is needed to requalify.
- **Never for isolated lots:** it only applies to continuing series in which inspected and
  uninspected lots are of similar quality.

It reduces inspection cost — and increases exposure, because an uninspected lot passes by
definition. The decision is economic and should be made with numbers:

**expected cost = inspection cost + P(bad lot passes) × consequence**

Note the **zero** nonconforming requirement: it's much tougher than "lots accepted." The standard
itself requires the process to be stable and well better than the AQL — passing inspection isn't
enough. The Bayesian updating from Chapter 136 is the quantitative formalization of this reasoning.

## 141.6 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "AQL 1.0 means I accept at most 1% defects" | False contractual expectation; dispute | Read the full OC curve |
| Applying Part 1 to an isolated lot | Real protection much lower than assumed | Use Part 2 (LQ) |
| Ignoring the switching rules | You lose most of the scheme's protection | Implement it as a state machine |
| Using the binomial with a large n/N | Pa estimated with the wrong curve (type A is steeper: the binomial underestimates Pa for good quality and overestimates it for bad quality) | Hypergeometric (type A curve) |
| Defining the lot for logistical convenience | A heterogeneous lot violates the base assumption | Define the lot by production homogeneity |
| Sampling the accessible boxes | Selection bias; invalid inference | Documented random sampling |
| Skip-lot without a stable process | Exposure with no basis | Verify stability, not just acceptance |
| Skip-lot with predictably chosen lots | The producer knows which lots will be checked | Random 2-in-5 selection (Annex C) |

    estado = "normal"
    pontuacao = 0
    ult5 = []          # rejeicoes nos ultimos 5 lotes (inspeccao original)
    aceites_seguidos = 0
    nao_aceites_apertada = 0
    aceites = 0
    suspenso_em = None
    inspecionadas = 0
    for i in range(n_lotes):
        nn, cc = PLANOS[estado]
        x = rng.binomial(nn, p_processo)
        inspecionadas += nn
        aceite = x <= cc
        aceites += int(aceite)
        if not usar_comutacao:
            continue
        # --- regras de comutacao
        ult5.append(0 if aceite else 1); ult5 = ult5[-5:]
        if estado == "normal":
            if sum(ult5) >= 2:
                estado = "apertada"; ult5 = []; nao_aceites_apertada = 0
                aceites_seguidos = 0; pontuacao = 0
            else:
                if aceite:
                    aceites_seguidos += 1
                    # pontuacao de comutacao simplificada
                    pontuacao += 3 if x <= max(cc - 2, 0) else 2
                else:
                    aceites_seguidos = 0; pontuacao = 0
                if aceites_seguidos >= 10 and pontuacao >= 30:
                    estado = "reduzida"; aceites_seguidos = 0
        elif estado == "apertada":
            if aceite:
                aceites_seguidos += 1
                if aceites_seguidos >= 5:
                    estado = "normal"; aceites_seguidos = 0; ult5 = []
            else:
                aceites_seguidos = 0
                nao_aceites_apertada += 1
                if nao_aceites_apertada >= 5:
                    suspenso_em = i + 1
                    break
        elif estado == "reduzida":
            if not aceite:
                estado = "normal"; ult5 = []; aceites_seguidos = 0; pontuacao = 0
    n_real = i + 1
    return {"aceites": aceites, "lotes": n_real, "suspenso": suspenso_em,
            "estado_final": estado, "inspecionadas": inspecionadas}

print("  bom nao e penalizado - a coluna 'suspensoes' fica a 0%.")

print("  com comutacao SUSPENDE a inspeccao em praticamente todas as series,")
print("  o que forca accao correctiva formal em vez de deixar passar lotes.")

| Lot quality | Pa | Reading |

**The contractual meaning:** if the contract says "AQL 1.0" and the buyer reads it as "at most 1%

consistently running at 2.5% will see ~62% of its lots accepted — and each accepted lot contains, on
average, 2.5% nonconforming parts.

**Where the real protection lives:** not in the isolated plan, but in the **switching rules**. A
supplier at 2.5% rejects ~38% of lots, which quickly triggers a move to tightened inspection, and
from there to suspension. It's the series, not the lot, that protects.

**The implication for contract wording:** if the requirement is a **per-lot** guarantee, the correct
instrument is **ISO 2859-2 (LQ)**, not Part 1. Specifying an LQ says "any lot at this quality has at
most a 10% probability of being accepted" — a per-lot statement, which is usually what the buyer
actually wants and thinks they're buying.

**E2 — α and β risks.**
*Show why no plan minimizes both risks simultaneously for every p.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

For a fixed plan (n, c), the curve Pa(p) is **monotonically decreasing**. The two risks are
readings of the same curve at different points:

- α = 1 − Pa(AQL) — we want Pa **high** at the AQL
- β = Pa(LTPD) — we want Pa **low** at the LTPD

**The structural conflict:** any change to the plan moves the entire curve.

- Increasing c → the curve rises at every p → α decreases **and** β increases.
- Decreasing c → the curve drops at every p → β decreases **and** α increases.

With n fixed, it's a pure trade-off: there's no way to lower both.

**The only way out is to increase n.** That makes the curve **steeper** (better discrimination),
which lets you have a high Pa at the AQL and a low Pa at the LTPD at the same time. That's exactly
what the inverse-problem table showed: the smaller the required LTPD/AQL ratio, the larger the
needed n.

**In the limit:** perfect discrimination — Pa = 1 below the AQL and Pa = 0 above — would require a
step curve, which you only get with **n = N**, i.e., 100% inspection. And even that is only perfect
if the inspection itself is perfect, which attribute MSA (Chapter 61) usually disproves.

**The engineering conclusion, and it's the chapter's most important one:** sampling is an economic
trade-off between inspection cost and risk. The way to escape the trade-off isn't to optimize the
plan — it's to **improve the process** until the distinction between AQL and LTPD stops having
practical consequences. That's why acceptance sampling has historically been a technology in
retreat against SPC.

</details>

**E3 — Switching rules.**
*Simulate 200 lots from a 3% supplier with and without switching rules. Quantify the difference.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The code cell does exactly this. Typical results at p = 3%:

- **Without switching:** ~48% of lots accepted, indefinitely. The supplier keeps shipping.
- **With switching:** the percentage of accepted lots drops to ~32%, and **100% of the series hit
  suspension** of inspection — which forces formal intervention before any new delivery.

**The mechanism:** at 3%, the per-lot rejection probability under normal inspection is ~1−0.48 =
0.52. The "2 rejections in 5" rule triggers almost immediately. Under tightened inspection (Ac = 2
instead of 3), rejection is even more likely, and the 5 accumulated non-acceptances arrive quickly.

**The management reading:** the switching rules turn an **inspection** mechanism into a **supplier
management** mechanism. Without them, sampling is a passive filter that lets a constant fraction of
bad lots through, forever. With them, sustained bad quality produces a contractual consequence that
forces corrective action.

**Practical consequence — what to check in a receiving audit:**

1. Is there a record of the **inspection state** (normal/tightened/reduced) per supplier and per
   family?
2. Are the transitions documented with the date and the reason?
3. Has there ever been a suspension? If a system with many years of history never suspended anyone,
   either the suppliers are excellent, or the rules aren't implemented.
4. Who's the "responsible authority" that approves the return to normal inspection after a
   suspension, and the move to reduced inspection?

The answer to these four questions tells apart whoever implemented the standard from whoever just
copied the sample-size table.

</details>

**E4 — Skip-lot: the economic decision.**
*Compare the expected cost of full inspection, conventional sampling, and 2-in-5 skip-lot for a
stable process at 0.5%, with lots of 2,000 parts.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Assumptions:** inspection cost €0.80/part; cost of an NC part reaching the customer €250;
conventional plan n = 125, Ac = 3; inspection catches the sample's NC parts but an accepted lot
still ships with its fraction of NC.

**(a) 100% inspection** — 2,000 × €0.80 = **€1,600/lot**. NC parts passing through ≈ 0 (assuming
effective inspection, which attribute MSA should confirm).

**(b) Conventional sampling** — 125 × €0.80 = €100/lot. Pa(0.005) ≈ 0.996, so practically every lot
is accepted, each carrying ~0.5% × 2,000 = 10 NC parts → 10 × €250 = €2,500 exposure per lot.
**Total ≈ €2,600/lot.**

**(c) 2-in-5 skip-lot** (the ISO 2859-1:2026 frequency) — inspects 40% of lots: 0.4 × €100 =
€40/lot in inspection cost. The exposure is essentially the same (€2,500), because the conventional
plan was already accepting nearly everything. **Total ≈ €2,540/lot.**

**But there's a prior question: this process doesn't even qualify.** The standard requires 10
consecutive lots accepted with **zero** nonconforming items. With p = 0.5% and n = 125, P(0 NC in a
lot) = 0.995¹²⁵ ≈ 0.53; ten lots in a row ≈ 0.53¹⁰ ≈ 0.002. Scenario (c) is, in practice,
unreachable — the entry rule exists precisely to block this situation.

**The result is surprising, and it's the exercise's lesson:** the difference between (b) and (c) is
€60/lot — irrelevant. And both are **much worse** than (a) in total cost, because exposure to NC
completely dominates the inspection cost.

**The right conclusion isn't "inspect 100%."** It's that **acceptance sampling doesn't protect
against a 0.5% base rate when the unit cost of the failure is €250.** No plan does: 0.5% of 2,000
parts is 10 bad parts per lot, and the sample only looks at 125.

**The real options, in order of merit:**

1. **Improve the process** to 50 PPM. At 0.005%, exposure drops to €25/lot and sampling starts to
   make sense. It's the only solution that fixes the problem instead of managing it.
2. **Automated 100% inspection** — if the per-part cost drops to cents, (a) clearly becomes optimal.
3. **Poka-yoke at the source** — prevent the NC from being made, instead of hunting for it.
4. **Skip-lot** — only justified **after** (1), as a way to reduce the verification cost of an
   already-demonstrably-capable process. That, not the savings by itself, is its correct role. At
   50 PPM, qualification becomes plausible: 0.99995¹²⁵ ≈ 0.994 per lot, ≈ 0.94 for ten in a row.

**The connection to Chapter 136:** the standard's entry rule (10 consecutive lots with zero NC) is a
simple, robust version of Bayesian reasoning; the decision can be refined with the posterior
probability that the rate exceeds the acceptable limit, updated lot by lot.
