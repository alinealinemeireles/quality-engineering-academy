## Chapter 131: Six Sigma in Government

### Benefits of Using Six Sigma in the Government Sector

**Eliminating Red Tape**

While government is commonly thought of as the ultimate governing body, it isn't often cited for implementing change quickly. That's because practically every decision made by government members involves outside parties. Citizens may be asked to vote on certain changes, while others simply require approval from higher-up officials. These additional steps add significant waiting time to carrying out approved items, often resulting in extra costs and dissatisfied citizens.

Bringing in Six Sigma can help any government office increase its efficiency. One of the main ways Six Sigma can make a noticeable difference is by identifying common areas where progress initiatives get stuck. It may turn out that a given office is the one responsible for delaying new actions, including improved processes that would help everyone. Officials can then work with those offices to develop new ways to get approval, which will speed up the rollout of new programs and processes.

Six Sigma can also highlight the real costs stemming from these delays. For example, a delay in rolling out an increase in car registration fees could ultimately cost a city or county millions of dollars. These delays not only paint the picture of a disorganized government, they also hurt the citizens of that community. Even if people feel they narrowly dodged having to pay the higher registration fee this year, they'll eventually have to pay extra money elsewhere, like taxes or park fees, to make up for the government's lost revenue.

Eliminating red tape lets government run more efficiently and transparently. This makes it easier for the community to trust its government more, which will ultimately benefit every elected official come re-election time.

**Improved Use of Funding**

One of the hardest aspects of government is getting things done on a limited budget. Contrary to what some may believe, most branches of government can't just print money at will. Financial prudence matters in government, just as it does in any business or sector.

A government branch that brings in Six Sigma will get a better sense of where its money is going, but Six Sigma's applications go much deeper. Thanks to the quality measures taken and the data gathered throughout the project, the government will get a better sense of how much return each investment provides. A government group that spent $500,000 to renovate an ice-skating rink might be pleased to find that residents attended skating sessions at that rink twice as often over the past three years. Discovering the financial potential of improving recreational facilities could inspire those officials to further upgrade other government-owned properties, like golf courses and batting cages.

This ability to track spending and return on investment can carry tremendous benefits for politicians. Instead of simply thanking donors for their contributions, elected officials can now state exactly where each individual's donations went, as well as how the community was positively impacted as a result of their generosity. Such personalized displays of appreciation and consideration would be impossible without Six Sigma's data-driven philosophy.

**Time Allocation**

Government officials have a lot on their plates. Beyond dealing with the red tape that swallows up many of their desired rollouts, officials need to set aside time to meet with their constituents, maintain a presence in the community, and attend important local events, like graduations. That's on top of the responsibilities typically associated with government, including passing laws, identifying paths for process improvements, and lobbying for the support needed to move their initiatives through the system.

These many activities take up a huge amount of time. With so much to do, stepping back to assess the typical workflow seems impossible. Once again, Six Sigma comes to the rescue, prompting a reassessment of the existing process and identifying ways to free up time without sacrificing productivity.

As with financial distribution, politicians will get a chance to see exactly how many hours a week they devote to the various aspects of their jobs. In addition, just as they did with funding, they'll be able to see how productive each of those hours actually was. For example, a politician might find that fundraising for eight hours a week is only marginally less effective than seeking donations for ten hours a week, proving that eight hours a week is the ideal amount of time to spend fundraising. Those two extra hours could be reallocated to a function that needs more attention, and that would generate a bigger return.

This approach to time management could be revolutionary for elected officials and any government employee who has too little time to carry out too many responsibilities. A responsible distribution of time commitments can result in a more efficient government, one better equipped to meet the needs of its community.

### Challenges of Using Six Sigma in the Government Sector

**Checks and Balances**

The United States Constitution was written with an emphasis on checks and balances. In other words, none of the three branches of government — judicial, executive, and legislative — should be able to gain too much power. Should one of those three branches become more prominent than the others, the remaining two branches have the means to use their power to rein in the growing power. On paper, this ensures no branch of government can amass too much influence.

Today, the threat when it comes to gaining power isn't so much the traditional checks-and-balances model, but rather the party system used in the United States. Republican officials are inclined to vote in favor of Republican measures, while striking down Democratic initiatives, and vice versa. The appeal of staying aligned with the party is one of solidarity. If a member of Congress is a Democrat and wants to pass a specific action, they'll receive far more support if that member of Congress typically supports their party on a regular basis.

Note that these decisions often have little to do with the actual preferences of the individuals in question. That is, politicians routinely pass laws they don't actually believe in, and veto measures they genuinely like, just to stay in their political parties' good graces. Again, the idea is that the whole party will support a politician if they work with that party on its ideas. However, the potential always remains for a perfectly good improvement proposal to die in a vote, even if it was backed by a Six Sigma project, and the data clearly shows it's a good idea.

**Limited Amount of Time**

Elected government officials take office with the trust of the people and of their respective parties. Unfortunately, the one thing they don't have going for them is time.

Elected officials' terms ensure a variety of voices contribute to solving the problems faced by the people of a given area. They also ensure no politician can rest on their laurels. They need to deliver results, and they need to do so with enough time left for voters to notice the improvements before the next election.

Six Sigma can be helpful to government officials in so many ways. However, the solutions carried out as a result of Six Sigma aren't always time-sensitive. Gathering data, assessing a flawed process, and working out ways to improve that process all take time, as does building the ultimate solution and training staff on that revised model. It's entirely possible the desired changes won't be seen until after the official has left office.

These occurrences are unfortunate, but they can't be avoided without compromising the quality of the solution. It's government's job to do what's needed to help people, even if the timeline for those improvements stretches beyond one official's term. It falls to each individual in office to make sure the future carries forward the updated processes and efficient workflow that were put in place through Six Sigma.

**Getting Buy-In**

The unfortunate reality of politics and government is that not everyone is willing to respect the wishes of other politicians. Every politician has their idea of how things should be, and every elected official is eager to see those ideas implemented as quickly as possible.

At the same time, politicians with a big vision for the future may see their ideas limited by the people above them. This can happen for a variety of reasons. The party-line issue, already mentioned, can shift politicians' opinions; beyond that, personal philosophies can take center stage, or more senior politicians may simply want to take credit for the ideas of people further down the ladder.

Whatever the situation, the fact remains that not every good idea comes to pass, and it's often because of a politician's ability to lobby for the needed support. This is unfortunate but unavoidable, because of the no-win dilemma involved. Six Sigma demands full buy-in from every stakeholder who stands to benefit from a project, but most senior politicians won't consider an improved process unless they can see the initiative's projected results. This results in improvements that are partially carried out — or not carried out at all — through no fault of the Six Sigma project team. This can happen even if the team asked for permission before embarking on the project.

### Six Sigma Case Study: Process Improvement in Houston, Texas

Houston, Texas, is the fourth-largest city in the United States. While the city's population has grown steadily over the years — Houston now counts more than 2 million residents — its finances haven't kept pace with the population growth. Despite Houston's $2 billion budget, the city found itself facing a $140 million deficit in July 2015.

Six Sigma has been part of Houston's infrastructure, in various forms, throughout the 2010s. However, once the deficit grew to such a high number in 2015, it was time for Houston to bring in its most experienced staff. Since many city employees had 20 to 30 years on the job, this shift was necessary, so those employees could keep their jobs and keep contributing to the city's well-being.

Houston's Six Sigma team started looking for ways to make flawed processes more efficient. One major source of defects wasn't hard to find. The city's External Services department was responsible for confirming and paying for the city's equipment rentals to other groups. Unfortunately, the department suffered from rampant inefficiency. It typically took the External Services department 111 days to resolve invoices received from vendors. That was far from the city's desired goal of 30 days. At the department's lowest point, vendors were shutting off services and calling the mayor's office directly, demanding answers.

The Six Sigma team implemented a series of process-improvement measures, which helped them develop a revised process for handling outside vendors. These methods included process mapping, waste elimination, and developing a system to verify whether External Services was actually responsible for a given invoice. The improved methods proved highly successful. After starting with an average cycle time of 111 days, External Services cut its average response time to 2.5 days. In addition, the group was able to shrink its staff from six employees to one.

However, that didn't mean those extra employees lost their jobs. In fact, Houston reinvested a variety of resources into its Six Sigma projects, including human capital. Houston's staff now counts 1,000 Six Sigma yellow belts and more than 50 Six Sigma green belts. The city is also developing a program to train black belts. In addition, the increased efficiency found across every city process means Houston doesn't need to hire new staff, saving $1 million a year in hiring costs.

Houston's long-serving employees not only continue to serve the community, they get to do so in new and exciting ways, which have reinvigorated the city's entire staff. The future is bright for Houston, just as it is for any city that implements Six Sigma and keeps investing in its solutions.

### Tips for Using Six Sigma in the Government Sector

Six Sigma is a great resource, for both government employees and elected officials alike. Since any improvement to government operations has a ripple effect that ultimately helps a lot of people, even the smallest Six Sigma initiative can carry large-scale benefits.

Inefficiency in government hurts everyone — elected officials and public employees feel the strain of flawed processes, and no one faces the impact of these processes more than the citizens of those affected areas. Raising any ideas that could help improve government's flow — ideas, money, time, etc. — has the potential to greatly benefit operations.

Government officials may be reluctant to carry out Six Sigma projects, out of fear they might highlight the things they're doing wrong. However, asking for help is the best way to use Six Sigma to its full potential. Talking with everyone involved in a process — from the most important people to the lowest-ranking officials — will help bring about a solution that benefits every party involved.

Politicians should always remember that the purpose of any Six Sigma project is to streamline internal operations, to help the people who actually need assistance. The focus should never stray from that goal. Re-elections and fundraising need to take a distant back seat to what actually matters.
