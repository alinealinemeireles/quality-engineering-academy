## Chapter 21: Continuous Flow and the Pull System
### The engineering question

*What authorizes production of the next part: a forecast or actual consumption?*

That single question separates a push system from a pull system, and the consequences ripple through the entire quality system.

### Push and Pull

**Push system.** Production is authorized by a plan built from forecast. Each station produces at its own pace and hands off to the next, regardless of whether the next one needs it. Decoupling is done with stock.

- Consequence 1: WIP builds up wherever the paces don't match.
- Consequence 2: by Little's Law, lead time grows with WIP.
- Consequence 3 (the one that matters to quality): the defect gets detected many hours or days after it was generated, and by then an entire lot is already affected.

**Pull system.** Production is authorized by a downstream consumption signal. A station only produces when the next station has pulled something out. WIP is capped by design — not by discipline.

- Consequence 1: WIP has an explicit, known ceiling.
- Consequence 2: lead time becomes predictable.
- Consequence 3: when something stops, it stops visibly and fast. This is a feature, not a flaw, of the system.

> **Pull isn't "produce less."** It's "produce only when consumption authorization exists." Installed capacity stays the same; what changes is the trigger criterion.

### Continuous flow: the ideal and the reality

Continuous flow (*one-piece flow*) means a part moves from operation to operation without stopping and without forming a batch. It's lean's ideal because it drives WIP to its theoretical minimum and the quality feedback loop down to one cycle.

In practice it isn't always achievable. The real barriers are:

| Barrier | Example | Lean solution |
|---|---|---|
| Long changeover times | A press with a 90-min setup | **SMED** |
| Processes that are batch by physical nature | Oven, treatment bath, autoclave | Supermarket + FIFO upstream and downstream |
| Physical distance | Operations in different buildings | Layout redesign, U-shaped cells |
| Low reliability | Equipment with frequent stoppages | **TPM** |
| High mix with erratic demand | 40 part numbers, erratic demand | **Heijunka** |

When continuous flow isn't possible at a stretch, the lean answer **isn't** to go back to push: it's to place a controlled **supermarket** at that point, with a defined maximum quantity, and keep pull between supermarkets.

### FIFO, Supermarket, and CONWIP

Three flow-control mechanisms, for different situations:

- **FIFO lane** — links two operations with a fixed maximum quantity and preserved order. When it fills up, the upstream operation **stops**. Simple and suitable when there's no part-number variety between the two stations.
- **Supermarket** — controlled stock of several part numbers, with a minimum and maximum quantity per number. The upstream operation replenishes what was consumed. Suitable when there's mix, or when the upstream process runs in batches.
- **CONWIP (*constant work in process*)** — limits the total WIP of the stretch instead of limiting it per part number. A part only enters when another leaves. It's the mechanism closest to what software kanban systems (boards with per-column limits) implement.

### Worked example: what's the lead-time cost of pushing?

A simulation of two operations with variable times, comparing push (no WIP limit) and pull (limited buffer).

```py-r
--- python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(20260818)

def simular(n_pecas=2000, limite_buffer=None, ct_medio=(10.0, 11.0), cv=0.35):
    """Duas operações em série. limite_buffer=None => push (buffer infinito)."""
    ct1 = rng.gamma(1/cv**2, ct_medio[0]*cv**2, n_pecas)   # tempos de ciclo variáveis
    ct2 = rng.gamma(1/cv**2, ct_medio[1]*cv**2, n_pecas)

    t_saida_op1 = np.zeros(n_pecas)
    t_saida_op2 = np.zeros(n_pecas)
    entrada = np.zeros(n_pecas)

    for i in range(n_pecas):
        # OP1 só pode começar se houver espaço no buffer (regra pull)
        pronto_op1 = t_saida_op1[i-1] if i > 0 else 0.0
        if limite_buffer is not None and i >= limite_buffer:
            # espera que a peça (i - limite) saia da OP2 para libertar espaço
            pronto_op1 = max(pronto_op1, t_saida_op2[i - limite_buffer])
        entrada[i] = pronto_op1
        t_saida_op1[i] = pronto_op1 + ct1[i]
        inicio_op2 = max(t_saida_op1[i], t_saida_op2[i-1] if i > 0 else 0.0)
        t_saida_op2[i] = inicio_op2 + ct2[i]

    lead_times = t_saida_op2 - entrada
    burn_in = n_pecas // 4          # descarta arranque
    lt = lead_times[burn_in:]
    throughput = (n_pecas - burn_in) / (t_saida_op2[-1] - t_saida_op2[burn_in])
    wip = throughput * lt.mean()    # Lei de Little
    return dict(LeadTime_medio=lt.mean(), LeadTime_p95=np.percentile(lt, 95),
                Throughput=throughput, WIP_Little=wip)

linhas = []
for nome, lim in [("PUSH (sem limite)", None), ("PULL buffer=10", 10),
                  ("PULL buffer=5", 5), ("PULL buffer=2", 2)]:
    r = simular(limite_buffer=lim); r["Sistema"] = nome; linhas.append(r)

res = pd.DataFrame(linhas)[["Sistema", "LeadTime_medio", "LeadTime_p95",
                            "Throughput", "WIP_Little"]]
res.columns = ["Sistema", "Lead time médio (s)", "Lead time p95 (s)",
               "Throughput (pç/s)", "WIP (Lei de Little)"]
print(res.round(2).to_string(index=False))

base = res.iloc[0]
print("\nComparação com o push:")
for _, r in res.iloc[1:].iterrows():
    dlt = 1 - r["Lead time médio (s)"] / base["Lead time médio (s)"]
    dth = 1 - r["Throughput (pç/s)"] / base["Throughput (pç/s)"]
    print(f"  {r['Sistema']:<16} lead time {dlt:+.1%} | throughput {-dth:+.1%}")
```

**Engineering reading.** The result is pull's central argument, and it's counterintuitive to anyone who manages by occupancy: **limiting WIP cuts lead time drastically and costs very little throughput**. Only when the buffer gets too tight (2 parts) does OP2 start running short on material often, and throughput drops noticeably.

The engineering decision point is choosing the smallest buffer that doesn't sacrifice throughput materially — and then **attacking variability** (cv) to be able to tighten it further. Buffer is the price paid for variability; reducing variability is what makes the buffer unnecessary.

### Common mistakes

1. **Implementing kanban with WIP equal to current stock.** Nothing changes. Kanban only has an effect when the number of cards is lower than the WIP that existed before.
2. **Confusing pull with just-in-time with no stock.** Pull has stock — controlled, sized, and visible. Zero stock with variable processes is a recipe for stoppages.
3. **Pulling from the forecast.** If the replenishment signal comes from MRP and not from consumption, it's push with colored cards.
4. **Tightening the buffer before fixing reliability.** In a system with frequent breakdowns, tightening WIP turns every breakdown into a full line stop. TPM comes first.
5. **Measuring pull's success by machine occupancy.** In a pull system, machines idle for lack of consumption authorization is the correct behavior. Measuring occupancy induces overproduction.
