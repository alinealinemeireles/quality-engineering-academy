## Chapter 135: Bootstrap, Permutation, Power, Effect Size and Multiple Testing
### The engineering question

> *"I need a confidence interval for Cpk, and the textbook formula assumes normality my data
> doesn't have. And I need to know whether the difference I found is worth anything, not just
> whether it's 'significant.'"*

This chapter covers the four skills that separate an analysis that survives scrutiny from one that
doesn't: **quantifying uncertainty without a closed-form formula** (bootstrap), **testing without
distributional assumptions** (permutation), **sizing before measuring** (power and MDE), and
**not fooling yourself when testing many things** (FDR).

---

## 135.1 Bootstrap: the sampling distribution by resampling

The idea is simple and profound. Don't know the sampling distribution of a statistic θ̂?
Resample **with replacement** from the observed sample, recompute θ̂ on each resample, and use the
distribution of those values as an approximation of the sampling distribution.

**Where it's indispensable in quality:**

- confidence interval for **Cpk** (the classic formula assumes normality and is fragile);
- interval for **percentiles** (P95 of cycle time, P99.865 of a characteristic);
- interval for **ratios** (ratio of defect rates between two lines);
- interval for the **median** and other robust statistics;
- interval for model metrics (AUC, economic gain).

**Variants, in order of practical preference:**

| Method | How | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Percentile | 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the bootstrap distribution | Fast; suited when the bootstrap distribution is roughly symmetric and unbiased |
| BCa | corrects for bias and acceleration (skewness) | Generally preferable, especially for Cpk and percentiles |
| Bootstrap-t | studentized | Better coverage, but requires a variance estimator |

**What bootstrap does NOT do** — and this is the most common misunderstanding:

- **it doesn't create new information.** It resamples the existing information. With n = 8 the
  interval will be wide, and it should be;
- **it doesn't correct selection bias.** If the sample doesn't represent the population, bootstrap
  faithfully reproduces the bias;
- **it doesn't solve dependence.** With autocorrelated data you need a *block bootstrap*, which
  resamples contiguous blocks instead of individual observations.

---

## 135.2 Permutation tests: inference from the design

If two groups were formed by **random assignment**, then under the null hypothesis of no effect the
labels are **exchangeable**: any relabeling is as likely as the one observed.

The procedure:

1. Compute the observed statistic (difference of means, of medians, whatever matters).
2. Shuffle the labels thousands of times, recomputing the statistic on each shuffle.
3. The p-value is the fraction of permutations with a statistic as or more extreme than observed.

**Advantages:** it doesn't require normality, it works with any statistic (including ones with no
theory), and its validity comes from the **design**, not from an assumption about the population.

**Limitation:** it requires exchangeability under H0. With grouped data, you permute the
**groups**, not individual observations — the same caution about pseudoreplication as in Chapter 134.

## 135.3 Power, MDE, and effect size

Sample-size calculation is the only moment when you get to **decide** the risk of being wrong. Once
the data has been collected, there's no choice left.

The four parameters are rigidly linked — fix three and the fourth is determined:

| Parameter | Meaning | Typical choice |
|---|---|---|
| α | risk of a false positive | 0.05 (but 0.01 if the action is expensive/irreversible) |
| β | risk of a false negative | 0.20 → power = 80%; 0.10 → power = 90% |
| δ | minimum difference worth detecting (**MDE**) | **an engineering decision, not a statistical one** |
| n | sample size | the thing you compute |

**MDE is an engineering and economic decision.** The question isn't "what difference do I expect to
find?" but **"what difference is big enough that I'd change something?"** If a 0.2% reduction in
scrap doesn't pay for the investment, there's no point sizing the study to detect it.

### Effect size

The p-value conflates magnitude with sample size: with a large n, irrelevant differences reach
p < 0.001. **Effect size** separates the two.

- **Cohen's d** = (x̄₁ − x̄₂)/s_pooled. Rough benchmarks: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.
- **Absolute difference in engineering units** — almost always more informative than d.
  "The mean rose by 0.04 mm" tells an engineer more than "d = 0.7."

> **Rule:** always report the **confidence interval of the effect in physical units**. It contains
> the information in the p-value (whether it includes zero) plus the information about magnitude and
> precision — which the p-value doesn't have.

### *Post hoc* power analysis: don't do it

Computing power **after** getting a non-significant result, using the observed effect, is an empty
operation: power computed this way is a deterministic function of the p-value and adds no
information at all. The right question after a non-significant result is
"**what effects are compatible with this data?**" — and you answer that with the confidence interval.

## 135.4 Multiple testing: FWER and FDR

Testing 20 characteristics at α = 0.05 each gives you a **64%** probability of at least one false
positive (1 − 0.95²⁰). In a typical Analyze phase, with dozens of candidate x's, some
"significant" ones show up by construction.

Two families of correction, with different philosophies:

**FWER — Family-Wise Error Rate.** Controls P(≥1 false positive).
- *Bonferroni:* α' = α/m. Simple, always valid, very conservative with large m.
- *Holm:* sequential, uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni, equally valid.

**FDR — False Discovery Rate.** Controls the **expected proportion of false positives among the
discoveries**.
- *Benjamini-Hochberg:* sorts the p-values, compares p₍ᵢ₎ with (i/m)·q. Much more powerful with large m.

**Which to use in quality?**

- **Exploratory screening** (which x's deserve investigation?) → **FDR**. You accept that some
  fraction of the leads will be false; the cost of following one is one extra experiment.
- **Confirmatory decision** (release a lot, approve a change, safety claim) → **FWER**.
  The cost of a false positive is high and asymmetric.

> **The correction no software applies:** the informal tests you ran and didn't report also count.
> If you looked at 15 charts and tested whichever looked interesting, the real error rate is that of
> 15 tests, not one.

## 135.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Bootstrapping a non-representative sample | A precise interval around the wrong number | Bootstrap doesn't correct selection bias |
| Bootstrapping while ignoring autocorrelation | Interval too narrow | *Block bootstrap* |
| Permuting observations in grouped data | Invalid p-value | Permute at the level of the experimental unit |
| Setting MDE from the effect you expect | A study sized for something irrelevant | MDE = smallest effect that changes the decision |
| Reporting only the p-value | Conflates magnitude with sample size | CI of the effect in physical units |
| *Post hoc* power | Circular calculation, no information | CI of the effect |
| Ignoring multiple testing | False discoveries in the Analyze phase | BH (exploration) or Holm (confirmation) |

```py-r
--- python
# ============================================================================
# Capitulo 135 - Exemplo resolvido
# (1) Bootstrap: IC para Cpk quando a formula classica falha
# (2) Teste por permutacao vs teste t com dados assimetricos
# (3) Poder / MDE: curva de dimensionamento
# (4) Multiplos testes: Bonferroni vs Holm vs Benjamini-Hochberg
# ============================================================================
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(135)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (1)
print("=" * 74)
print("(1) BOOTSTRAP - intervalo de confianca para Cpk (dados NAO normais)")
print("=" * 74)

LIE, LSE = 8.0, 12.0
# Processo real: lognormal deslocada -> assimetrico a direita
dados = 8.6 + rng.lognormal(mean=0.0, sigma=0.45, size=60)

def cpk(x, lie=LIE, lse=LSE):
    mu, sd = np.mean(x), np.std(x, ddof=1)
    return min((lse - mu) / (3 * sd), (mu - lie) / (3 * sd))

cpk_obs = cpk(dados)
p_norm = stats.shapiro(dados).pvalue

B = 10_000
boot = np.array([cpk(rng.choice(dados, size=len(dados), replace=True)) for _ in range(B)])

# --- IC percentil
ic_perc = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])

# --- IC BCa (corrige vies e aceleracao)
z0 = stats.norm.ppf((boot < cpk_obs).mean())
jack = np.array([cpk(np.delete(dados, i)) for i in range(len(dados))])
jbar = jack.mean()
a = ((jbar - jack) ** 3).sum() / (6.0 * (((jbar - jack) ** 2).sum()) ** 1.5)
def bca_lim(alpha):
    z = stats.norm.ppf(alpha)
    return stats.norm.cdf(z0 + (z0 + z) / (1 - a * (z0 + z)))
ic_bca = np.percentile(boot, [100 * bca_lim(0.025), 100 * bca_lim(0.975)])

# --- IC classico (assume normalidade) - para comparacao
n = len(dados)
se_aprox = cpk_obs * np.sqrt(1 / (9 * n * cpk_obs**2) + 1 / (2 * (n - 1)))
ic_class = (cpk_obs - 1.96 * se_aprox, cpk_obs + 1.96 * se_aprox)

print(f"  n = {n} | Shapiro-Wilk p = {p_norm:.4f}  "
      f"({'NAO normal' if p_norm < 0.05 else 'compativel com normal'})")
print(f"  Cpk pontual                  : {cpk_obs:.4f}")
print(f"  IC 95% classico (normalidade): [{ic_class[0]:.4f}, {ic_class[1]:.4f}]")
print(f"  IC 95% bootstrap percentil   : [{ic_perc[0]:.4f}, {ic_perc[1]:.4f}]")
print(f"  IC 95% bootstrap BCa         : [{ic_bca[0]:.4f}, {ic_bca[1]:.4f}]")
print("\n  Leitura de engenharia: um Cpk PONTUAL acima de 1,33 nao autoriza")
print("  a conclusao de que o processo e capaz se o LIMITE INFERIOR do intervalo")
print("  estiver abaixo. Em PPAP e o limite inferior que sustenta a alegacao.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (2)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(2) TESTE POR PERMUTACAO vs TESTE t  -  tempos de ciclo assimetricos")
print("=" * 74)

a_grp = rng.gamma(shape=2.0, scale=6.0, size=60)
b_grp = rng.gamma(shape=2.0, scale=8.4, size=60)

dif_obs = a_grp.mean() - b_grp.mean()
juntos = np.concatenate([a_grp, b_grp])
n_a = len(a_grp)

n_perm = 20_000
difs = np.empty(n_perm)
for i in range(n_perm):
    perm = rng.permutation(juntos)
    difs[i] = perm[:n_a].mean() - perm[n_a:].mean()

p_perm = (np.abs(difs) >= abs(dif_obs)).mean()
p_t = stats.ttest_ind(a_grp, b_grp, equal_var=False).pvalue
p_mw = stats.mannwhitneyu(a_grp, b_grp).pvalue

# IC bootstrap para a diferenca (o que realmente interessa reportar)
db = np.array([rng.choice(a_grp, n_a, True).mean() - rng.choice(b_grp, len(b_grp), True).mean()
               for _ in range(10_000)])
ic_dif = np.percentile(db, [2.5, 97.5])
d_cohen = dif_obs / np.sqrt(((n_a-1)*a_grp.var(ddof=1) + (len(b_grp)-1)*b_grp.var(ddof=1))
                            / (n_a + len(b_grp) - 2))

print(f"  media A = {a_grp.mean():6.2f} min | media B = {b_grp.mean():6.2f} min")
print(f"  diferenca observada  : {dif_obs:+.3f} min")
print(f"  p (permutacao, 20k)  : {p_perm:.4f}   <- valido sem hipotese distribucional")
print(f"  p (Welch t)          : {p_t:.4f}")
print(f"  p (Mann-Whitney)     : {p_mw:.4f}")
print(f"  IC 95% da diferenca  : [{ic_dif[0]:+.3f}, {ic_dif[1]:+.3f}] min")
print(f"  d de Cohen           : {d_cohen:+.3f}")
print("\n  O IC na unidade fisica (minutos) e o que permite a decisao economica.")
print("  O valor-p sozinho nao diz se a diferenca paga a mudanca.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (3)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(3) PODER e MDE  -  dimensionar ANTES de medir")
print("=" * 74)

def n_por_grupo(delta, sigma, alfa=0.05, poder=0.80):
    """n por grupo, teste t bilateral de 2 amostras (aproximacao normal + ajuste)."""
    z_a = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alfa / 2)
    z_b = stats.norm.ppf(poder)
    n0 = 2 * ((z_a + z_b) * sigma / delta) ** 2
    return int(np.ceil(n0)) + 1

sigma = 2.0
print(f"  sigma do processo = {sigma}  |  alfa = 0,05  |  poder = 80%\n")
print(f"  {'MDE (delta)':>12} {'d de Cohen':>12} {'n por grupo':>12}")
print("  " + "-" * 38)
for delta in (0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0):
    print(f"  {delta:>12.2f} {delta/sigma:>12.2f} {n_por_grupo(delta, sigma):>12d}")
print("\n  Relacao chave: n cresce com o INVERSO DO QUADRADO do MDE.")
print("  Detetar metade do efeito custa QUATRO vezes mais amostra.")
print("  Por isso definir o MDE pela economia (o que muda a decisao) e nao pela")
print("  esperanca (o que gostaria de encontrar) e a decisao mais importante.")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ (4)
print("\n" + "=" * 74)
print("(4) MULTIPLOS TESTES  -  20 caracteristicas, 3 com efeito real")
print("=" * 74)

m = 20
n_reais = 3          # 3 caracteristicas tem efeito real; 17 nao tem nenhum
efeito = 0.9
n_obs = 30
alfa = 0.05
N_REP = 1000         # repete a "fase Analisar" 1000 vezes

def corrigir(pvals, alfa=0.05):
    m = len(pvals)
    ordem = np.argsort(pvals)
    p_ord = pvals[ordem]
    bonf = pvals < alfa / m
    holm = np.zeros(m, dtype=bool)
    for i, idx in enumerate(ordem):
        if p_ord[i] < alfa / (m - i):
            holm[idx] = True
        else:
            break
    bh = np.zeros(m, dtype=bool)
    k = np.where(p_ord <= (np.arange(1, m + 1) / m) * alfa)[0]
    if len(k):
        bh[ordem[: k.max() + 1]] = True
    return {"Sem correcao": pvals < alfa, "Bonferroni (FWER)": bonf,
            "Holm (FWER)": holm, "Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR)": bh}

metodos = ["Sem correcao", "Bonferroni (FWER)", "Holm (FWER)", "Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR)"]
acum = {k: {"vp": 0, "fp": 0, "rep_com_fp": 0, "n_desc": 0, "fdr_soma": 0.0} for k in metodos}

efeitos = np.zeros(m); efeitos[:n_reais] = efeito
for _ in range(N_REP):
    pvals = np.empty(m)
    for j in range(m):
        g1 = rng.normal(0, 1, n_obs)
        g2 = rng.normal(efeitos[j], 1, n_obs)
        pvals[j] = stats.ttest_ind(g1, g2).pvalue
    for nome, mask in corrigir(pvals, alfa).items():
        acum[nome]["vp"] += mask[:n_reais].sum()
        acum[nome]["fp"] += mask[n_reais:].sum()
        acum[nome]["n_desc"] += mask.sum()
        acum[nome]["rep_com_fp"] += int(mask[n_reais:].any())
        # FDR correta: E[V/R], com V/R = 0 quando R = 0 (nao a razao de esperancas)
        R = mask.sum()
        acum[nome]["fdr_soma"] += (mask[n_reais:].sum() / R) if R > 0 else 0.0

print(f"  {n_reais} de {m} caracteristicas tem efeito real (d = {efeito}), "
      f"n = {n_obs} por grupo")
print(f"  Resultados medios sobre {N_REP} repeticoes da fase Analisar\n")
print(f"  {'Metodo':<26}{'Poder':>8}{'FP medios':>11}{'P(>=1 FP)':>11}{'FDR real':>10}")
print("  " + "-" * 66)
for nome in metodos:
    a = acum[nome]
    poder = a["vp"] / (N_REP * n_reais)
    fp_med = a["fp"] / N_REP
    p_fp = a["rep_com_fp"] / N_REP
    fdr = a["fdr_soma"] / N_REP        # E[V/R] - a definicao correta de FDR
    print(f"  {nome:<26}{100*poder:>7.1f}%{fp_med:>11.2f}{100*p_fp:>10.1f}%{100*fdr:>9.1f}%")
print("  " + "-" * 66)
print("\n  Poder      = % dos efeitos REAIS detetados")
print("  P(>=1 FP)  = % de analises com pelo menos um falso positivo (o que FWER controla)")
print("  FDR real   = E[V/R] com V/R=0 quando R=0 - a definicao correta.")
print("               E isto que o BH controla, ao nivel q = 5%.")
print("               (Cuidado: a razao de somas, sum(V)/sum(R), NAO e a FDR e")
print("                da valores sistematicamente diferentes.)")
print("\n  Sem correcao: ~"
      f"{100*acum['Sem correcao']['rep_com_fp']/N_REP:.0f}% das analises produzem")
print("  pelo menos uma falsa descoberta. BH mantem o poder alto e a FDR controlada;")
print("  Bonferroni/Holm sao mais seguros mas perdem efeitos reais.")
```

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Bootstrapping the Cpk.**
*Using the data in the cell above, check whether the process can be declared capable (Cpk ≥ 1.33) with
95% confidence.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The right question isn't "is the point Cpk ≥ 1.33?" but **"is the lower bound of the confidence
interval ≥ 1.33?"**

A point Cpk of 1.40 with a 95% CI of [0.98, 1.85] does **not** support a capability claim: the data
is compatible with a frankly incapable process. Capability is only declared when the interval's
lower bound clears the criterion.

**Practical consequence in PPAP:** this is why automotive customers require a minimum number of
parts (typically 100 for Ppk), and why increasing the sample "improves" the reported Cpk —
not because the process changes, but because the interval narrows. The point doesn't move
systematically; the lower bound rises.

To narrow the interval, there are two paths: more data (the interval narrows with √n) or less
variation (the process improves). Only the second one improves the product.

</details>

**E2 — Permutation with grouped data.**
*You have 6 lots, 3 treated and 3 control, with 40 parts measured per lot. How do you run the
permutation test correctly?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Wrong:** pool all 240 parts and permute labels part by part. That assumes the parts are
exchangeable under H0 — and they're not: parts from the same lot share conditions. It's the same
pseudoreplication from Chapter 134, now hidden inside a nonparametric method.

**Correct:** permute at the level of the **experimental unit**, i.e., the **lots**.

1. Reduce each lot to a summary statistic (mean, or median if there's skew) → 6 values.
2. Compute the observed difference between the means of the 3 treated and 3 control lots.
3. Enumerate all C(6,3) = **20** possible assignments of 3 lots to the treated group.
4. The p-value is the fraction of those 20 with a difference as or more extreme.

**Unavoidable consequence:** with only 20 possible permutations, the smallest two-sided p-value
achievable is 2/20 = **0.10**. No result can be significant at 5%, no matter how strong the effect.
This isn't a limitation of the method — it's the method telling the truth about the information
available: **6 experimental units are too few.**

The engineering conclusion is about the design, not the analysis: if the decision matters, you need
more lots. The 40 parts per lot improve the precision of the estimate **within** each lot and don't
make up for the lack of replicates at the level that matters.

</details>

**E3 — MDE and economics.**
*Current scrap is 4%. A reduction to 3.5% would pay for the investment in 8 months; to 3.8%, in 30
months (unacceptable). What MDE should you use?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**MDE = 0.5 percentage points** (from 4.0% to 3.5%). This is the smallest effect that changes the
decision — and that, not expectation, is the operational definition of the MDE.

Sizing for proportions, α = 0.05 two-sided, 80% power:

n per group ≈ [(z_{α/2}+z_β)² · (p₁(1−p₁) + p₂(1−p₂))] / (p₁−p₂)²
with p₁ = 0.040, p₂ = 0.035 → n ≈ **(1.96+0.84)² × (0.0384+0.0338) / 0.000025 ≈ 22,600 parts per group**.

The result is the most valuable piece of information in the exercise: **detecting half a percentage
point in a 4% process requires tens of thousands of parts per group.** This has three practical
consequences:

1. If that volume isn't available, the simple comparative study **isn't feasible** — and it's
   better to know that before spending three months gathering data that won't conclude anything.
2. Alternatives: use a **continuous** variable correlated with the defect (far more efficient per
   observation), use a **paired design** (same machine before/after, which removes machine-to-machine
   variation), or apply **DOE** (which estimates several effects with the same number of runs).
3. If the volume exists (on a high-throughput line, 22,600 parts might be two days), the study is
   feasible and the sizing is justified.

**Mistake to avoid:** sizing for 0.2 percentage points "for margin." Since n grows with the inverse
square of the MDE, going from 0.5 to 0.2 multiplies the sample by 6.25 — to detect an effect that,
by assumption, doesn't pay for the investment.

</details>

**E4 — FDR in the Analyze phase.**
*You tested 40 process variables against the defect rate and got 6 with p < 0.05. How do you proceed?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**First, calibrate expectations.** If none of the 40 had an effect, the expected number of
"significant" ones at α = 0.05 would be 40 × 0.05 = **2**. Finding 6 is more than expected by
chance, but not by a huge margin — and some of the 6 are almost certainly false.

**Apply Benjamini-Hochberg** with q = 0.10 (reasonable for exploratory screening):

1. Sort the 40 p-values in ascending order: p₍₁₎ ≤ … ≤ p₍₄₀₎.
2. Find the largest i such that p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ (i/40) × 0.10.
3. Reject every hypothesis up to that position i.

The interpretation of q = 0.10 is: **among the declared discoveries, roughly 10% are expected to be
false**. If 4 variables survive, roughly 0.4 are expected to be false — which is acceptable in a
screening phase, where the cost of chasing a false lead is one extra experiment.

**Second, and more important: BH doesn't finish the job.** In an Analyze phase, these variables are
the result of **observational** analysis — subject to confounding (Chapter 137). The next step
isn't to implement, it's to **confirm by experiment**: a DOE with the surviving 3-4 variables
establishes causality and quantifies effects and interactions in a way observation can't.

**Third, the correction software doesn't apply:** if, before these 40 formal tests, you looked at
charts and chose which variables to test, the effective number of comparisons is larger than 40.
Informal tests count. The honest defense is to state the selection process in the report.

</details>
