## Chapter 14: Customer Relations

### Customer needs and wants

The organizational goal should be making sure customers want and need the company's products or services. Keeping direct, in-person contact with customers, in their own environment, is the surest way to fight what's called organizational myopia — a system's tendency to develop in such a way that customers end up adapting to how the supplying organization does things, when it should be the other way around. This happens across practically every kind of business, from small local shops to large-scale manufacturing organizations.

The quality engineer's role — whether at a manufacturing organization or a service provider — is to help the organization see that customers are the reason it exists, not the other way around. Surveys and questionnaires remain the primary method for measuring customer satisfaction and loyalty, now supplemented by online surveys, but special attention is needed for the measurement error inherent to these instruments, since response rates tend to be low — the goal should be getting measurements that faithfully represent customers' actual attitudes. It's important to go beyond simply collecting a sample of opinions (surveys, focus groups, facility visits): satisfaction cards at hotels and restaurants are an everyday example, but it's worth questioning the validity of this kind of effort, given the response rate and its non-randomness. This kind of data collection — sitting, in the terminology of four-stage evaluation models like Kirkpatrick's, at the "reaction" level, or level one — is sometimes disparagingly called "smiley sheets," referring to the halo effect resulting from in-the-moment enthusiasm at the event, or the respondent's desire to please whoever's running the survey. The real question the quality engineer should ask is: what do my customers think after using the product or service for a while, under real conditions, and what are they telling other people about my organization?

### Quality Function Deployment (QFD)

Quality function deployment (QFD) is a powerful planning technique, perhaps the most comprehensive ever devised for quality planning. QFD is a general concept that provides a means for translating customer requirements into the appropriate technical requirements for each stage of product development and production — marketing strategies, planning, product design and engineering, prototype evaluation, production process development, production, and sales. QFD is especially well suited to large-scale products, like airplanes and automobiles, which have heavy tooling, high design costs, and many optional features that need to be selected and then produced or purchased. It was introduced to American industry in the 1980s by the American Supplier Institute.

Five key terms are associated with QFD: the voice of the customer (VOC), which is the customer's requirements expressed in their own terms; counterpart characteristics, an expression of the voice of the customer in technical language that specifies the quality the customer requires; product quality deployment, that is, the activities needed to translate the voice of the customer into counterpart characteristics; quality function deployment proper, that is, the activities needed to make sure the quality the customer requires is actually achieved, by assigning specific quality responsibilities to specific departments (any activity needed to ensure quality is, by definition, a quality function, no matter which department carries it out); and quality tables, a series of matrices used to translate the voice of the customer into the product's final control characteristics.

All these key relationships can be combined into a single diagram known as the house of quality, so called because it resembles a house with a sloped roof. In practice, a typical project will only need a few tools out of the more than thirty grouped under the QFD umbrella; the most common documents are the customer requirements planning matrix, the design matrix, the final product characteristics deployment matrix, the manufacturing/purchasing matrix, the process plan and quality control charts, operating instructions, and human-factors studies or surveys for capturing the voice of the customer. When applying QFD, customer expectations get translated into directly related work requirements; the basic input-output matrix organizes the process of determining the relationships between what the customer wants (generally described in non-technical terms, the "whats") and how the supplier satisfies those wants (the "hows," in technical terms). Wants generally fall into three categories — must-haves, expected, and desirable — and the strength of each relationship between a "what" and a "how" is generally rated as strong, medium, or weak, and can be converted into numeric weights to calculate each technical requirement's relative importance.

As a planning technique, QFD brings important benefits: product goals based on customer requirements aren't misinterpreted at later stages; specific marketing strategies and selling points aren't lost or diluted while translating from marketing to planning and then to execution; important production control points aren't overlooked; and efficiency increases because misinterpretations are minimized.

### Customer value analysis and customer-driven quality

Seven customer value analysis tools deserve mention: the market-perceived quality profile (an indicator of how well the organization is performing overall for customers in its target market); the market-perceived price profile (a weighted indicator of how customers perceive the price performance of the various competitors); the customer value map (which reveals a meaningful cluster of business units getting premium prices not fully backed by superior perceived quality); win/loss analysis (an analysis of the factors that caused a sale to be won or lost); the customer value head-to-head area graph (a chart showing where the organization does well and where it does worse compared to a single competitor); the key events timeline (a chronological list of the events that shifted the market's perception of the organization's and its competitors' performance on each quality attribute); and the what/who matrix (a method for tracking who's responsible for the actions that will make success with the customer possible).

A growing number of approaches focus on greater understanding of, and greater interaction with, customers. Two types of customer-driven quality have proven successful, though neither guarantees customer satisfaction on its own: reactive customer-driven quality (RCDQ), which responds to customer requirements after the fact, and planned customer-driven quality, which is anticipatory and proactive, assessing customer needs and looking for ways to satisfy them before they're even voiced. Since every organization seeking to meet customer expectations is chasing a moving target, RCDQ's reactive nature tends to leave the supplier always a step behind. Planned customer-driven quality is best achieved through some form of strategic quality planning — which isn't necessarily identical to the overall strategic planning process, though the two are closely related.

Among the typical characteristics of truly customer-centered organizations are: the total consumer experience (the ability to see, from every angle, how the organization's products and services are actually experienced in the real world); product "delighters" (using models like Kano's to continuously delight the customer with novelties they might not even have known they wanted); consumer loyalty; distribution and resale, creating a win for the organization, distributors, and customers all at once; the branding process; logistics, delivering what's needed at the point of use at the right time (JIT); build-to-order production, with lean processes capable of quick changeovers; a consumer-knowledge system, continuously gathering information about expectations and wants; e-commerce; and continuous growth through faster service, better value, and constant innovation.
