## Chapter 60: Measurement System Accuracy: Bias, Linearity, and Stability

### Bias: the gap between the observed mean and the reference value

Bias is defined as the difference between the mean of the observed measurements and a known reference value for the characteristic being measured. In simple terms, bias answers the question: on average, does the instrument measure higher or lower than the true value, and by how much?

Mathematically, bias is given by:

Bias = Observed mean − Reference value

The reference value should come from a reliable, traceable source — typically a certified standard part, measured with a higher-accuracy reference instrument, traceable to a national or international standard (like those maintained by NIST in the United States or equivalent metrology institutes in other countries). Without a reliable reference value, it's impossible to calculate bias, since there'd be nothing to compare the measurement system under evaluation against.

In practice, bias is assessed through the following procedure: a part (or a small set of parts) whose reference value is already known is selected, and that same part is measured repeatedly by the measurement system under evaluation — typically between ten and thirty times — under identical conditions (same operator, same environment, same method). The mean of these repeated measurements is then calculated and compared against the certified reference value. The difference between the two is the bias estimate.

Once bias is estimated, it's common to run a hypothesis test (for example, a one-sample t-test, comparing the observed mean against the reference value) to determine whether the bias found is statistically significant or can be attributed just to the random variation inherent to the measurement process. If the bias is found to be significant, the root cause should be investigated. Among the most common causes of significant bias are:

- An incorrect reference value, whether because the standard used isn't reliable, or because it was misidentified or swapped for another.
- An instrument worn down by use, whose contact surfaces, sensors, or scales have deteriorated over time.
- Incorrect or expired calibration of the instrument, causing it to measure in a consistently offset way.
- Incorrect use of the instrument by the operator, such as applying excessive force, positioning the part improperly, or using a reading technique different from the one specified.

Correcting an identified bias generally involves recalibrating the instrument, replacing the reference standard, retraining the operator, or, in simpler cases, simply applying a known correction factor to future readings (though this last solution is only a stopgap and doesn't eliminate the problem's root cause).

### Linearity: how bias behaves across the measurement range

While bias describes the systematic deviation at a single point on the measurement scale, linearity describes how that deviation behaves as the size or magnitude of the measured characteristic changes across the instrument's operating range. In other words, linearity answers the question: is the measurement system's bias the same for small, medium, and large parts, or does it change with part size?

An ideally accurate measurement system should show bias close to zero — or at least a constant bias — across its entire operating range. When that doesn't happen, that is, when bias grows, shrinks, or changes sign as the measured value moves along the scale, the system is said to show nonlinearity.

Linearity assessment is done by testing the instrument across several measurement ranges along its operating scale. In practice, a set of parts or reference standards (typically between five and ten) is selected whose certified reference values span the instrument's entire expected operating range — from the smallest to the largest value it'll normally measure in production. Each of these parts is then measured repeatedly (for example, ten to twelve times each), the observed mean for each part is calculated, and, from it, the bias corresponding to each level of the scale.

Once bias is calculated for each reference part, the bias values are plotted against their respective reference values on a scatter plot, and a regression line is fitted to those points. If the fitted line is approximately horizontal — that is, if its slope isn't statistically different from zero — the measurement system is concluded to be linear: bias doesn't change meaningfully as part size varies. If, instead, the line shows a slope significantly different from zero, that's evidence of nonlinearity: the instrument tends to underestimate parts of a certain size and overestimate parts of another size (or vice versa).

Nonlinearity often has specific physical causes, like uneven wear on a mechanical scale, an electronic sensor's nonlinear response outside certain operating ranges, or a calibration that was performed at only one point on the scale and so doesn't properly correct the other points. When nonlinearity is statistically confirmed and its magnitude matters relative to the process tolerance, the typical solution involves multi-point recalibration, building a correction curve or table specific to each range of the scale, or, as a last resort, replacing the instrument with one that performs better in the range of interest.

### Stability: how bias behaves over time

The third component of accuracy is stability, also called drift in some contexts. Stability measures how well the measurement system holds its performance over time — that is, it describes how bias varies when the same part, with a known (or assumed known) reference value, is measured repeatedly at different moments, over days, weeks, or months.

It's important not to confuse stability with linearity. Linearity assesses bias behavior at a single point in time, but at different points on the measurement scale (parts of different sizes). Stability, meanwhile, assesses bias behavior at the same point on the scale (the same standard part), but across different points in time. While linearity answers "does bias change with part size?", stability answers "does this same part's bias change with the passage of time and continued use of the instrument?"

The standard way to assess a measurement system's stability is to periodically measure the same reference standard part (or a check standard dedicated to this purpose) and log those measurements on a control chart over time — typically an individuals and moving range (I-MR) chart or, when several measurements are taken on each occasion, an X-bar and range chart. Each point on the chart represents the measurement (or the average of a small group of measurements) of the standard part taken on a given day, shift, or calibration interval.

As long as the points stay within the control limits and show no non-random patterns (trends, level shifts, cycles), the measurement system is considered stable: its bias stays constant within the variation expected from common causes. When points outside the control limits appear, along with consistent upward or downward trends, or abrupt level shifts, that's evidence of a special cause affecting the system's stability — for example, progressive instrument wear, sensor degradation, contamination, uncompensated environmental changes (temperature, humidity), or even replacing a component of the instrument. This ongoing stability monitoring is, in practice, one of the statistical justifications for setting instrument calibration and recalibration intervals: when the reference standard shows signs of drift before the planned calibration interval, that interval should be shortened; when the instrument proves stable over long periods, the interval can eventually be extended.

### Putting the three accuracy components together

Bias, linearity, and stability each describe a different aspect of the same fundamental question — how far the measured value strays from the true value. Bias shows that gap at a fixed point and a fixed moment. Linearity shows how that gap behaves as the characteristic's actual value varies. Stability shows how that same gap behaves as time varies. A fully accurate measurement system should show negligible (or corrected) bias, linear behavior across its entire operating range, and stability over time, with only common-cause variation around a constant bias.

It's important to note that accuracy and precision are independent properties of a measurement system. An instrument can have excellent accuracy (bias close to zero, linear, and stable) and still have poor precision, if the variation between repeated measurements of the same part is large. It's precisely this second set of properties — repeatability and reproducibility, brought together in what's popularly known as a Gage R&R study — that will be explored in depth in this manual's next chapter.
