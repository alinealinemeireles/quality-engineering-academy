## Chapter 13: ASQ Code of Ethics for Professional Conduct

### Fundamental principles and the three expectations of the quality professional

The code's current version, published and maintained at asq.org, sets out a fundamental principle that sums up the whole standard's spirit: ASQ representatives should be honest and transparent; avoid conflicts of interest and plagiarism; do no harm to others; treat people with respect, dignity, and fairness; act professionally and in a socially responsible way; and promote the role and public perception of the quality professional. From that general principle, the code unfolds into three concrete conduct expectations.

The first expectation is to **act with integrity and honesty**. This means striving to uphold and promote the quality profession's integrity, honor, and dignity; being truthful and transparent in every professional interaction; making informed, objective decisions; accurately representing one's own qualifications, without misleading others; and providing services only within areas of genuine technical competence — a professional shouldn't, for example, agree to lead an audit in a technical area they're unfamiliar with.

The second expectation is to **demonstrate accountability, respect, and fairness**. This means putting people's, the public's, and the environment's safety, health, and well-being first; avoiding any conduct that harms the society's or the profession's reputation; treating others with fairness, courtesy, dignity, and without prejudice; behaving professionally; and respecting diversity of opinion.

The third expectation is to **protect proprietary information and avoid conflicts of interest**. This covers protecting the integrity of confidential information received from employers, clients, or colleagues; not using confidential information for personal gain; proactively disclosing any conflict of interest that could compromise the professional's objectivity; giving proper credit for others' work; and not plagiarizing or using someone else's intellectual property without authorization.

### Ethics in practice: common dilemmas for the quality engineer

Beyond the code itself, the QE needs to stay alert to a set of legal issues and practical situations where ethical decisions come up in day-to-day work. One example is the Sarbanes-Oxley legislation (informally known as Sarbox), passed in the United States in response to large-scale corporate fraud, which imposes strict control requirements over corporate financial reporting — requirements that, in practice, apply the same kind of quality-assurance discipline already familiar to the quality engineer to the finance function. Another recurring topic is protecting personally identifiable information (PII) — phone numbers, addresses, ID numbers, credit card data — which needs to be protected by appropriate organizational and regulatory policies, like HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, in healthcare) and FERPA (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, in academia). In the information technology world, recurring ethical issues include misusing an employer's computing resources for personal purposes, violating privacy and confidentiality rights, copyright and trademark infringement, failing to maintain the accuracy and integrity of stored data, and failing to communicate critical information in time to those who need to make decisions.

Another sensitive area is conduct during hiring processes: engineers without proper training can unknowingly expose themselves and their employers to legal risk while conducting job interviews. The basic guidelines are simple — ask only questions directly related to the position; don't ask about age, race, national origin, marital status, or religion; keep the focus on the skills and competencies the position requires; and avoid any casual conversation unrelated to the job. Finally, the QE often needs to deal with occupational safety and health regulatory requirements, like the U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) standards, covering topics like hearing protection, hazardous-waste operations, personal protective equipment, eye and face protection, hazardous energy control (lockout/tagout), and hazard communication.

Across all these scenarios, the guiding principle remains simple to state, even if not always simple to apply: the quality professional's behavior should, at all times, be such that it brings no embarrassment to suppliers, the employer, clients, or themselves — the professional should be polite, diplomatic, respectful of everyone, and, above all, honest with themselves about having acted fairly and lawfully.
