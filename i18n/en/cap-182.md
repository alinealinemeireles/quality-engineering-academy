## Chapter 182: Theory of Constraints (TOC): the Five Focusing Steps

<h3 id="a-pergunta-de-engenharia">The engineering question</h3>
<p><em>A production line has five processes in series. Cutting waste in four of them in half, while leaving the fifth unchanged, changes the line's total output by how much? The uncomfortable answer is: usually nothing, or almost nothing — if the fifth process is the slowest of all, it — and only it — determines the pace of output for the entire line.</em></p>
<p>This observation, seemingly obvious once stated, is the starting point of a process-improvement paradigm with its own identity: the <strong>Theory of Constraints (TOC)</strong>, developed by Eliyahu M. Goldratt in the 1980s and popularized by his book <em>The Goal</em> (1984).</p>
<h3 id="toc-isn-t-lean-under-another-name-they-are-operationally-distinct-philosophies">TOC isn't Lean under another name — they are operationally distinct philosophies</h3>
<p>It's tempting to treat TOC as "just another tool" inside the Lean toolbox already covered in earlier modules. The literature is explicit about why that's a framing mistake: TOC and Lean start from different, and at times opposite, operating principles.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Focus.</strong> Lean seeks to eliminate waste <em>across the entire process</em>, treating every step as equally deserving of attention. TOC deliberately concentrates on a single step at a time — the constraint — and, for now, ignores optimizations on steps that aren't the constraint.</li>
<li><strong>Inventory.</strong> Lean seeks to minimize inventory across the whole line, as a sign and a cause of waste. TOC tolerates — and sometimes deliberately creates — an inventory <em>buffer</em> right before the constraint, to make sure it never sits idle waiting for material.</li>
<li><strong>Local vs. global success criteria.</strong> Lean, applied with discipline, also thinks about global flow — but TOC makes that priority explicit and formal: improving a step that isn't the constraint may change the entire line's output by nothing at all, and TOC treats that as wasted improvement effort, not merely as a lower-priority optimization.</li>
</ul>
<p>The process-improvement literature acknowledges this distinction by treating TOC, Lean, and Six Sigma as <strong>three complementary paradigms</strong> — not one subordinate to another — under the label <strong>TLS Continuum</strong> (Theory of Constraints + Lean + Six Sigma): each answers a different question ("where to focus," "how to eliminate waste," "how to reduce variation"), and the three together cover more than any one alone. Despite this module's track name, TOC isn't a Lean technique — it's the third paradigm in that combination, included here in the same track because, in practice, it's taught and applied by the same process-improvement teams.</p>
<h3 id="the-five-focusing-steps">The Five Focusing Steps</h3>
<p>TOC's practical method for applying this principle to a real process follows five steps, meant to repeat continuously — today's constraint may not be tomorrow's, once it's resolved:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Identify</strong> the constraint — the step that limits the output of the entire system.</li>
<li><strong>Exploit</strong> the constraint — squeeze the maximum capacity out of it with no additional investment (eliminate micro-stoppages, make sure it's never idle for lack of material, prioritize the work that goes through it).</li>
<li><strong>Subordinate</strong> everything else to the decision above — the other steps adjust to the constraint's pace, even if that means leaving them with deliberately idle capacity.</li>
<li><strong>Elevate</strong> the constraint — if, after exploiting it to the fullest, it's still not enough, invest to increase its capacity (new machine, an extra shift, automation).</li>
<li><strong>Repeat</strong> the process — once the constraint is resolved, another step becomes the new limiting factor, and the cycle starts over from step 1. TOC explicitly warns against letting organizational inertia become the new constraint after the original one is resolved.</li>
</ol>
<h3 id="drum-buffer-rope-and-toc-s-three-metrics">Drum-buffer-rope and TOC's three metrics</h3>
<p>To operationalize "subordinate everything else to the constraint," TOC uses the <strong>drum-buffer-rope</strong> metaphor: the constraint is the <strong>drum</strong> — the pace the entire line follows; a <strong>buffer</strong> (inventory buffer) protects the constraint from ever sitting idle for lack of upstream material; and the <strong>rope</strong> is the mechanism that stops upstream steps from producing faster than the constraint can process — preventing unnecessary inventory from piling up anywhere else on the line.</p>
<p>To decide where to invest improvement effort, TOC uses three simple financial metrics, deliberately more direct than traditional costing indicators:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Throughput</strong> — the rate at which the system generates money through sales (not production — a part produced but not sold isn't throughput).</li>
<li><strong>Inventory</strong> — all the money the system has invested in things it intends to sell (raw material, work in process, finished goods).</li>
<li><strong>Operating Expense</strong> — all the money the system spends to turn inventory into throughput.</li>
</ul>
<p>Any improvement decision under TOC is judged by its effect on these three metrics — and, specifically, an improvement in a step that isn't the constraint, however positive it may look locally, typically moves global throughput by nothing at all.</p>

<p><strong>Engineering reading.</strong> The example is deliberately simple, but the conclusion generalizes: any capacity improvement on a step <em>that isn't the constraint</em> doesn't move the entire line's throughput, no matter how large the percentage improvement is on that step in isolation. It's exactly this result — counterintuitive to anyone measuring success by each process's local indicators — that makes TOC a prioritization discipline, not just another waste-reduction technique.</p>
<h3 id="constraint-candidate-vs-confirmed-constraint-methodological-rigor">Constraint candidate vs. confirmed constraint: methodological rigor</h3>
<p>Identifying the step with the lowest nominal capacity (as in the example above) gives you a <strong>candidate</strong> constraint — a hypothesis, based on theoretical or design capacity data. It isn't yet a <strong>confirmed constraint</strong>: actual capacity, measured on the shop floor, can diverge from nominal capacity due to micro-stoppages, cycle-time variability, or material availability. Treating a candidate as if it were already confirmed — and redirecting improvement effort there without checking real production data — is the most common methodological error in applying TOC outside the textbook.</p>
<p>An additional signal of a confirmed constraint, beyond measured capacity, is <strong>starvation</strong> — when a downstream process repeatedly sits idle waiting for material, it's evidence that the upstream step is in fact limiting flow. The absence of starvation in downstream processes, conversely, is a sign the constraint may be misidentified.</p>

<h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3><ol>
<li><strong>Treating TOC as a technique within Lean.</strong> They are paradigms with different operating priorities (focusing on one step vs. eliminating waste across all of them) — the TLS literature treats them as complementary, not one subordinate to the other.</li>
<li><strong>Investing improvement effort in a step that isn't the constraint.</strong> As in the worked example, the entire line's throughput doesn't change — it's technically successful and strategically wasted effort.</li>
<li><strong>Confusing a constraint candidate with a confirmed constraint.</strong> Design-time nominal capacity rarely matches exactly the actual capacity measured in production — validating with data before acting avoids optimizing the wrong step.</li>
<li><strong>Removing the inventory buffer before the constraint out of a Lean reflex to "minimize inventory everywhere."</strong> That specific buffer exists deliberately to protect throughput — removing it on principle, without distinguishing its purpose, can reduce overall output.</li>
<li><strong>Forgetting step 5 (Repeat).</strong> After elevating a constraint, another step automatically becomes the new limiting factor — treating the improvement as "finished" ignores that the focusing process is, by design, continuous.</li>
