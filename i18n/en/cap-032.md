## Chapter 32: Selecting the Right Projects

### Balancing the Right Number of Projects

A fundamental part of Six Sigma's success for organizations is knowing when teams reach maximum project load. Even when organizations hire employees dedicated to process improvement, they can only sustain a certain number of improvement projects without substantially reducing those projects' positive results. Although project work, including data collection and analysis, can be handled by employees 100% dedicated to improvement projects, teams generally need to engage and pull resources from regular employees. An organization that overloads on projects puts day-to-day output at risk. In seeking to improve processes, a company that selects too many projects at once can actually negatively impact quality.

There's no formula for how many Six Sigma projects a company should run at a given time, but a few well-designed projects are more likely to have a bigger impact than many poorly designed, overlapping, or unfunded projects. Organizations should only launch projects they can:

- **Fund.** Six Sigma projects require monetary resources, which means organizations need to prioritize based on financial criteria.
- **Support with human resources.** Six Sigma projects require work from employees at every level. Companies shouldn't launch three projects at once that all heavily depend on IT resources, or attempt multiple simultaneous projects that require regular input from the Compliance Officer. Over-relying on resources for multiple projects can burn out employees, lower morale, impact quality, and get in the way of the work needed to keep the company running day to day.
- **Manage.** Project teams need leadership; Six Sigma teams are generally led by Black Belts, sometimes alongside certified Project Managers. Since Black Belts are supported by Green Belts who handle much of the data-collection and analysis work, a single Black Belt can generally manage more than one project at a time, if needed. This is especially true for experienced Black Belts who aren't responsible for any kind of day-to-day operations. Even so, organizations with few Six Sigma specialists on staff can't launch dozens of projects without overloading those resources.

### Enterprise-Level Selection Process

When companies are working to apply Six Sigma culture across the whole enterprise, executive leaders and other decision-makers should work directly with Six Sigma specialists to identify improvement opportunities and launch projects. This lets leadership align project selection with organizational objectives, ensures projects are organized in a way that's compatible with resources, and maintains a bird's-eye view of improvement efforts. Organizations can apply a five-step procedure to identify viable Six Sigma improvement projects.

**1. Data-Based Review of the Organization's Current State**

Organizations can start with a high-level view of internal and external sources of performance information. Internal information can include complaints or issues raised by employees, existing performance metrics or reports, financial reports, and quality reports. External sources include all the Voice of the Customer tools we covered in Chapter 8. When reviewing internal and external information, organizations should ask:

- What kinds of things are customers or employees complaining about?
- Where is the organization falling short of benchmarks or competitor performance?
- What needs do customers have that the organization isn't meeting?
- What needs might customers have in the near future that the organization still can't meet?
- Which processes are generating the most defects?
- Which processes are known for the highest rework?
- What are the organization's slowest or most expensive processes?
- What are some obstacles keeping the organization from reaching its objectives?

**2. Brainstorm and Describe Potential Projects**

The answers to step one's questions become a brainstorming list of potential projects. What kinds of things are customers complaining about? Perhaps surveys and feedback forms show customers complaining about long shipping times, low product quality, or rude customer service. With just one question, an organization already has a list of possible projects:

- A project to reduce how long it takes customers to receive orders
- A project to increase product quality
- A project to create better customer service

Granted, the scope is huge in these examples, so organizations would need to dig a bit deeper. Why do customers think product quality is low? In Chapter 9, we covered the 5 Whys brainstorming method, and that method is relevant here. During the brainstorming process, organizations and teams should repeatedly ask "Why?" to get a more granular view of the project possibilities.

For example, if a carpet-installation company's feedback form indicates customers aren't satisfied with the service they received, the team can ask "Why are customers dissatisfied?" Digging further into customer feedback might show they're dissatisfied because the carpet's edges start coming loose shortly after installation. Why is that happening? The short answer is that something's wrong in the installation process. The organization can add "Improve the carpet-installation process" to a list of possible projects.

Building a list of possible projects this way isn't always a matter of a single brainstorming session. As questions get raised, more information may be needed to list possible projects, but if you gather the right group of people for a few brainstorming sessions, chances are someone already has that information or knows some basic answers. Remember: the goal of this exercise is to point to possible areas for improvement, not to validate assumptions or arrive at solutions.

Once teams have a large list of possible projects, they should start creating short descriptions that will become the basis for step three. The descriptions also let teams quickly identify things that aren't really problems or wouldn't apply within an improvement-project setting. The descriptions should include answers to three questions:

- How is the issue painful for the customer, the employees, or the organization? In short, how does the issue keep someone from getting what they want or need?
- What's the goal that would be achieved with an improvement?
- Why should the organization address this issue now?

If this is starting to sound familiar, it's because the answers to these questions create something similar — though a bit less formal — to the problem statements discussed in Chapter 9. Using the carpet-installation example, the description could be:

*Customers aren't satisfied with carpet installation because the edges come loose a few weeks after installation. The poorly finished edges are creating safety and appearance problems and increasing expenses and rework for teams who have to return to sites to fix the defects. The goal is to reduce the number of times carpet edges come loose by 80%. The organization should address this issue now because it's costing $20,000 a month in errors.*

Some basic idea of what the team wants to do is provided, and leadership has a pretty real measure of why the improvement matters. Even better, the measure — $20,000 in extra costs every month — can be compared against other project opportunities.

**3. Apply Some Basic Criteria to Narrow the List**

Once a list of possible projects has been created, teams can apply a few very basic criteria to remove projects that are unsuitable, wouldn't work with the Six Sigma methodology, aren't properly scoped, or have little likely return on investment. This step generally starts during the second part of step two, while teams are writing short descriptions of possible projects.

First, teams can remove items from the list where there's no real pain point. If there's no meaningful gap between the desired state and the current state, then there's nothing to improve. For example, if a single employee complaint about software efficiency made it onto the initial list, a company wouldn't move forward with improvements if it found out no one else had that problem.

Second, teams can remove issues that have very obvious problems and/or solutions. Consider the carpet-installation problem: if the edge issue came up in the last month and someone on the team reviewing potential problems recently got an email about defects in finishing materials from a carpet supplier, the solution might be obvious. Maybe the supplier sent a notice that materials from a particular carpet batch were defective and provided instructions for a fix. In that case, action is needed from the organization, but that action isn't a Six Sigma project. We'll talk more in Chapter 33 about the DMAIC process, but if a problem is already defined and a solution provided, you don't need to spend time going through the DMAIC phases.

**4. Create Unique Business Criteria**

After removing project ideas that don't fit the Six Sigma methodology, teams should create and apply business criteria to further filter the list. Business criteria generally come in the form of expenses, monetary gains, impact on customer satisfaction, and urgency. Some questions teams can ask include:

- How will the improvement impact revenue-facing measures, like profit, orders, or income?
- What savings will the improvement create?
- How is the problem evolving? Is it becoming a bigger, more urgent issue quickly, or can the organization operate with minimal impact without making an immediate or near-future change?
- How much will the improvement cost?
- How many employees/employee-hours will the improvement require?
- What resources are needed for the improvement?

**5. Use Business Criteria to Prioritize Project Lists**

Using the business criteria, teams should prioritize projects and select projects from the top of the prioritized list for immediate work. One of the best ways to prioritize projects is to create a selection matrix with defined criteria and a numeric rating system.

For example, using the sample questions from step four, we can create the following criteria list:

- Potential savings
- Potential cost
- Potential revenue increase
- Ability to access required resources

A matrix can be built using the criteria and a list of projects. Teams can then rate each project against each criterion using a numeric scale. In the example below, we apply a scale of 1 to 10, where 1 is the most negative and 10 the most positive.

#### The Project Feasibility Model

Teams can choose to create their own criteria for a project-selection matrix, or they can use a 15-point feasibility model, as defined below. One benefit of the project feasibility model is that it provides some weighting, letting teams make some criteria more important than others. It also removes some of the subjective nature of the selection matrix defined in the previous section.

This model is based on 15 criteria, defined in the table below.

<tr>

<td>The project will likely be sponsored at a high level (see more on project sponsorship in Chapter 16). Sponsorship increases the chance teams will have access to the funds and resources needed for a successful potential project.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>2. Corporate alignment</td>
<td>The project's objectives are aligned with company objectives. Working on potential projects that aren't aligned with business objectives can reduce enterprise effectiveness.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>3. Data</td>
<td>Data is available or accessible, so the team can design project metrics. Without data access, a Six Sigma methodology can't be applied. If data is excessively time-consuming or expensive to collect, then the potential project generally isn't the best choice.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>4. Defect definition</td>
<td>There's a specific, well-defined defect or problem. Without a well-defined defect, potential projects risk scope creep.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>5. Stability</td>
<td>The potential process is stable and there's no expectation the process will be overhauled, redesigned, or changed in the near future. There's generally no reason to spend time and money improving a process that's going to change drastically soon anyway.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>6. Customer</td>
<td>The potential project's planned goal would create a substantial, positive impact on customer satisfaction or perceived quality.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>7. Benefits</td>
<td>The potential project has a strong cost-benefit ratio.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>8. Timeline</td>
<td>The timeline for a potential project is relatively short. Timelines for most Six Sigma improvement projects run around 6 months, though some run longer. Longer timelines decrease the chance an improvement fits within the DMAIC methodology.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>9. Solution</td>
<td>The potential project's purpose is to find a solution that isn't yet known or defined. As we've said, if a solution is obvious, you don't need to run a project to find it.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>10. Likely implementation</td>
<td>A solution identified and verified by the potential project is likely to be implemented. If, for whatever reason, change is very unlikely within a process, then doing the Six Sigma improvement work is a waste of resources.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>11. Investment required</td>
<td>The potential project requires a large investment of money. Generally, the greater the cash or capital investment required, the lower the likelihood a project will be selected or a solution implemented, due to cost-benefit analysis.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>12. Available Six Sigma resources</td>
<td>The Black Belts and Green Belts needed for the project are available.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>13. Inputs can be controlled</td>
<td>For a Six Sigma process-improvement project to succeed, at least some of the inputs need to be under the team's or organization's control. For example, a team can't work to improve the quality of a part supplied entirely by a vendor.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>14. Redesign</td>
<td>The process can be improved as it is and doesn't need a full redesign.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>15. Process quality is improved/maintained</td>
<td>The improvement doesn't negatively impact the quality of services or products across the value chain.</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>

Next, teams answer each question by marking a 1 in the relevant grid box; the answers correspond to no, mostly no, possibly, mostly yes, and yes. The score calculation follows these steps:

1. Divide each weight by 3; a weight of 3 equals 1, but a weight of 5 equals 5/3, or 1.7.
2. Convert each of the 1s marked in the grid into a weighted value, by multiplying it by the converted weight from step one.
3. Add up the numbers in each of the five columns (No, Mostly No, Possibly, Mostly Yes, Yes).
4. Multiply each of the summed weighted scores by the number at the top of the column (1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 respectively).
5. Add up the answers from the previous step.
6. Divide the sum from step five by the sum of the weighted totals from step three.
7. The answer from step 6 is your project's score.

In the carpet-installation example, these steps result in a final score of approximately 3.28 (59.4 divided by 18.1).

It's worth noting that the 15-point matrix described above can only be used to determine whether a project is viable within a DMAIC framework. A process might still need improvement even if it doesn't fit the DMAIC methodology; in the case of a redesign, the DMADV framework can let Six Sigma teams approach the improvement. The differences between the DMAIC and DMADV methodologies, and how to determine which method is best for a project, are covered further in Chapter 33.

### Process-Level Project Selection

The goal of a Six Sigma team isn't to define appropriate enterprise-level projects. A department or team responsible for just a few processes may be looking to make an improvement. In an organization where Six Sigma is important to the corporate culture, department leaders are probably familiar with some Six Sigma tools and may even be Green Belts or Black Belts themselves. Although these leaders have day-to-day responsibilities unrelated to Six Sigma, they can bring Six Sigma reasoning to their department.

Department leaders may want to identify potential opportunities to bring to leadership. They may also want to identify areas where they and their teams can work on improvements on their own. In some organizations, department leaders can run smaller-scale versions of projects with guidance from internal Six Sigma specialists — especially when such projects require little in the way of capital or resources.

The department team can use all the tools in this chapter to identify possible projects. Often, though, they're close enough to the situation to identify improvement possibilities without going through the brainstorming steps. If the data is already on hand, the department team can use Pareto charts to identify some areas where improvement would generate results; they can then use the selection matrix to validate those assumptions and prioritize efforts.
