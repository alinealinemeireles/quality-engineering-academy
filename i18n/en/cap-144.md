## Chapter 144: Advanced Risk Identification and Analysis Techniques (IEC 31010)
### The engineering question

> *"We do FMEA for everything. When does FMEA fall short, and what do I use instead?"*

FMEA is excellent at one thing: enumerating failure modes **of a component or a process step, one
at a time**. It fails structurally in three situations:

1. When the problem is a **combination** of failures — FMEA analyzes modes in isolation;
2. When an event's **consequences** branch depending on whether controls work or not;
3. When the risk comes from continuous-process **parameter deviations**, not component failure.

IEC 31010:2019 catalogs the techniques that fill these gaps. This chapter teaches the five a quality
engineer actually has to be able to apply: **HAZID, HAZOP, SWIFT, FTA, ETA** — and their synthesis,
the **Bow-Tie**.

---

## 144.1 The map: which technique for which question

| Question | Technique | Direction | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| What hazards exist in this system? | **HAZID** | Inductive, broad | Hazard register |
| What parameter deviations can occur, and with what effect? | **HAZOP** | Systematic, by nodes and guide words | Deviations, causes, consequences, safeguards |
| What could go wrong here? (fast version) | **SWIFT** | Checklist-structured | Prioritized scenarios |
| How can this component fail? | **FMEA/FMECA** | Inductive, bottom-up | Failure modes, RPN/criticality |
| What combinations cause this top event? | **FTA** | **Deductive**, top-down | Minimal cut sets, top-event probability |
| What consequences follow from this initiating event? | **ETA** | **Inductive**, forward | Final scenarios with probabilities |
| Integrated causes → event → consequences view | **Bow-Tie** | Synthesis (FTA + ETA) | Preventive and mitigating barriers |

**The choice rule in one sentence:** *FMEA asks "what can fail?"; FTA asks "how did we get here?";
ETA asks "where does this go?"*

---

## 144.2 HAZOP: guide words applied to process nodes

Developed in the chemical industry, it applies to any process with continuous parameters. The
method:

1. Divide the process into **nodes** (a step, a line, a piece of equipment).
2. For each node, list the relevant **parameters** (temperature, pressure, flow, time,
   concentration, speed, torque).
3. Systematically apply the **guide words** to each parameter.
4. For each combination: credible cause? consequence? existing safeguard? action needed?

| Guide word | Meaning | Example (furnace temperature) |
|---|---|---|
| NO / NONE | Total absence | Heating doesn't turn on |
| MORE | Quantitative increase | Overheating |
| LESS | Quantitative decrease | Underheating |
| AS WELL AS | Unintended addition | Contamination with another material |
| PART OF | Only part of the intention | Uneven heating across the load |
| REVERSE | Opposite of the intention | Cooling instead of heating |
| OTHER THAN | Complete substitution | Wrong recipe loaded |
| EARLY / LATE | Timing deviation | Load removed before the cycle finishes |

**HAZOP's strength** is forced exhaustiveness: the team is required to consider combinations that
would never come up in free brainstorming. **The cost** is time — a serious HAZOP consumes days of
cross-functional team effort.

---

## 144.3 FTA: deductive logic and minimal cut sets

You start from the **top event** (what must not happen) and work down through the causes, linked by
logic gates:

- **AND gate:** the output event requires **all** inputs → P = ∏Pᵢ (if independent).
- **OR gate:** **one** input is enough → P = 1 − ∏(1−Pᵢ).

**Minimal cut set:** the smallest set of basic events whose simultaneous occurrence causes the top
event. It's the most useful FTA concept, for three reasons:

1. **Order-1 cuts** (a single event is enough) are single points of failure — the absolute priority.
2. **Cut order** measures system robustness: the higher the order, the more simultaneous failures
   are needed.
3. **An event that appears in several cuts** is a **common cause** — and common causes destroy
   apparent redundancy.

> **FTA's number-one trap:** assuming independence under AND gates. Two redundant pumps fed from the
> same electrical panel are not independent. With P(failure) = 10⁻³ per pump, the AND gate gives
> 10⁻⁶ — but with a common-cause factor β = 0.10 the real probability is ≈10⁻⁴, **a hundred times
> higher**. And the error gets worse as individual barriers get more reliable: the smaller p is, the
> more the common-cause term (β·p) dominates the independent term (p²). That's why **common-cause**
> analysis is mandatory in safety FTA.

---

## 144.4 ETA and Bow-Tie

**ETA** starts from an initiating event and branches at each barrier: it works (success) or it
doesn't (failure). Each path to a leaf is a scenario, with probability equal to the product of the
branches and an associated consequence. The output is the **consequence profile** — not a single
number, but a distribution of possible outcomes.

**Bow-Tie** joins the two: the FTA on the left (causes), the top event in the center, the ETA on the
right (consequences). **Barriers** are drawn on each branch, with their type and effectiveness. It's
the most effective representation for communicating risk to a decision-maker, because it shows
simultaneously where the defenses are and where they aren't.

**The element that gives the Bow-Tie its real value:** **escalation factors** — the conditions that
make a barrier fail (missing training, deferred maintenance, workload overload) — and the barriers
that control those factors. That's where the real failures live, and it's what a risk matrix never
shows.

---

## 144.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Using FMEA for everything | Failure combinations never analyzed | FTA for events with combined causes |
| FTA with assumed independence | Top-event probability underestimated by orders of magnitude | Common-cause analysis (beta factor) |
| Ignoring order-1 cuts | Single points of failure left untreated | Enumerate and prioritize minimal cuts |
| HAZOP without a cross-functional team | Credible deviations go unidentified | Process, maintenance, quality, operations |
| Decorative Bow-Tie, no barrier effectiveness | False sense of protection | Measure effectiveness (Ch. 147) |
| Choosing the technique out of habit | Effort in the wrong place | Use the map in section 144.1 |
| Not recording escalation factors | The real causes of failure stay invisible | Include them explicitly |

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Choosing the technique.**
*Choose between FMEA, FTA, HAZOP, Bow-Tie, and ETA for five problems, and justify the choice.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

| Problem | Technique | Why |
|---|---|---|
| New welding process: what failure modes and what controls? | **PFMEA** | Bottom-up, step-by-step analysis, with a direct link to the control plan |
| "Hazardous product reaches the customer" — quantify the probability | **FTA** | Deductive, represents logical combinations, allows calculation and minimal cuts |
| New furnace with atmosphere control: identify parameter deviations | **HAZOP** | Continuous-parameter process; guide words force exhaustiveness |
| Communicate recall risk to leadership and show where the defenses are | **Bow-Tie** | Integrated view of causes/consequences/barriers; the most readable for decision-makers |
| A safety sensor failed: what scenarios follow, and with what probability? | **ETA** | Inductive from the initiating event; produces the consequence profile |

**The criterion that unifies all five choices:** the direction of the question.

- **Bottom-up / inductive** ("what can fail here?") → FMEA, HAZOP
- **Top-down / deductive** ("how did we get to this event?") → FTA
- **Forward from an event** ("where does this go?") → ETA
- **Synthesis for communication** → Bow-Tie

**A typical mistake to avoid:** using FMEA to answer the second question. FMEA lists failure modes
but doesn't represent combinatorial logic — it can't say "this only happens if A **and** B fail
simultaneously," which is exactly the information that matters for a safety event.

**E2 — Fault tree.**
*Build a fault tree for "hazardous product reaches the customer" and identify the minimal cut sets.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The tree is built and solved in the code cell. Its structure:

```
                    TOP: NC part reaches the customer
                                 OR
                ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
          production path                    rework path
                AND                                AND
     ┌──────┬───┴────┬──────┐                 ┌────┴────┐
     A    OR(B,D)    C      E                 F         E
```

**The three results the analysis produces that the diagram alone doesn't give:**

1. **There are no order-1 cuts.** No isolated event triggers the top event — there is defense in
   depth. If there were, it would be the absolute treatment priority.

2. **The dominant cut is {F, E} — order 2.** The unre-inspected reworked part has only the shipping
   audit after it. It's the shortest path to the top, which is why it dominates the probability
   despite F having a low probability (0.015).

3. **Event E appears in every cut.** It's the barrier common to all paths: improving it reduces the
   probability of every cut simultaneously. It's the point of highest leverage — and, symmetrically,
   the biggest vulnerability if it degrades.

**The action that follows:** strengthening the rework process (F) and the shipping audit (E) pays
off more than improving SPC (B), despite B having a much higher failure probability (0.25). Intuition
would say the opposite — which is exactly why the analysis is done.

</details>

**E3 — Event tree.**
*Build an event tree starting from the failure of a safety sensor.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The code cell solves the structure with four sequential barriers. The method:

1. **Initiating event:** nonconforming lot produced (P = estimated frequency).
2. **Barriers in temporal sequence**, each with its success probability.
3. **Each path** to a leaf = product of the branch probabilities.
4. **Each leaf** has a consequence with an associated cost.
5. **Mandatory check:** path probabilities sum to 1.0.

**What ETA gives that FTA doesn't:** the **complete consequence profile**. FTA answers "what's the
probability of the top event"; ETA answers "given the event, what's the distribution of outcomes."
They're complementary questions, and a serious analysis needs both — which is exactly what the
Bow-Tie joins.

**The most useful calculation, and the one in the cell:** the **value of each barrier**, measured by
the increase in expected loss if it's removed.

The result overturns two wrong intuitions at once. It's **not** the least-effective barrier that
pays off most to strengthen — nor is it automatically the last one before the customer. In the
example, the most valuable is the **final inspection**: effective (0.90) **and** positioned right
before the consequence jump (€15k → €40k → €250k). The last barrier (customer detection) is the
**least** valuable, because the probability of a case reaching it is already very low.

A barrier's value is always the product of two factors: **how much risk reaches it** and **how much
it removes**. This turns "where to strengthen the defenses" from an opinion discussion into a
calculation in euros.

</details>

**E4 — Bow-Tie and escalation factors.**
*Turn the same risk into a Bow-Tie representation and identify preventive and mitigating barriers.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

```

  ─────────────────────      ────────────────       ─────────────────────────
  Process drift        ──╮                       ╭── Contained in process
  NC material          ──┤                       ├── Contained before shipping
  Setup error          ──┼──► NC lot produced ──┼── Contained at shipping
  Worn tool            ──┤                       ├── Returned by customer
  Inadequate MSA        ──╯                       ╰── Reaches the market

   · Tool maintenance plan                          · Recall procedure
   · Validated MSA                                  · Customer communication
```

**The escalation factors — and this is where the Bow-Tie's real value lies:**

| Barrier | Escalation factor (what makes it fail) | Barrier for the escalation factor |
|---|---|---|
| SPC | Operator not trained on detection rules | Training with effectiveness verification |
| SPC | Frequent alarms → normalization of deviance | Review limits; appropriate chart (Ch. 68) |
| First-piece check | Production pressure; the step gets skipped | Interlock; layered audit |
| Final inspection | Overload at production peak | Resource sizing |
| Final inspection | Visual fatigue at end of shift | Task rotation; automation |
| Traceability | Incomplete manual logging | Automatic capture |

**Why the escalation factors are the most important part:** barriers rarely fail for technical
reasons. They fail because someone wasn't trained, because maintenance was deferred, because
delivery pressure led to skipping a step. A Bow-Tie that lists barriers without their escalation
factors documents the system's intent — not its real behavior.

**The link to auditing:** escalation factors are exactly what a process audit should look for. "Does
the barrier exist?" is the easy question. "Under what conditions does it stop working, and did those
conditions occur last quarter?" is the question that finds real risk.
