## Chapter 166: MTBF, MTTR, and Basic Reliability Indicators

<h3 id="the-engineering-question">The engineering question</h3>
<p><em>Two machines on the line go down for the same total number of hours per month. The first one fails once, and the repair takes eight hours. The second fails sixteen times, and each repair takes half an hour. The two have exactly the same availability. Is it reasonable to give them the same maintenance priority?</em></p>
<p>The answer is no — and the reason is hidden behind a single aggregate metric (availability) that conceals two completely different root causes. Decomposing downtime into MTBF (how often it fails) and MTTR (how long it takes to repair) is the first step in any reliability engineering, and it's the missing rung between the OEE of <a href="#/aula/cap-025">Chapter 25</a> and the advanced statistical failure modeling in this module.</p>
<h3 id="mtbf-and-mttr-what-each-one-measures">MTBF and MTTR: what each one measures, and what neither one tells you</h3>
<p>For a <strong>repairable</strong> piece of equipment — most production equipment — two simple averages are defined over an observation period:</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\text{MTBF} = \frac{\text{Total time in operation}}{\text{Number of failures}}"></div>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\text{MTTR} = \frac{\text{Total repair time}}{\text{Number of repairs}}"></div>
<p><strong>MTBF</strong> (<em>Mean Time Between Failures</em>) measures <strong>frequency</strong> of failure: how long, on average, the equipment stays operational between one failure and the next. <strong>MTTR</strong> (<em>Mean Time To Repair</em>) measures <strong>recovery speed</strong>: how long, on average, it takes to get back up after failing. These are two different engineering problems with two different countermeasures — raising MTBF is a design-reliability and autonomous-maintenance problem (see <a href="#/aula/cap-025">Chapter 25</a>); lowering MTTR is a parts-logistics, diagnostics, and repair-procedure problem.</p>
<p>One naming distinction matters: for <strong>non-repairable</strong> equipment (a component that gets replaced rather than fixed), the correct metric is called <strong>MTTF</strong> (<em>Mean Time To Failure</em>), not MTBF — the distinction exists because "between failures" doesn't make sense for something that only fails once, at the end of its useful life.</p><h3 id="inherent-availability">Inherent availability: where the two meet</h3>
<p>MTBF and MTTR can be combined into **inherent availability** under clearly defined time boundaries and assumptions. This does not mean it is the same availability definition used by OEE (<a href="#/aula/cap-025">Chapter 25</a>): the metrics are related, but their denominators and purposes differ.</p>
<div class="math-block" data-math="\text{Inherent availability} = \frac{\text{MTBF}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}}"></div>
<p>This formula has a direct practical implication: <strong>there are two independent paths to higher availability</strong> — failing less often (higher MTBF) or repairing faster (lower MTTR) — and they don't always cost the same. Doubling a machine's MTBF might require investment in predictive maintenance and design review; cutting MTTR in half might be as simple as pre-positioning a spare part on the shop floor. The question "where should we invest first?" has no answer without separating the two metrics — which is exactly what aggregate availability hides.</p><h3 id="mtbf-mttr-criticality-matrix">MTBF × MTTR criticality matrix</h3>
<p>Cross-tabulating the two metrics by piece of equipment, instead of looking at each one in isolation, is what turns a downtime log into a maintenance-prioritization plan. Split the fleet into four quadrants, using thresholds set by the plant (for example, the median across the equipment fleet):</p>
<table>
<thead><tr><th></th><th>Low MTTR (repairs fast)</th><th>High MTTR (repairs slowly)</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>High MTBF (fails rarely)</strong></td><td>Healthy — monitor</td><td>Latent risk: rare, but painful when it happens. Prioritize spare-parts stock and repair procedure</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Low MTBF (fails often)</strong></td><td>Operational nuisance, but recoverable — prioritize root cause of the failure</td><td><strong>Critical</strong>: fails often and takes a long time to recover. Top maintenance priority, and likely a redesign candidate</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>The "high MTBF, high MTTR" quadrant is the one most often mismanaged: because the equipment fails rarely, it doesn't show up at the top of a cumulative-downtime Pareto — but when it fails, the outage is long and expensive, precisely because the rarity of the failure means the team lacks recent repair practice and, often, the spare part in stock. It's the "black swan" quadrant of maintenance.</p><h3 id="what-mtbf-doesnt-tell-you">What MTBF doesn't tell you: the failure rate isn't always constant</h3>
<p>MTBF is a <strong>descriptive average</strong>; by itself, it does not assume a constant failure rate or an exponential distribution. A <strong>constant hazard</strong> is a property of the exponential model and lets us relate failure rate to the reciprocal of MTBF under that model. Real equipment can show decreasing hazard during infant mortality, roughly stable hazard during useful life, or increasing hazard during wear-out — which is why MTBF alone does not describe how risk changes over time.</p>
<p>Two pieces of equipment can have <strong>the same MTBF</strong> and completely different risk profiles: one with a constant failure rate (safe to extrapolate) and another in accelerating wear-out (dangerous to extrapolate — the next failure is closer than the average suggests). MTBF alone doesn't distinguish the two cases. That requires modeling the <strong>shape</strong> of the failure distribution — Weibull's β parameter, covered in <a href="#/aula/cap-098">Chapter 98</a> and taken further with censoring and maximum-likelihood estimation in <a href="#/aula/cap-139">Chapter 139</a>. <h3 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Comparing MTBF across equipment with different operating regimes.</strong> A machine running 3 shifts and another running 1 shift aren't comparable on MTBF without normalizing by actual time in operation.</li>
<li><strong>Confusing MTBF with MTTF.</strong> MTBF applies to repairable items (the equipment returns to service after failure); MTTF applies to non-repairable items, which get replaced. Using the wrong formula isn't just a naming slip — it implies a different statistical model.</li>
<li><strong>Treating aggregate availability as sufficient for prioritizing maintenance.</strong> As the worked example shows, two pieces of equipment with similar availability can have opposite risk profiles.</li>
<li><strong>Extrapolating MTBF to predict the next failure without checking the shape of the distribution.</strong> MTBF assumes a roughly constant failure rate; on equipment in the wear-out phase, the next failure is closer than the historical average suggests — see <a href="#/aula/cap-098">Chapter 98</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Calculating MTBF/MTTR from a small sample of failures.</strong> With few observed failures, the average is unstable — treat the number with the same caution you'd give any small-sample estimate.</li>
</ol>
