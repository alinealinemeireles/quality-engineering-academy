## Chapter 153: Choosing the Right Risk Framework — Sector Map ✦

### The engineering question

> *"I know how to do FMEA, SPC, and capability. I'm going to work in medical devices /
> pharmaceuticals / aerospace / food. What changes?"*

Quality engineering's technical core is **cross-cutting**: variation is variation, measurement is
measurement, risk is risk. What changes by sector is:

1. **What the consequence of failure is** — and therefore the admissible risk appetite;
2. **Which normative framework is mandatory** — and non-negotiable;
3. **What evidence the regulator or customer requires** — and in what form;
4. **Which risk analysis tool is expected** — and sometimes imposed.

This chapter is the map. **It doesn't replace sector-specific competence**, which requires dedicated
training — but it avoids the most expensive mistake: applying one sector's framework to another.

---

## 153.1 The map

| Sector | Typical mandatory framework | Expected risk tool | Dominant consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Automotive** | IATF 16949, AIAG-VDA FMEA, APQP/PPAP, MSA, SPC | PFMEA/DFMEA with **AP**; control plan | Recall, contractual penalties, loss of nomination |
| **Medical devices** | ISO 13485, **ISO 14971**, market regulation (MDR/QMSR), IEC 62304 (software) | **ISO 14971** — risk/benefit, not RPN | Harm to the patient; suspension of marketing |
| **Pharmaceutical** | GMP, **ICH Q9** (quality risk management), Q8, Q10; data integrity (ALCOA+) | FMEA, HACCP, **HAZOP**; design space | Harm to the patient; warning letter; suspension |
| **Aerospace / defense** | AS9100 / AS9145 (aerospace APQP), authority requirements | FMEA, FTA, system safety (ARP4761) | Loss of life; fleet grounding |
| **Food** | HACCP (Codex), ISO 22000, GFSI scheme (FSSC/BRCGS/IFS), health legislation | **HACCP**, hazard analysis, CCP validation | Harm to the consumer; recall; closure |
| **Rail** | IRIS/ISO/TS 22163, **EN 50126 (RAMS)** | FTA, FMECA, safety analysis (SIL) | Accident; grounding |
| **Energy / process** | Sector standards; **IEC 61511** (functional safety) | **HAZOP**, LOPA, SIL | Major accident; environmental |
| **Software / data** | ISO/IEC 27001, GDPR/LGPD, **ISO/IEC 42001** (AI) | Security risk analysis; **ISO/IEC 23894** for AI | Data breach; sanction; disruption |
| **General / industrial** | ISO 9001 | Risk-based thinking; FMEA | Cost, customer, reputation |

---

## 153.2 The four differences that confuse sector-switchers most

### 1. Medical devices: risk/benefit, not RPN

**ISO 14971** requires assessing **residual risk against clinical benefit**. A risk can be
acceptable if the benefit justifies it — something with no equivalent in automotive, where there's
no "benefit" that justifies a brake that fails.

It also explicitly requires considering the **entire life cycle**, including **reasonably
foreseeable misuse** — not just use as instructed. That's why UFMEA (use FMEA) carries far more
weight in this sector.

**The mistake made by people coming from automotive:** applying RPN and numeric action thresholds.
ISO 14971 doesn't use them, and the notified body will question them.

### 2. Pharmaceuticals: the risk is the patient's, and data integrity is a requirement

**ICH Q9** structures quality risk management around two principles: the evaluation must be based on
**scientific knowledge** and ultimately connect to **patient protection**; and the level of effort
must be **proportional to the level of risk**.

What surprises people coming from manufacturing is the centrality of **data integrity** (ALCOA+:
attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, accurate, and also complete, consistent, enduring,
available). A corrected record with no audit trail isn't an administrative error — it's a critical
inspection finding.

**The mistake made by people coming from automotive:** underestimating computerized system
validation and qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ). In pharma, the validation process is as regulated as the
product.

### 3. Aerospace and rail: system safety, not component safety

The analysis centers on **function** and its failure at the system level, using deductive methods
(FTA) and quantitative failure-rate requirements per function. Concepts like **DAL** (Design
Assurance Level) and **SIL** (Safety Integrity Level) assign development rigor levels proportional
to the consequence.

**The mistake made by people coming from automotive:** thinking in terms of component FMEA. It's
necessary, but not sufficient — the question is "what combinations of failures lead to the loss of
this function?", and that's FTA.

### 4. Food: control is preventive because verification arrives too late

Covered in Chapter 151. The structural difference is that verification is destructive, slow, and
frequently inconclusive — which makes process control the **only** real barrier.

## 153.3 What's common to every sector

Despite the differences, the **technical core transfers entirely**:

- Variation, capability, and SPC (Parts VII and VIII);
- Measurement systems and MSA (Part VI);
- Sampling and statistical risk (Chapters 141-142);
- Statistical reasoning and uncertainty quantification (Chapters 134-140);
- Risk architecture, barriers, and effectiveness (Chapters 143-150);
- Continuous improvement and structured problem-solving.

**The competence that really sets a senior engineer apart** isn't knowing a sector's tables — it's
knowing how to **translate** the problem into the framework that sector recognizes, and knowing how
to justify why the chosen method was chosen. It's exactly Chapter 150's evaluation criterion.

---

## 153.4 How to approach a new sector — the checklist

1. **What's the maximum consequence of failure?** Sets the appetite and the required rigor.
2. **Who's the regulator or authority, and what does it require as evidence?** The form of the
   evidence matters as much as the substance.
3. **Which standards are mandatory, and what's the current edition?** Check the official source —
   editions change, and citing a superseded edition damages credibility.
4. **Which risk analysis tool is expected?** Use the expected one, and justify any addition.
5. **What data and record requirements exist?** Retention, integrity, traceability, signature.
6. **What process qualification is required?** PPAP, IQ/OQ/PQ, process validation, first article.
7. **What's the change process?** In regulated sectors, a change may require notification or prior
   approval — and implementing it without that is a serious nonconformance.
8. **What formal competence is required** of whoever executes and whoever approves?

## 153.5 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Applying one sector's framework to another | Evidence rejected; documentation rework | Use the map; confirm at the source |
| Using RPN in medical devices | Questioned by the notified body | ISO 14971: risk/benefit, life cycle |
| Component FMEA for system safety | Failure combinations not analyzed | FTA and system safety analysis |
| Underestimating data integrity in pharma | Critical inspection finding | ALCOA+, audit trail, system validation |
| Citing a superseded standard edition | Loss of technical credibility | Verify the current edition at the official source |
| Implementing a change with no notification in a regulated sector | Serious nonconformance | Map the change process beforehand |
| Assuming certification is enough | Certification ≠ legal compliance | Identify jurisdiction, product, market, and customer |

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Switching sectors.**
*An engineer with 10 years in automotive moves to medical devices. What three habits does he have
to unlearn?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**1. RPN and numeric action thresholds.** ISO 14971 doesn't use RPN and doesn't define thresholds
like "RPN > 100 requires action." It requires assessing **residual risk against clinical benefit**,
documented in the risk management file. Presenting an RPN table to a notified body immediately
raises a question about the method's compliance.

**2. Analyzing only use as instructed.** ISO 14971 requires considering **reasonably foreseeable
misuse** throughout the entire life cycle. In automotive, the user is implicitly trained and the
product is used as intended; in a medical device, the user might be a patient at home, tired, with
poor eyesight, using the device in a way no one anticipated. **UFMEA** stops being an add-on and
becomes central.

**3. Treating post-market as warranty.** In automotive, field data is mostly a warranty cost. In
medical devices, **post-market surveillance** is a regulatory obligation with incident-notification
deadlines, and it mandatorily feeds back into the risk management file's review. It isn't a
commercial process — it's a regulatory process with legal consequences.

**And what transfers unchanged:** SPC, capability, MSA, DOE, statistical reasoning, structured
problem-solving, risk architecture. It's most of the technical competence — and that's why the
transition is viable.

</details>

**E2 — Multiple frameworks.**
*A company supplies both automotive and medical devices. How does it structure the system?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**An integrated system with a common core and sector layers.** The high-level structure (Annex SL)
makes this viable because clauses 4 through 10 are common.

**Common core** (once, for the whole organization):
- Context, leadership, policy, objectives;
- Competence, communication, documented information;
- Process control, measurement, MSA, SPC;
- Internal audit, management review, improvement;
- Risk register and risk management architecture.

**Automotive layer:**
- IATF-specific requirements; APQP/PPAP; AIAG-VDA FMEA with AP; control plan;
- Customer-specific requirements (CSR), which are mandatory and vary by automaker.

**Medical devices layer:**
- ISO 13485; ISO 14971 risk management file; design validation;
- Post-market surveillance and incident notification;
- Destination market regulation requirements.

**The three points where integration fails in practice:**

1. **Records and retention.** Retention requirements differ (medical devices require periods tied to
   the device's service life). The rule of thumb is to apply **the stricter one** to the common core,
   and the specific one where they diverge.
2. **Change control.** In medical devices, some changes require notification or prior approval. The
   common process needs a **decision point** that routes to the regulatory path when applicable —
   and that point has to be foolproof against being forgotten.
3. **Training and competence.** An internal auditor competent in IATF isn't automatically competent
   in ISO 13485. The competence matrix has to reflect both layers.

**The mistake to avoid:** maintaining two parallel systems. It duplicates effort, creates divergence
between procedures that should be the same, and produces cross-cutting nonconformances at the next
audit.

</details>

**E3 — Verifying editions.**
*An internal document cites "ISO 2859-1:2016" and "ISO 19011:2018." What do you do?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**"ISO 2859-1:2016" doesn't exist.** The previous edition was **ISO 2859-1:1999** (with Amd 1:2011
and Cor 1:2001), replaced by **ISO 2859-1:2026**, published January 22, 2026, and identified by ISO
as the **3rd edition**. Always confirm the edition number and date on the standard's official record,
not from memory. A citation of "2016" indicates someone wrote from memory or copied from an
unverified source — which raises the question of what else in the document wasn't verified.

**"ISO 19011:2018" did exist**, but was replaced by **ISO 19011:2026** (4th edition, published May
2026). The citation is outdated, not wrong.

**The actions, in order:**

1. **Verify at the official source** (iso.org or the national standardization body) the current
   edition of both. Never trust blogs or old internal documents.
2. **Correct the citations** and log the change in document control.
3. **Assess the technical impact**, which is distinct from correcting the citation: ISO 2859-1:2026
   introduces skip-lot procedures; ISO 19011:2026 brings changes to the audit approach. The procedure
   may need more than a reference correction.
4. **Investigate the systemic cause.** A citation of a nonexistent edition suggests there's no
   **normative surveillance** process. The questions: who checks whether cited standards are current?
   how often? how did the organization find out about ISO 19011:2026?
5. **Implement the missing control:** a list of applicable standards with edition and verification
   date, and a named responsible party. It's the same "review date" field as the obligations register
   in Chapter 152.

**Why this matters more than it seems:** in an audit or a dispute, citing a nonexistent or
superseded standard undermines the credibility of the entire document — including the parts that are
correct.

</details>

**E4 — Choosing the method.**
*The same product — a valve — is sold to automotive, chemical process, and medical devices. What
risk analysis do you do in each case?*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**The product is the same. The analysis isn't** — because the consequence of failure and the
expected framework are different.

| | Automotive | Chemical process | Medical device |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Main method** | DFMEA + PFMEA (AIAG-VDA, with AP) | System HAZOP + LOPA | ISO 14971: risk/benefit |
| **Complementary method** | FTA if it's a safety characteristic | FTA; SIL assignment (IEC 61511) | UFMEA; FTA |
| **Focus** | Component and process failure mode | System **parameter deviation** | Harm to the **patient**, including misuse |
| **Evidence** | PPAP, control plan, capability | HAZOP report, SIL verification, proof test | Risk management file, validation, PMS |
| **Acceptance criterion** | AP + customer requirements | SIL required by LOPA | Acceptable residual risk against benefit |

**What's common to all three, and is most of the technical work:**
- characterization of dimensional and sealing variation;
- capability of the critical characteristics;
- MSA of the measurement systems;
- reliability testing and life analysis (Chapter 139);
- process control and control plan.

**The answer to the question, in one sentence:** you do **the same engineering** and **three
different risk analyses**, because what changes isn't the valve — it's the consequence of its
failure and the party you have to demonstrate to that it won't fail.

**The practical trap:** the temptation to do one analysis and reformat it three times. It doesn't
work, because the questions are structurally different: PFMEA asks "how can this process fail?",
HAZOP asks "what parameter deviations can occur in this system?", and ISO 14971 asks "what harm can
this cause the patient, and does the benefit justify it?" Reformatting one answer doesn't answer the
other two.

</details>
