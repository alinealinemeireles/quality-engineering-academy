## Chapter 80: Risk Fundamentals and Types of Risk Management

### 2026 update — what ISO 9001 actually changes about risk

ISO 9001:2026 is in the publication pipeline, and the official page indicates an expected publication date of September 2026; so, in this manual, any detail based on the FDIS is flagged as a **confirmed/likely implementation direction**, not as an already-published requirement.

The change relevant to the engineer is a stronger separation between **determining risks and opportunities**, risk analysis/assessment, planning actions, integrating actions into processes, and evaluating effectiveness. The approach also reinforces context, change, technology, and climate factors.

> **It isn't correct to say ISO 9001:2026 will require every organization to adopt ISO 31000 or a specific quantitative method.** The public guidance itself indicates the organization may choose a more comprehensive methodology. This manual's value is teaching the engineer to select and justify methods proportional to the risk, not turning one tool into a universal requirement.

### What Risk Is

Before diving into the specific risk analysis tools — FMEA, FMECA, risk matrices, and others that will be covered in the coming chapters — it's important to establish a shared vocabulary, because the word "risk" is used almost intuitively in everyday life, but has a precise technical meaning within quality engineering. Risk can be defined as the effect of uncertainty on objectives: it's the combination of the probability an undesired event will occur with the severity (or magnitude) of that event's consequences, should it actually happen. This definition carries an important implication: risk isn't just "the chance something goes wrong," it's the chance multiplied by the size of the damage. An extremely rare but catastrophic event can represent a risk just as significant as a common, low-impact one — and it's precisely this balance between frequency and severity that every risk management tool tries to capture and quantify.

Risk management, in turn, is the disciplined, ongoing process by which an organization identifies, assesses, prioritizes, and treats the risks that can affect its products, processes, projects, or operations. Notice the word "ongoing": risk management isn't a one-off event, carried out once at a project's start and then filed away. It's cyclical, and generally involves three core phases that feed back into each other: (1) identifying the risks, (2) assessing (analyzing and prioritizing) those risks, and (3) mitigation/control, with ongoing monitoring of both the risks already treated and the ones still being watched (the so-called "watch list"). As risks evolve — because the process changes, because new field data comes in, because technology advances — the cycle restarts, with existing risks reassessed and new risks identified.

### Core terminology: severity, occurrence, detection, and risk-based thinking

Three terms come up repeatedly in practically every risk assessment tool covered in this manual, and they're worth defining here, generically, before seeing them applied specifically to FMEA in the coming chapters:

**Severity** is a measure of how serious the consequence of a failure mode or risk event is, should it occur — that is, how serious the effect is on the customer, the operator, the system, or the environment. **Occurrence** is a measure of how often, or how likely, a failure's cause, or the risk event itself, is to happen over the product's or process's lifetime. **Detection** is a measure of how well existing controls — whether design, process, or inspection controls — can identify the cause or failure mode before it produces an unwanted effect on the customer. These three concepts form the backbone of quantitative tools like the Risk Priority Number (RPN), which will be detailed in the next chapter.

**Risk-based thinking**, meanwhile, is a broader concept, one that became explicit starting with ISO 9001's 2015 revision: the idea that the whole organization — not just the product engineering area — should build risk (and opportunity) consideration into its everyday decisions, replacing the old, isolated concept of "preventive action" with a permanent mindset of anticipating problems. In practice, this means design, purchasing, production-process, and even supplier-relationship decisions should be made while explicitly considering "what could go wrong here, with what probability, and with what consequence" — instead of reacting only after the failure has already happened.

It's also worth highlighting the notion of **risk attitude**: different individuals, teams, and organizations have distinct mental dispositions toward uncertainty. People speak of risk-averse, risk-tolerant, risk-neutral, and risk-seeking profiles. These classifications matter because they help explain why two organizations, faced with the same objective probability and severity data, can arrive at completely different risk-treatment decisions — one might accept a risk the other judges unacceptable. Historically, risk management teams tend to adopt a defensive/reactive posture (avoiding losses), but an offensive/proactive approach is increasingly recognized, one that also treats risk events as potential improvement or competitive-advantage opportunities, not just threats to be neutralized.

### Types of Risk Management: Enterprise, Operational, and Product

A useful, didactic way to organize the universe of risk management within an organization is splitting it into three broad categories, based on the scope and organizational level at which the risk shows up: enterprise risk management, operational risk management, and product risk management. This categorization isn't a rigid boundary — many risks cut across more than one category — but it helps the quality engineer quickly place "what kind of risk are we talking about" and, consequently, "who should be in the room discussing this risk."

**Enterprise risk management (ERM)** handles risks that affect the organization as a whole, at a strategic, corporate level. Within this category are: **strategic** risk (long-term decisions about markets, product portfolio, mergers and acquisitions, which can jeopardize the business's viability); **software** risk (information-system failures, cybersecurity, corporate data integrity); **business** risk proper (market fluctuations, competition, dependence on key customers); **regulatory** risk (changes to legislation, industry standards, or government agency requirements that can render a product or process nonconforming overnight); **medical** risk (especially relevant at organizations that develop healthcare products, where a product failure can have direct consequences for patient life and safety, with a strong regulatory interface, for example with the U.S. FDA); and **audit** risk (the possibility that the organization's own internal control and compliance systems fail to detect significant nonconformances).

**Operational risk management** deals with risks tied to the organization's day-to-day operational execution — more tactical than strategic, but still at a level that transcends a single product. It includes **supplier** risk (a key supplier failing to deliver, going bankrupt, or supplying out-of-specification material); **supply chain** risk (logistics disruptions, single-source dependence, geopolitical risks affecting material flow); **safety** risk (worker occupational safety, facility safety); **project** risk (project risk management — delays, budget overruns, scope changes on specific initiatives, one of the most mature, widely applied fields of the risk management discipline); **manufacturing** risk (process variation, insufficient plant capacity, equipment breakdowns); **operations** risk in a broader sense (internal logistics, maintenance, resource availability); and **quality system** risk (the possibility that the organization's own quality management system — audited and certified against standards like ISO 9001, covered in another unit of this manual — isn't effective enough at preventing nonconformances).

Finally, **product risk management** focuses on the risks inherent to a specific product's life cycle, from the first conceptual sketch to the customer's final use. It splits, quite naturally, into **design** risk (failures resulting from design decisions — the target protected by the DFMEA, which we'll cover in detail further on), **process** risk (failures resulting from how the product is manufactured or the service is delivered — the target of the PFMEA), **use** risk (failures resulting from how the customer or end operator actually uses the product, including foreseeable misuse — the target of the UFMEA), and **safety** risk (the possibility that a product failure causes physical harm to the user, the operator, or third parties, generally the highest-severity category within any product risk analysis). It's precisely this triad — design, process, and use — that structures the different types of FMEA detailed in the following chapters, and it's worth remembering that a product's reliability over its lifetime, a topic covered in another unit of this manual (bathtub curve, MTTF, MTBF, Weibull distribution), is exactly one of the quantities these product risk analyses seek to protect and optimize.

With this vocabulary and mental map established, the next chapters dig deeper into the most widely used tool for operationalizing product and process risk management — FMEA — before returning, in this sequence's final chapter, to the rest of the risk planning, assessment, and treatment toolkit at a broader organizational level.
