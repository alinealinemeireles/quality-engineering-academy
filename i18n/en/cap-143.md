## Chapter 143: Risk Engineering Architecture — Context, Taxonomy, Appetite, and Criteria
### The engineering question

> *"We have a 5×5 matrix and a risk register with 140 rows, all 'medium.' How does this help anyone
> decide anything?"*

It doesn't — and that's this chapter's starting point. Risk management in 2026 quality engineering
has stopped being a matrix-filling exercise. It's a **decision architecture**: it defines what the
organization considers risk, who's accountable for each one, how much it's willing to bear, and
what signals trigger action.

**Normative references.** ISO 31000:2018 provides principles, framework, and process. IEC
31010:2019 provides the assessment techniques (Chapter 144). ISO 31073:2022 provides the
vocabulary. None of them is certifiable or required for ISO 9001 — they're professional-competence
references, and that's how they should be taught.

---

## 143.1 Risk: the definition that changes practice

**Risk = the effect of uncertainty on objectives** (ISO 31000).

Three practical consequences the definition imposes:

1. **With no stated objective, there's no identifiable risk.** "Risk of machine breakdown" isn't a
   risk — it's an event. The risk is "failing to meet the contracted delivery deadline *due to*
   machine breakdown." Risk registers full of events with no objective are the most common
   pathology.
2. **The effect can be positive.** ISO 31000 is neutral about the sign. In quality practice, the
   downside is treated as risk and the upside as opportunity, in separate processes — which is also
   how ISO 9001:2026 treats them (6.1.2 and 6.1.3).
3. **It's about uncertainty, not about what already happened.** A detected nonconformance isn't a
   risk: it's a fact, one that requires correction and corrective action.

### The chain that has to be explicit

```
SOURCE  →  CAUSE  →  EVENT  →  CONSEQUENCE  →  IMPACT ON OBJECTIVE
                        │
              PREVENTIVE BARRIERS (before)   MITIGATING BARRIERS (after)
```

Confusing these five elements is the mistake that makes most risk registers useless. Example:

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Source | Single-source supplier of a critical component |
| Cause | Fire at the supplier's plant |
| **Event** | Component supply disruption |
| Consequence | Assembly line stopped for 3 weeks |
| Impact on objective | €2.4M margin loss; delivery failure to 3 customers |

Treatment differs at each level: qualifying a second supplier acts on the **source**; requiring a
continuity plan from the supplier acts on the **cause**; keeping safety stock acts on the
**consequence**.

## 143.2 Taxonomy and risk universe

A **taxonomy** is the category structure the organization uses to organize its risks. It serves
three functions: ensuring coverage (not leaving categories unidentified), enabling aggregation
(summing comparable risks), and assigning ownership.

Reference taxonomy for an industrial operation:

| Category | Typical sub-categories |
|---|---|
| **Product quality** | Conformance, product safety, recall, warranty |
| **Process and operation** | Capability, breakdown, capacity, measurement |
| **Supply chain** | Disruption, supplier quality, dependency, logistics |
| **People** | Competence, availability, health and safety |
| **Data and digital** | Integrity, availability, cybersecurity, privacy |
| **AI and models** | Model risk, drift, bias, explainability (Ch. 149) |
| **Compliance and legal** | Regulatory, contractual, environmental, ethics |
| **Continuity** | Disruption, infrastructure, climate (Ch. 148) |
| **Reputation** | Customer, market, crisis communication |

The **risk universe** is the complete set of identifiable risks within the taxonomy. It's what tells
apart "we identified the risks that occurred to us" from "we systematically walked the universe."

## 143.3 Appetite, tolerance, and capacity — three concepts, three decisions

| Concept | Definition | Who defines it | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Capacity** | The maximum the organization **can** absorb without compromising viability | Determined by balance sheet, insurance, liquidity | **Fact** |
| **Appetite** | The level of risk the organization **wants** to take on in pursuit of its objectives | Leadership | **Strategic choice** |
| **Tolerance** | The acceptable deviation around the appetite, by category | Leadership / risk owners | **Operational** |

The required relationship is **capacity > tolerance > appetite**. If the stated appetite exceeds
capacity, the statement is fiction.

**The rule that makes appetite useful:** an appetite has to be **actionable** — there has to be an
observation capable of violating it. "We have low appetite for quality risk" isn't actionable. "No
lot with a safety characteristic out of specification may ship; any detection suspends shipment of
the family pending verification" is actionable.

---

## 143.4 Inherent, current, and residual risk

- **Inherent** — the risk ignoring the controls. Useful for sizing the value of the control system.
- **Current** — with the controls **as they actually work** (not as documented).
- **Residual** — the risk predicted once the planned treatment is implemented and effective.

**The distinction between current and residual is where most registers fail:** they classify the
risk by the effect of controls whose effectiveness was never measured. A control with 40% real
effectiveness and 95% assumed produces a risk classification wrong by a factor greater than 10.

That's why Chapter 147 treats **barrier effectiveness** as a quantity to measure, not assume.

---

## 143.5 KRIs: indicators that anticipate, not that report

A **KRI** (Key Risk Indicator) is a **leading** metric that signals rising exposure before the event
occurs. It's distinct from a KPI, which is **lagging**.

| Risk | KPI (reports) | KRI (anticipates) |
|---|---|---|
| Customer nonconformance | Customer PPM | Cpk trending down; % of unaddressed SPC alarms |
| Supply disruption | Days of downtime | Stock coverage; supplier delivery delays; concentration |
| Measurement system failure | Complaints from a wrong measurement | % of overdue calibrations; standard drift |
| AI model degradation | Bad parts approved | PSI of the inputs; calibration drift (Ch. 138) |

**How to set the thresholds — and this is where engineering comes in:** the green/yellow/red limits
shouldn't be chosen by intuition. They should derive from (a) the KRI's historical distribution over
a stable operating period, and (b) the required response time. A KRI that triggers when there's no
longer time to act is a KPI with a different name. The code cell computes thresholds by ARL, exactly
as with a control chart — because it's the same problem.

## 143.6 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Logging events instead of risks tied to objectives | An unactionable register | Word it as "failing to hit X due to Y" |
| Confusing cause, event, and consequence | Treatment at the wrong level | Spell out the chain |
| A 5×5 matrix with no defined scales | Classifications incomparable between people | Define each level with an objective criterion |
| A non-actionable appetite | A statement with no consequence | Word it as a checkable rule |
| "Current" risk with assumed control effectiveness | Systematic underestimation | Measure the effectiveness (Ch. 147) |
| A KRI that's actually a KPI | The signal arrives too late | Check whether it precedes the event |
| KRI thresholds by intuition | Noise or blindness | Derive them from the distribution and the response time |
| No named risk owner | No one acts | A name, not a department |

### Exercises with solutions

**E1 — Taxonomy.**
*Build a taxonomy for a factory covering quality, supply chain, cyber, people, continuity,
environment, compliance, and reputation.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The table in section 143.2 is the reference structure. Three criteria distinguish a useful taxonomy
from a list of categories:

**1. Mutually exclusive at the register level.** Each risk falls into one, and only one, category —
otherwise aggregation double-counts. Risks that seem to belong to two categories should be broken
down: "a cyberattack that stops production" splits into a cyber risk (the attack) and a continuity
risk (the stoppage), linked by an explicit causal relationship.

**2. Collectively exhaustive at the category level.** The test is to walk through the organization's
last 20 relevant occurrences and check whether all of them would fit. If any doesn't, a category is
missing.

**3. Aligned with the ownership structure.** Every category should have a natural owner within the
organization. A category with no natural owner is a sign the taxonomy doesn't match how the
organization actually works.

**Mistake to avoid:** taxonomies with 60 sub-categories. Excessive granularity makes classification
inconsistent between people and aggregation useless. Between 8 and 12 top-level categories, with 3
to 5 sub-categories each, is the workable range.

</details>

**E2 — Causal chain.**
*Distinguish risk, cause, event, consequence, and control using a supplier failure.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

| Element | Example | How it's treated |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Single-source supplier of a critical component | Qualify a second supplier; redesign toward a common component |
| **Cause** | Fire, insolvency, strike, sanctions | Require a continuity plan; monitor financial health |
| **Event** | Supply disruption | Early detection (delivery-delay KRI) |
| **Consequence** | Line stopped for 3 weeks | Safety stock; alternative production |
| **Impact on objective** | €2.4M; OTIF failure for 3 customers | — (what you want to avoid) |
| **Risk (wording)** | *"Failing to hit 98% OTIF due to supply disruption of component X"* | — |

**Preventive barriers** (before the event): a second supplier, a continuity audit, financial
monitoring, a contract with penalties.

**Mitigating barriers** (after the event): safety stock, a priority agreement with an alternative
supplier, fast-redesign capability, customer communication.

**Why the distinction matters operationally:** a register that just says "risk: supplier failure"
doesn't let you choose among these six actions. Spelling out the chain shows that qualifying a
second supplier (source) and keeping stock (consequence) are partial substitutes — and lets you
compare them by cost and effectiveness, which is the real decision.

</details>

**E3 — Actionable appetite.**
*Define appetite, tolerance, and escalation limit for a safety characteristic.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**The useless wording (but common):** *"We have low appetite for product-safety risks."* It isn't
checkable and it's never violated.

**The actionable wording:**

> **Appetite.** Zero parts with a safety characteristic out of specification shipped to the
> customer. Operational target: Cpk ≥ 1.67 and zero occurrences.
>
> **Tolerance.** Up to 2 internal detections (caught before shipment) per quarter, provided each has
> corrective action closed within 30 days and the cause identified.
>
> **Escalation limit.** Any of the following conditions escalates immediately to industrial
> leadership and suspends shipment of the affected family:
> - 1 out-of-spec part shipped to the customer;
> - 3 internal detections in the quarter;
> - Cpk below 1.33 on two consecutive checks;
> - any SPC alarm not investigated within 24 h on this characteristic.
>
> **Capacity.** A recall of this family costs ~€8M. The computed absorption capacity
> (0.35 × EBITDA + liquidity ≈ €12.8M) covers **one** event, but consumes 62% of the organization's
> entire capacity on a single risk — and doesn't cover two events, or one event alongside any other.
> Hence the appetite is zero: not for rhetoric, but because a single event would exhaust nearly all
> the absorption margin available for the risk portfolio as a whole.

**Why this wording works:** every element is observable, has a numeric threshold, and a defined
consequence. Anyone can check, at any moment, whether the organization is within its appetite. And
the last paragraph ties the appetite back to capacity, which is what makes it defensible instead of
arbitrary.

</details>

**E4 — Statistically justified KRIs.**
*Build five KRIs and set green/yellow/red thresholds with justification.*

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

| # | KRI | Threshold source | Green / Yellow / Red |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cpk of the critical characteristic | Historical distribution + customer requirement | ≥1.67 / 1.33-1.67 / <1.33 |
| 2 | Days of stock coverage for the single-source component | μ−kσ of the stable history, **calibrated to the replenishment lead time** | >μ−1σ / μ−3σ to μ−1σ / <μ−3σ |
| 3 | % of SPC alarms not investigated within 24 h | Operational target + team capacity | <5% / 5-15% / >15% |
| 4 | Maximum PSI of the AI model's inputs | Established convention (Ch. 138) | <0.10 / 0.10-0.25 / >0.25 |
| 5 | % of critical equipment with overdue calibration | Normative requirement (zero tolerance) | 0% / >0% / >5% or any critical unit |

**The method, applied to KRI 2 and demonstrated in the code cell:**

1. Gather the KRI's history over a period of **demonstrably stable operation** (it's not enough to
   say "no incidents" — you need to check stability, just as before computing capability).
2. Compute μ and σ.
3. Choose k by **ARL₀**: with k = 3, a false alarm every ~740 days; with k = 1, every ~6 days.
4. **Check the ARL₁** — the average time to detect a real degradation of the magnitude that matters.
5. **Weigh the ARL₁ against the required response time.** If the replenishment lead time is 20 days
   and the red threshold only fires after 28 days on average, red arrives too late: the action has
   to happen at yellow.

**The point this exercise is meant to establish:** a KRI whose threshold was never weighed against
the response time is decorative. It either fires constantly (and gets ignored), or fires when
there's nothing left to do. It's exactly the same ARL reasoning used to choose between a Shewhart
chart and an EWMA (Chapter 69) — and that's why SPC competence transfers directly into risk
management.

</details>
