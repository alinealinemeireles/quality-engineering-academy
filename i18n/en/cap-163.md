## Chapter 163: Attribute MSA — Kappa and Percent Agreement

> **Editorial note.** Chapter written from scratch for the web edition, as a companion to Chapter 61. Chapter 61 covers Gage R&R for **variable** characteristics (dimensions measured on a continuous scale); this chapter covers measurement system analysis for **attribute** characteristics (conforming/nonconforming, pass/fail), an explicit topic in the ASQ CQE BOK (IV.F) and the CSSBB BOK (V.C.1).

### The engineering question

*Inspector A approved the part. Inspector B rejected the same part. Which one is right — and how do you measure that without simply assuming one of them is the standard?*

When the inspected characteristic is binary — conforming or nonconforming, pass or fail — there's no instrument standard deviation to calculate a %GRR like in Chapter 61. What exists is the rate at which different appraisers (or the same appraiser, at different times) reach the same decision about the same part. Measuring and improving that rate is what attribute MSA is about.

### Why simple percent agreement isn't enough

The most direct measure is **percent agreement** — the proportion of parts on which every appraiser agreed:

$$\%\text{Agreement} = \frac{\text{Parts with identical decisions across all assessments}}{\text{Total parts assessed}} \times 100$$

The problem is that this measure doesn't discount agreement that would happen just by chance. If 95% of the parts in a sample are clearly good, two random appraisers will already agree most of the time simply because most parts are easy to judge — even if, on the truly ambiguous parts (the ones that matter), they disagree systematically. That's exactly the bias the kappa coefficient corrects for.

### Cohen's kappa (two appraisers)

Cohen's kappa measures agreement **beyond** what would be expected by chance:

$$\kappa = \frac{P_o - P_e}{1 - P_e}$$

where $P_o$ is the observed proportion of agreement (the sum of the diagonal cells of an appraiser-A-by-appraiser-B contingency table, divided by the total), and $P_e$ is the proportion of agreement expected by chance alone, calculated from each appraiser's marginal distributions:

$$P_e = \sum_{i} \frac{(\text{row } i \text{ total}) \times (\text{column } i \text{ total})}{n^2}$$

**Interpretation (AIAG criterion):** $\kappa \geq 0.75$ indicates good-to-excellent agreement; $0.40 \leq \kappa < 0.75$ indicates moderate agreement, acceptable only conditionally; $\kappa < 0.40$ indicates poor agreement — the attribute measurement system shouldn't be released. $\kappa = 1$ is perfect agreement; $\kappa = 0$ is equivalent to chance; $\kappa < 0$ is worse than chance (rarer, but indicates a serious problem, like inverted decision criteria between appraisers).

### Fleiss's kappa (more than two appraisers) and the study's three comparisons

When the study involves more than two appraisers, use **Fleiss's kappa**, a generalization of Cohen's kappa for multiple appraisers classifying the same parts into the same categories — the logic of correcting observed agreement by chance-expected agreement is the same, just extended to more columns.

A complete attribute MSA study normally reports three distinct comparisons, each answering a different question:

- **Within-appraiser repeatability** — does the same appraiser, assessing the same part at different times (blind, without knowing it's a repeat), reach the same decision? Measures each appraiser's internal consistency.
- **Between-appraiser reproducibility** — do different appraisers, assessing the same part, reach the same decision as each other? Measures whether the decision criterion is actually standardized between people.
- **Appraiser vs. standard accuracy** — when a known reference standard exists (for example, parts pre-classified by an expert or by objective measurement), does each appraiser agree with that standard? This is the only one of the three comparisons that measures accuracy, not just consistency — a group of appraisers can be perfectly consistent with each other and still be entirely wrong relative to the standard.

### Worked example: two appraisers, one binary characteristic

```py-r
--- python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

# 50 parts, assessed twice by two appraisers (A and B), binary decision
# 1 = conforming, 0 = nonconforming. The "standard" is the reference classification.
n = 50
padrao = rng.choice([0, 1], size=n, p=[0.30, 0.70])

def avaliar(padrao, taxa_erro, rng):
    ruido = rng.random(len(padrao)) < taxa_erro
    return np.where(ruido, 1 - padrao, padrao)

A1 = avaliar(padrao, 0.08, rng)
A2 = avaliar(padrao, 0.08, rng)     # appraiser A's repeat
B1 = avaliar(padrao, 0.14, rng)     # appraiser B, noisier

def kappa_cohen(x, y):
    tab = pd.crosstab(x, y)
    tab = tab.reindex(index=[0, 1], columns=[0, 1], fill_value=0)
    n_tot = tab.values.sum()
    Po = np.trace(tab.values) / n_tot
    marg_l = tab.sum(axis=1).values / n_tot
    marg_c = tab.sum(axis=0).values / n_tot
    Pe = (marg_l * marg_c).sum()
    kappa = (Po - Pe) / (1 - Pe) if Pe != 1 else np.nan
    return Po, Pe, kappa

def classificar(k):
    if k >= 0.75:
        return "good to excellent"
    if k >= 0.40:
        return "moderate (conditional)"
    return "poor -- review criterion or training"

print("STEP 1 -- Within-appraiser repeatability, appraiser A (A1 vs A2):")
Po, Pe, k = kappa_cohen(A1, A2)
print(f"  %Agreement = {Po*100:.1f}%  |  Kappa = {k:.3f}  ({classificar(k)})\n")

print("STEP 2 -- Between-appraiser reproducibility (A1 vs B1):")
Po, Pe, k = kappa_cohen(A1, B1)
print(f"  %Agreement = {Po*100:.1f}%  |  Kappa = {k:.3f}  ({classificar(k)})\n")

print("STEP 3 -- Accuracy vs. reference standard:")
for nome, aval in [("A1", A1), ("A2", A2), ("B1", B1)]:
    Po, Pe, k = kappa_cohen(aval, padrao)
    print(f"  {nome} vs. standard: %Agreement = {Po*100:.1f}%  |  Kappa = {k:.3f}  ({classificar(k)})")

print("\nDIAGNOSIS:")
print("  Appraiser B can be internally consistent with A on most parts,")
print("  but if kappa vs. the standard is systematically lower, the problem isn't")
print("  agreement between people -- it's accuracy: B is applying the wrong criterion,")
print("  not just a different criterion from A.")
```

**Engineering reading.** Notice the three comparisons can tell different stories: it's entirely possible for A and B to agree very well with each other (high reproducibility) and still both disagree with the standard (low accuracy) — in that case, the problem isn't training B to "think like A," it's fixing both of their decision criteria, likely via revising the visual standard or the work instruction.

### Tools: Excel, Power BI, and Minitab

**Excel 365.** There's no native kappa function. Build the contingency table with `COUNTIFS` crossing the two appraisers' decisions, sum the diagonal for $P_o$, calculate the row and column marginal totals for $P_e$, and apply the formula $\kappa = (P_o - P_e)/(1 - P_e)$ in a cell — it's a calculation of just a few lines, but manual; for Fleiss's kappa with more than two appraisers, the setup becomes too cumbersome to be worth it outside Python/R or dedicated software.

**Power BI (DAX).** Useful for the measurement system's ongoing tracking dashboard — a percent-agreement measure by period (`% Agreement = DIVIDE(CALCULATE(COUNTROWS(Assessments), Assessments[Decision_A]=Assessments[Decision_B]), COUNTROWS(Assessments))`) is straightforward; kappa itself, like in Excel, has no native function and should be calculated outside and imported as a value.

**Minitab** (current version — check minitab.com): **Stat → Quality Tools → Attribute Agreement Analysis**. This is the correct, dedicated tool for this study — Minitab asks for the appraiser, part, replicate, and (optionally) reference standard columns, and automatically returns the percent agreement and Fleiss's kappa for all three comparisons (within appraiser, between appraisers, and vs. standard), along with the corresponding bar charts. For this specific topic, unlike variable Gage R&R, Minitab tends to be more direct than assembling everything manually in Excel.

### Proposed exercise

Using the worked example's data set above, replace 10 of appraiser B's decisions with random values (simulating an appraiser who "guesses" on ambiguous parts) and recalculate kappa between A1 and B1. Notice how kappa drops much more sharply than raw percent agreement — and discuss why this difference in sensitivity is exactly the reason to prefer kappa over percent agreement when releasing an attribute measurement system.

### Common mistakes

1. **Reporting only percent agreement, without kappa.** In samples dominated by easy-to-judge parts, raw agreement is deceptively high.
2. **Not including blind repeats from the same appraiser.** Without repeats, there's no way to separate appraiser inconsistency from real disagreement between appraisers.
3. **Assessing only clearly good or clearly bad parts.** A good attribute study deliberately includes ambiguous parts, close to the decision boundary — that's where measurement systems fail.
4. **Confusing high reproducibility with accuracy.** Appraisers can agree with each other and still be systematically wrong relative to the reference standard.
5. **Using Cohen's kappa with more than two appraisers.** In that case, use Fleiss's kappa — the two-appraiser formula doesn't generalize directly.
